Core Practical 4 — Initial Rate of Enzyme Reactions

Investigate the effect of temperature, pH, enzyme concentration and substrate concentration on the initial rate of enzyme-catalysed reactions.

Key Knowledge — Rapid Recall

The system: Trypsin (a protease) hydrolyses casein, the protein that makes milk opaque. As casein is broken down, the solution becomes clearer. A colorimeter measures the change in absorbance (or transmission) over time. Use a red filter — casein scatters red light most strongly, giving the largest signal.

Initial rate vs average rate: Initial rate is calculated from the gradient of a tangent at t = 0. It is preferred because at the start, substrate concentration is at its known value and the enzyme has not been denatured by prolonged exposure. As the reaction proceeds, substrate is used up and rate falls — an average rate would underestimate the true rate.

Variables (for substrate concentration investigation): IV = % milk concentration. DV = initial rate (gradient of tangent in s⁻¹ or absorbance units s⁻¹). CVs = trypsin concentration, temperature, pH (use buffer), volume of solutions, time intervals.

Calculating the gradient: Draw a tangent to the curve at t = 0. Construct a large right-angled triangle. Gradient = Δabsorbance / Δtime. A larger triangle gives more accurate values — at least two-thirds of the line.

Random vs systematic errors: A scratched cuvette causes a systematic error — every reading is offset in the same direction. Variation in shaking the cuvette or in pipetting volumes causes random errors — readings vary either side of the true value.

Know It

Q1 2 marks
Explain why the initial rate of reaction is measured rather than an average rate over a longer time period.
Mark Scheme
  • At the start of the reaction the substrate concentration is at its known/maximum value (1)
  • As the reaction proceeds, substrate is used up so rate falls / enzymes may be denatured / product may inhibit the reaction (1)
Examiner note: Either of "substrate gets used up" or "rate slows over time" earns the second mark — but linking it to why initial rate gives the true rate at known [S] is the discriminator. Avoid "the reaction speeds up at the start" — it is fastest at t = 0 and slows from there.

Nail It

Q3 6 marks
A student investigates the effect of substrate concentration on the rate of trypsin activity. They use milk solutions at concentrations of 0.2%, 0.4%, 0.6%, 0.8% and 1.0%, with a constant 1% trypsin solution. The student finds that the initial rate increases as substrate concentration increases up to 0.6%, after which the rate plateaus.
Explain these results in terms of enzyme-substrate interactions.
Mark Scheme
  • At low substrate concentration, the rate is limited by the number of substrate molecules available (1)
  • Increasing substrate concentration increases the frequency of successful collisions between substrate and enzyme active sites (1)
  • This results in more enzyme-substrate complexes forming per second (1)
  • At higher substrate concentrations, all active sites are occupied / the enzyme is saturated (1)
  • Enzyme concentration becomes the limiting factor (1)
  • The rate plateaus at Vmax / cannot increase further without adding more enzyme (1)
Examiner note: This is a classic "describe the curve, then explain it" question. Strong answers explicitly link rising rate to "more collisions / more E-S complexes" and the plateau to "saturation of active sites". Avoid "the enzyme runs out" — it doesn't, it just becomes saturated.
Stretch & Challenge

Q10 calculation: A student measures the initial rate of reaction at 20°C as 0.0048 s⁻¹ and at 30°C as 0.0102 s⁻¹. Calculate the temperature coefficient (Q₁₀) for this reaction. State what this value tells you about the reaction.

Hint: Q₁₀ = rate at (T+10) ÷ rate at T. A Q₁₀ of approximately 2 is typical for enzyme-catalysed reactions below the optimum.

Teacher Note — Worked Solution
Q₁₀ = 0.0102 ÷ 0.0048 = 2.13. This value (≈2) is consistent with an enzyme-catalysed reaction operating below its optimum temperature. The rate roughly doubles for every 10°C rise because higher kinetic energy increases collision frequency and the proportion of successful collisions. Above the optimum, Q₁₀ falls below 1 as denaturation begins to outweigh kinetic effects.

Core Practical 5 — Light Microscopy & Graticule Use

(i) Use a light microscope to make observations and labelled drawings of suitable animal cells; (ii) use a graticule with a microscope to make measurements and understand the concept of scale.

Key Knowledge — Rapid Recall

The eyepiece graticule is a scale (typically 0–100) etched onto a disc inside the eyepiece. It moves with the eyepiece, so it always appears the same size to the observer. The stage micrometer is a scale on a slide (each small division usually = 100 μm). Calibration matches the two: place the stage micrometer on the stage, line up the scales, and count how many epu correspond to a known length.

Calibration must be repeated when the objective lens is changed, because the apparent size of the stage micrometer changes with magnification but the eyepiece graticule does not. Each magnification has its own conversion factor (μm per epu).

Magnification equation: magnification = size of image ÷ size of real object. Always convert to the same units (usually μm) before calculating. Magnification has no units — it is a ratio.

Why methylene blue? Cheek cells are nearly transparent under a light microscope. Methylene blue is a basic stain that binds to the acidic phosphate groups in DNA and RNA, making the nucleus and ribosomes visible by contrast.

Drawing rules: Sharp HB pencil, continuous (not feathery) lines, no shading or colour. Label lines drawn with a ruler, no arrowheads, lines just touch the structure. Drawing should fill at least half the available space. Include a scale bar and the magnification used.

Resolution vs magnification: Light microscopes magnify up to ~×1500 but their resolution is limited to ~200 nm by the wavelength of light. This is why organelles such as ribosomes (~20 nm) are not visible — even at high magnification, they lie below the resolution limit.

Know It

Q4 2 marks
State two rules that should be followed when producing a labelled biological drawing of a cell.
Mark Scheme — any 2
  • Use a sharp HB pencil with clear, continuous (not feathery) lines (1)
  • No shading or colour (1)
  • Label lines drawn with a ruler, with no arrowheads, just touching the structure (1)
  • The drawing should be large enough to fill at least half the available space (1)
  • Draw only what you can see — do not include structures you know are there but cannot resolve (1)
  • Include a scale bar / state the magnification (1)

Q5 5 marks
A student calibrates the eyepiece graticule using a stage micrometer where each small division represents 100 μm. They observe that 4 small divisions on the stage micrometer line up exactly with 10 eyepiece units (epu).

Without changing magnification, the student then measures a cheek cell. The cell is 1.4 epu wide.
(a) Calculate the value of 1 epu in μm at this magnification. (2)

(b) Calculate the actual width of the cheek cell. Give your answer in μm. (1)

(c) Explain why the graticule must be re-calibrated if the student switches to a different objective lens. (2)
Mark Scheme
  • (a) 4 divisions × 100 μm = 400 μm corresponds to 10 epu (1)
  • (a) 1 epu = 400 ÷ 10 = 40 μm (1)
  • (b) Cell width = 1.4 × 40 = 56 μm (1)
  • (c) Changing the objective lens changes the magnification, so the apparent size of the stage micrometer changes (1)

Q6 6 marks
A student examines a cheek cell at a total magnification of ×100 and produces a biological drawing. Their drawing has the following features:
  • The drawing fills approximately one quarter of the available space.
  • The cytoplasm has been shaded with diagonal pencil lines.
  • The cell membrane and nucleus are shown, alongside several small circles labelled "ribosomes" and oval shapes labelled "mitochondria".
  • Labels are written with arrowheads pointing to each structure.
  • No scale bar or magnification has been recorded.
Evaluate the student's drawing. For each error, explain why it is incorrect and state how it should be corrected.
Mark Scheme
  • The drawing should be large enough to fill at least half the available space, to allow detail to be shown clearly (1)
  • No shading or colour should be used in biological drawings — use only continuous outlines (1)
  • Labels should be written horizontally with label lines drawn in pencil with a ruler, without arrowheads, just touching the structure (1)
  • Ribosomes (~20 nm) and mitochondria (~1 μm) are below the resolution limit of a light microscope at ×100 — they should not be drawn / draw only what is visible (1)
  • At ×100 only the cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus would typically be visible (1)
  • A scale bar should be included and the magnification should be recorded next to the drawing (1)

Stretch & Challenge

Novel context: A student photographs a cheek cell through the eyepiece of a microscope and prints the image. The printed cell measures 84 mm wide. The student knows the actual cell is 70 μm wide. Calculate the magnification of the printed image, and state what this tells you about the difference between magnification and resolution.

Hint: Convert both measurements to the same unit before applying M = I ÷ A.

Teacher Note — Worked Solution
Convert 84 mm to μm: 84 × 1000 = 84 000 μm. Magnification = 84 000 ÷ 70 = ×1200. This is the magnification of the printed image (not the microscope alone). However, magnification is independent of resolution: increasing magnification beyond the resolution limit produces "empty magnification" — a larger image but no extra detail. The resolution of a light microscope (~200 nm) is the true ceiling on what can be seen, regardless of how much the image is enlarged. Tie back to ribosomes/mitochondria from Q6.

Core Practical 6 — Root Tip Squash & Mitosis

Prepare and stain a root tip squash to observe the stages of mitosis.

Key Knowledge — Rapid Recall

Why garlic root tips? The meristem at the very tip of the root contains actively dividing cells, so a high proportion will be in mitosis at any moment. Garlic cells have only 8 chromosomes (2n = 16 in some sources), which makes them relatively easy to count once condensed.

The role of each reagent — every step has a reason:

• Hydrochloric acid (1 mol dm⁻³, 55°C, 5 min): hydrolyses the pectins in the middle lamella, breaking down the "glue" that holds plant cells together. This allows the cells to be separated when squashed.

• Acetic orcein stain (55°C, 5 min): binds strongly to chromosomes and stains them dark red, making them visible against the cytoplasm. The acetic acid also helps to fix the cells, freezing them at the stage they were in.

• Maceration with mounted needles + squashing under a paper towel: spreads the cells into a single layer, so individual chromosomes can be seen rather than overlapping clumps.

Mitotic index: the proportion of cells visibly undergoing mitosis at the moment of fixation.

mitotic index = cells in mitosis ÷ total cells in field of view

What cell counts tell you: the snapshot freezes mitosis at one moment, so the proportion of cells in each stage is roughly proportional to the time spent in that stage. If 18 cells are in prophase but only 4 are in anaphase, prophase takes longer than anaphase. This is why prophase typically dominates.

Why this matters clinically: high mitotic index is a marker of rapid cell division, used to assess tumour aggressiveness in cancer biopsies.

Know It

Q7 3 marks
Explain why root tips are heated in hydrochloric acid before staining.
Mark Scheme
  • The acid hydrolyses the pectins / breaks down the middle lamella (1)
  • The middle lamella is the layer that holds plant cells together (1)
  • This separates the cells so they can be spread into a single layer when squashed / so individual cells and chromosomes can be observed (1)
Examiner note: Just saying "to separate the cells" caps at 1 mark. Marks come from explicitly naming pectins / middle lamella, and linking the separation to the practical purpose (single layer of cells for observation).

Nail It

Q9 6 marks
Describe the procedure a student should follow to prepare a stained root tip squash, explaining the purpose of each key step.
Mark Scheme
  • Place root tips in 1 mol dm⁻³ HCl at 55–60°C for 5 minutes — to hydrolyse pectins in the middle lamella so cells can be separated (1)
  • Rinse the root tips in water — to stop the acid reaction / prevent further damage to the cells (1)
  • Cut 5–10 mm of the meristem region from the very tip — this is where mitosis occurs / contains the highest proportion of dividing cells (1)
  • Transfer to acetic orcein at 55–60°C for 5 minutes — orcein binds to and stains chromosomes dark red / the acetic acid fixes the cells (1)
  • Macerate (tease apart) with mounted needles and add a coverslip, then press gently through paper towel — spreads cells into a single layer so individual cells and chromosomes can be observed (1)
  • Examine under low power first to locate the meristem, then switch to high power (×400) to identify chromosomes and stages — low power gives orientation, high power gives detail (1)
Examiner note: This is a "method + reasoning" question — the pattern technique → reason earns full marks. Listing techniques alone caps at 3 marks. Watch for "the acid kills the cells" — it doesn't kill them in the sense of denaturing protein, it specifically hydrolyses pectin in the middle lamella.
Stretch & Challenge

Novel context: An oncologist is studying biopsy samples from two patients. Patient A's tumour shows a mitotic index of 0.08; Patient B's tumour shows a mitotic index of 0.42. Explain what this tells the oncologist about the two tumours, and suggest one reason why the mitotic index alone may not be sufficient to compare the aggressiveness of the two cancers.

Teacher Note — Worked Discussion
Higher mitotic index = more cells in division = faster-growing tumour, generally associated with greater aggressiveness. However: mitotic index is a snapshot and varies between fields of view, between regions of the same tumour, and between sample preparations. It does not capture invasiveness, vascularisation, or genetic profile. Modern oncology combines mitotic index with other markers (Ki-67, grade, stage). This is a great link to A2 cancer content and synoptic Paper 5 questions.

Q10 12 marks
A student is investigating whether different concentrations of a plant extract affect the rate of cell division in onion root tips. They have access to a light microscope with an eyepiece graticule, a colorimeter, milk-trypsin solutions, and the standard root tip squash equipment.

Their plan is in two parts:
Part 1: Treat onion root tips with five different concentrations of the plant extract (0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, 1%) for 24 hours, then prepare squashes and calculate the mitotic index for each.
Part 2: Use the colorimeter to test whether the plant extract also affects the rate of trypsin-catalysed digestion of casein.
(a) The student calibrates the eyepiece graticule using a stage micrometer. They observe that 5 small divisions on the stage micrometer (each 100 μm) line up with 8 epu. Calculate the value of 1 epu in μm at this magnification. (2)

(b) Describe the procedure the student should follow to prepare a stained root tip squash from one of the treated root tips. (3)

(c) For the 0.5% extract treatment, the student counts 96 cells in the field of view: 72 in interphase, 12 in prophase, 6 in metaphase, 2 in anaphase, 4 in telophase. Calculate the mitotic index, giving your answer to 2 significant figures. (2)

(d) When the student measures the absorbance of the milk-trypsin mixtures with different extract concentrations, they notice the readings vary by ±0.04 between repeats at the same concentration. Suggest one source of random error and one source of systematic error in this colorimetric investigation, and explain how each could be reduced. (3)

(e) Evaluate the conclusion that "the plant extract inhibits both mitosis and enzyme activity." (2)
Mark Scheme — Q10 (12 marks)
  • (a) 5 × 100 μm = 500 μm corresponds to 8 epu (1)
  • 1 epu = 500 ÷ 8 = 62.5 μm (1)
  • (b) Place root tips in 1 mol dm⁻³ HCl at 55°C for 5 minutes (to hydrolyse pectins) (1)
  • Rinse and transfer to acetic orcein at 55°C for 5 minutes (to stain chromosomes) (1)
  • Macerate with mounted needles, add coverslip, press gently through paper towel to produce single layer (1)
  • (c) Cells in mitosis = 12 + 6 + 2 + 4 = 24 (1)
  • Mitotic index = 24 ÷ 96 = 0.25 (or 25%) (1)
  • (d) Random error: variation in mixing/shaking the cuvette before measurement / pipetting volumes / time before reading (1)
  • Reduction: take more repeats and calculate a mean / use a datalogger to reduce timing variation (1)
  • Systematic error: scratched or dirty cuvette / colorimeter not zeroed correctly / cuvette placed in different orientation (1) — accept any one paired with appropriate reduction (clean and inspect cuvette / re-zero before each reading / mark cuvette to ensure consistent orientation)
  • (e) The conclusion may be supported by the mitosis data if mitotic index falls with increasing extract concentration, but this only shows a correlation — repeats and statistical analysis are needed (1)
  • The same plant extract may affect mitosis and trypsin via different mechanisms, so the conclusion that one effect "explains" the other is not justified / further investigation needed to identify the active component (1)
Examiner note: Part (e) is the discriminator. Weak answers will say "yes, both rates went down". Strong answers question whether correlation = causation, raise sample size, and note that a single extract may have multiple active compounds acting through different mechanisms. This is pure AO3 thinking.
