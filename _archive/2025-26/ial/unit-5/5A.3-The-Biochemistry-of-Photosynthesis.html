Section 02 · The Two Stages Overview

A biochemical relay race

~10 min

Photosynthesis is not a single reaction. It is a two-stage process involving a complex series of reactions[cite: 1]. The first stage needs light. The second stage does not—but it desperately needs what the first stage produces.

The two stages of photosynthesis. The light-dependent reactions produce materials (ATP and reduced NADP) to be used in the light-independent reactions[cite: 1].

The whole process occurs all the time during the hours of daylight[cite: 1].

1. Light-Dependent Reactions: Occurs on the thylakoid membranes. Photons hit chlorophyll, exciting electrons. Water is broken down (photolysis) to provide hydrogen ions (H⁺) and electrons. ATP is generated, and NADP is reduced to form reduced NADP[cite: 1].

2. Light-Independent Reactions: Occurs in the stroma. It uses the ATP and reduced NADP from the first stage to reduce carbon dioxide into simple sugars in a cycle known as the Calvin cycle[cite: 1]. These reactions can continue in the dark, but only as long as ATP and reduced NADP remain available[cite: 2].

Exam Hint

Do not refer to these as the "light stage" and "dark stage". The light-independent reactions occur in both light and dark conditions[cite: 1].

Section 03 · Light-Dependent Reactions

Excited electrons and photolysis

~15 min

When a photon of light hits a chlorophyll molecule, the energy is transferred to the electrons. These electrons are raised to higher energy levels. If raised high enough, the electron leaves the chlorophyll molecule completely and is collected by an electron acceptor[cite: 1].

Breakout Room Task: The Electron Relay Race

In your groups, you have 5 minutes to compare the two pathways below. Discuss and note down answers to these questions:

  1. Which photosystem(s) are involved in cyclic vs non-cyclic photophosphorylation?
  2. What happens to the electron at the end of each pathway?
  3. Why is water only split in the non-cyclic pathway?
Cyclic Photophosphorylation
Non-Cyclic Photophosphorylation
Involves only photosystem I (PSI)[cite: 1].

Light hits chlorophyll in PSI. An excited electron leaves, is collected by an electron acceptor, and transferred directly along an electron transport chain[cite: 1].

This drives the production of ATP. The electron returns to the chlorophyll molecule in PSI, ready to be excited again[cite: 1].
Involves both PSI and PSII[cite: 1].

Light hits both photosystems. An excited electron from PSII is passed along an electron transport chain to PSI, driving the synthesis of ATP[cite: 1].

Simultaneously, an excited electron leaves PSI and is passed to an acceptor, then to NADP, forming reduced NADP[cite: 2].

Because PSI lost an electron to NADP, it takes the electron arriving from PSII. Because PSII lost its electron to PSI, it must replace it. It gets this replacement electron from the splitting of water (photolysis)[cite: 2].
Feynman move, explaining Photolysis

Water molecules naturally dissociate into hydrogen ions (H⁺) and hydroxide ions (OH⁻)[cite: 2]. When PSII loses an electron, it becomes a powerful electron thief. It steals electrons from the OH⁻ ions. Once the OH⁻ ions lose their electrons, they react together to form oxygen and water.

The equation is: 4OH⁻ - 4e⁻ (lost to chlorophyll) → O₂ + 2H₂O[cite: 2].
The leftover H⁺ ions are scooped up by NADP to make reduced NADP[cite: 2].

Progress Check Formative
Recall · 1
What are the two main products of non-cyclic photophosphorylation that are required for the next stage? [2]
ATP and reduced NADP[cite: 2].
Apply · 2
Explain why oxygen is produced in non-cyclic photophosphorylation but not in cyclic photophosphorylation. [3]
In non-cyclic photophosphorylation, electrons leave photosystem II and do not return. To replace these lost electrons, water molecules are broken down (photolysis), releasing oxygen as a byproduct[cite: 2]. In cyclic photophosphorylation, the excited electron from photosystem I returns to the same photosystem, so no replacement electrons from water are needed, and no photolysis occurs[cite: 1].
Section 04 · Light-Independent Reactions

The Calvin Cycle

~15 min

Now we move to the stroma. Here, a series of enzyme-controlled steps reduces carbon dioxide from the air, using the reducing power (reduced NADP) and energy (ATP) produced by the light-dependent stage[cite: 2]. This is the Calvin cycle.

The Three Steps of the Calvin Cycle

Step 1: Carbon Fixation

Carbon dioxide combines with a 5-carbon compound called ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP). This vital step needs the enzyme ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RUBISCO)[cite: 2].

Step 2: Reduction

The theoretical 6-carbon compound formed is highly unstable and immediately separates into two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP), a 3-carbon compound[cite: 2]. GP is then reduced (hydrogen is added) to form glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (GALP), a 3-carbon sugar. The hydrogen comes from reduced NADP, and the energy required comes from ATP[cite: 2].

Step 3: Regeneration

Much of the 3-carbon GALP follows a series of steps (using more ATP) to replace the RuBP needed in the first step of the cycle. However, some GALP is synthesised into the 6-carbon sugar glucose or transferred directly into the glycolysis pathway[cite: 2].

Section 05 · RUBISCO & Photorespiration

The world's most important, yet inefficient enzyme

~8 min

RUBISCO makes up about 30% of the total protein of a leaf, making it probably the most common protein on Earth[cite: 3]. But it has a fatal flaw.

Feynman move: The Confused Bouncer

Imagine RUBISCO is a bouncer at a club (the Calvin cycle), told to only let in Carbon Dioxide. But RUBISCO has terrible eyesight. The active site cannot distinguish between the carbon-oxygen double bonds of CO₂ molecules and the oxygen-oxygen double bonds of O₂ molecules[cite: 3].

When there is lots of CO₂, RUBISCO functions as a carboxylase, letting CO₂ in to combine with RuBP[cite: 3].

When there is lots of oxygen, RUBISCO functions as an oxygenase. It binds to oxygen and combines it with RuBP. This process is known as photorespiration[cite: 3]. It uses oxygen, releases carbon dioxide, and wastes both carbon and energy[cite: 3]. In plants, about 25% of the products of the Calvin cycle are lost this way[cite: 3].

Progress Check Formative
Apply · 1
Using your knowledge of enzymes, what type of inhibition is occurring during photorespiration? [1]
Competitive inhibition[cite: 3]. Oxygen and carbon dioxide compete for the same active site on the RUBISCO enzyme.
Stretch · 2
Why didn't evolutionary selection pressure eliminate this inefficiency in RUBISCO? [2]
When RUBISCO evolved, the atmosphere was high in carbon dioxide with very little oxygen. Because of this, photorespiration never occurred, so there was no selection pressure against it[cite: 3].
Section 06 · Using the Products

The fates of GALP

~8 min

GALP is the primary end-product of photosynthesis. It is the most important molecule for the synthesis of everything else needed in the plant[cite: 3].

Some of the GALP produced in the Calvin cycle is used to produce glucose in a process called gluconeogenesis[cite: 3]. But that is just the beginning.

Molecule to be Synthesised
How GALP is used
Polysaccharides (Starch, Cellulose)
Glucose is converted into disaccharides like sucrose for transport, into starch for energy storage, and into cellulose for structural support.
Amino Acids & Proteins
GALP entering cellular respiration pathways provides carbon skeletons. These combine with nitrates absorbed from the soil to form amino acids.
Nucleic Acids (DNA, RNA)
GALP combines with phosphates from the soil to produce nucleic acids.
Lipids (Membranes, Storage)
Some GALP is converted into acetyl coenzyme A. This is used to synthesise fatty acids needed for the production of phospholipids for membranes and lipids for storage.
Section 07 · Limiting Factors

What slows the reaction down?

~10 min

Photosynthesis is limited by the factor that is nearest to its minimum value. We focus on three main limiting factors: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, and temperature.

Exam Hint

Always refer to "light intensity" rather than the "amount of light". When describing the effect, state "as light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases". Similarly, refer to the "concentration of carbon dioxide".

Factor
How it limits the process
Light Intensity
Light intensity affects the amount of chlorophyll excited. If light is low, insufficient ATP and reduced NADP are produced to allow the light-independent stage to progress at its maximum rate.
Carbon Dioxide Concentration
If there is not enough CO₂ available for fixing in the Calvin cycle, the reactions cannot proceed at maximum rate. In natural environments, CO₂ is the most common limiting factor.
Temperature
All Calvin cycle reactions (and many light-dependent ones) are controlled by enzymes. Therefore, they are sensitive to temperature. A plant can only photosynthesise rapidly when temperature is right, even if light and CO₂ are abundant.

Stretch · synoptic
Describe the light-independent reactions of photosynthesis and explain how they rely on the light-dependent stage. [6]

Model answer:

The light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle) take place in the stroma of the chloroplast[cite: 2]. The process begins with carbon fixation, where carbon dioxide from the air combines with a 5-carbon compound, ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP), catalyzed by the enzyme RUBISCO[cite: 2].

This forms a highly unstable 6-carbon compound that immediately splits into two molecules of the 3-carbon compound glycerate 3-phosphate (GP)[cite: 2]. GP is then reduced to form glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (GALP)[cite: 2].

This reduction step relies entirely on the products of the light-dependent stage: it requires hydrogen from reduced NADP and energy from ATP[cite: 2]. Without the light-dependent stage producing these two molecules on the thylakoid membranes, the Calvin cycle would stop. Finally, some GALP is used to produce glucose, while the rest undergoes a series of reactions (using more ATP) to regenerate RuBP so the cycle can continue[cite: 2].
