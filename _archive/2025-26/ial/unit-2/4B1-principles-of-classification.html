Core Content

The Background to Biodiversity

Biodiversity is a measure of the variety of living organisms and their genetic differences. It is a critical concept right now: the Earth's biodiversity is reducing rapidly, and many scientists think this may affect the future health of the planet.

To understand biodiversity, we need a way of identifying and organising the different groups of organisms. We classify the living world by putting organisms in groups based on their similarities and differences.

Why classification matters

Three reasons scientists need to classify organisms:

① Monitor population changes in different types of organism  |  ② Understand how different types of living organism are related to each other  |  ③ Communicate about organisms accurately across different countries and languages

Core Content

The History of Taxonomy

Taxonomy is the science of describing, classifying and naming living organisms. From the time of Aristotle onwards, people grouped organisms by morphology — their physical appearance.

Early biologists used analogous features (features that look similar but don't share biological origin). The problem? You could end up grouping bats, birds, and flying insects together, simply because they all fly.

A valid classification system must use homologous structures — structures that genuinely show common ancestry, regardless of current function.

In the 18th century, the Swedish botanist Carolus Linnaeus (1707–78) developed the first scientifically devised classification system. We still use many of his principles today. Modern techniques allow us to go beyond observation — we now use molecular and genetic data to confirm evolutionary relationships.

Analogous vs Homologous: A common error is to confuse these. Remember — analogous features look similar but evolved independently (convergent evolution). Homologous structures share a common ancestor. In the exam, always link homologous structures to common ancestry, not just similar appearance.

1 kingdom

1 kingdom

4 kingdoms

The Binomial System

Devised by Linnaeus, the binomial system gives every organism two Latin names. Binomial means "two names". The system is used universally by biologists — and updated constantly as new data emerges.

Homo sapiens
Homo
Genus name · Upper case
+
sapiens
Species name · Lower case

Rules for writing binomial names:

  • Always written in italics
  • Genus name has an upper-case first letter; species name is all lower-case
  • After first use, genus can be abbreviated to initial: H. sapiens
  • Both names are always in Latin (or Latinised)
💡

Same genus ≠ same species: The genus Vanessa contains the Painted Lady (Vanessa cardui), Red Admiral (Vanessa atalanta), and Indian Red Admiral (Vanessa indica). They share enough characteristics to be in the same genus — but are clearly distinct species. Two species with the same second name in different genera are completely unrelated.

T2 · Apply Use your understanding in a new context
Q2.1
An organism is described as: eukaryotic, multicellular, heterotrophic, and has chitin in its cell walls. Identify the kingdom it belongs to and justify your answer. (3 marks)
Fungi (1). Justification: eukaryotic and multicellular rules out bacteria (1); chitin in cell walls (not cellulose) distinguishes Fungi from Plantae (1). Heterotrophic rules out Plantae which is autotrophic.
Q2.2
A student writes the binomial name for the wolf as canis lupus. Identify two errors in this name and rewrite it correctly. (3 marks)
Error 1: genus name should have an upper-case first letter — Canis, not canis (1). Error 2: must be written in italics (1). Correct form: Canis lupus (1).
T3 · Evaluate Longer response — structure your argument
Q3.1
Explain why using analogous features to classify organisms can lead to errors in classification systems, using an appropriate example. (4 marks)
Analogous features look similar or have similar function but are NOT from the same biological origin (1). This means they evolved independently — they do not indicate common ancestry (1). Example: wings of bats (mammals) and wings of insects — both used for flight but evolved completely differently (1). Using such features would incorrectly group bats and insects together, masking their true evolutionary relationships — a valid classification must use homologous structures that show common ancestry (1).

Subject Vocabulary

Key Terms

Click any term to see the definition.

biodiversity
A measure of the variety of living organisms and their genetic differences.
taxonomy
The science of describing, classifying and naming living organisms.
morphology
The study of the form and structure of organisms.
analogous features
Features that look similar or have a similar function, but are not from the same biological origin.
homologous structures
Structures that genuinely show common ancestry — they may have different current functions but share evolutionary origin.
domain
One of the three largest classification categories: Eukaryota, Bacteria, and Archaea.
kingdom
The classification category smaller than domains. There are six kingdoms: Archaeabacteria, Eubacteria, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
phylum (division for plants)
A group of classes that all share common characteristics.
genus
A group of species that all share common characteristics. All members share the same first (genus) name in the binomial system.
species
A group of closely related organisms that are all potentially capable of interbreeding to produce fertile offspring.
Archaea
Domain made up of bacteria-like prokaryotic organisms found in extreme conditions and soil; thought to be early relatives of eukaryotes.
Eubacteria
True bacteria — prokaryotic organisms.
Protista
A kingdom containing all single-celled organisms, green and brown algae and slime moulds.
evolution
The process by which natural selection acts on variation to bring about adaptations and eventually speciation.
