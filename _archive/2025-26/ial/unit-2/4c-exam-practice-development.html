Read this briefing before starting

This lesson is not about re-learning content — it is about turning what you know into marks. Exam performance is a skill, and like any skill, it can be trained. Work through the parts in order. Every question has a mark scheme; use them honestly.

What are AO1, AO2, and AO3?

Every exam question targets one or more Assessment Objectives (AOs). Understanding what each demands is the foundation of every mark you earn. The exam is weighted roughly 35% AO1, 45% AO2, 20% AO3 — AO2 is the biggest source of marks.

AO1 — Knowledge

Demonstrate you know it

Recall facts, definitions, and processes. State scientific information accurately. Show understanding through description and explanation of familiar content.

Command words: State, Define, Describe, Identify, Name
AO2 — Application

Apply it to something new

Use your knowledge in unfamiliar contexts. Carry out calculations. Apply principles to novel data or scenarios. Explain biological mechanisms in practical situations.

Command words: Explain, Calculate, Suggest, Apply, Predict
AO3 — Analysis

Analyse, evaluate, synthesise

Interpret data. Evaluate evidence and arguments. Make judgements and support them. Construct extended responses that weigh multiple factors.

Command words: Analyse, Evaluate, Discuss, Compare, Justify, Assess

Part 1 — Command Word Deep Dive

Click each command word to reveal what it actually demands of you

Why this matters more than you think

Most dropped marks in exams come from students knowing the biology but answering the wrong command word. Writing a brilliant "describe" answer when asked to "explain" still scores zero on the explanation marks. The command word is the instruction — treat it with precision.

AO1 Command Words

State / Name / Identify +
Demand: Give the answer in one or two words. No explanation required.

Examples from 4C: "Name the formula for the heterozygosity index." "State three adaptations of the camel that reduce water loss." "Identify the type of adaptation shown in fig A."

Strong answer: "Anatomical." Wasted writing: "It is anatomical because it involves the physical structure of the hump..." — this is a "state" question. You don't need the explanation.
Define +
Demand: Give the precise scientific meaning. Include all key qualifying words.

For 4C definitions, marks are often lost by omitting one key word. "Endemic" — not just "found in one place" but "evolves in geographical isolation AND is found in only one place." Both halves are required.

Strong: "A biodiversity hotspot is an area with a particularly high level of biodiversity." Weak: "A place with lots of species." — missing "particularly high" and lacks precision.
Describe +
Demand: State what can be observed. For graphs/maps: pattern + data. For processes: what happens. Do NOT explain why.

For 4C data questions (biodiversity maps, heart rate graphs, allele frequency tables): name the pattern, use specific values or areas, note exceptions. Explaining is a different command word.

Strong: "Species richness is highest in tropical regions, particularly the Amazon and Central Africa, with values above 200 species per 0.1 hectare. Arctic regions show the lowest richness, at 1–3 species." Weak (crosses into explain): "Species richness is highest in tropical regions because of stable climates..."

AO2 Command Words

Explain +
Demand: Give the biological reasons — the mechanism, the cause, the WHY. Build a chain of reasoning.

For 4C: every explanation link needs to be stated. "The camel allows its body temperature to vary 6°C → this delays sweating → sweating uses water → conserving water extends survival" — four separate points, each of which could score a mark.

Strong: "Bradycardia reduces heart rate from ~150 to ~20 beats/min, meaning less oxygen is consumed, so the seal can remain underwater longer." Weak: "Bradycardia helps the seal stay underwater." — no mechanism, just restates the observation.
Calculate +
Demand: Show every step. Write down the formula. State substituted values. Give the final answer with correct decimal places.

For D index and Hardy-Weinberg: never skip to the answer. Method marks are available. If your final answer is wrong but your method is right, you still score most of the marks. Always present a table for D calculations.

Strong method for H-W: q² = 0.16 → q = √0.16 = 0.4 → p = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6 → 2pq = 2×0.6×0.4 = 0.48 ✓ Check: 0.36 + 0.48 + 0.16 = 1.00. Weak: "0.48" — no method shown, only 1 mark available even if correct.
Suggest +
Demand: Apply knowledge to an unfamiliar situation. There may be more than one valid answer.

"Suggest" signals that the specific answer isn't in your notes — you need to reason from principles. Use the framework from studied examples. Marks are awarded for biologically sound reasoning, not for guessing the "right" answer.

Strategy: Identify the relevant biological concept, apply it to the scenario, link to the specific details given. Even if your answer isn't the "model" one, it can score full marks if the biology holds up.

AO3 Command Words

Compare +
Demand: Identify similarities AND differences. Structure as paired points, not two separate descriptions.

A common mistake: describing organism A fully, then organism B fully, with no direct comparison. A proper compare answer uses linking words — "similarly," "however," "in contrast," "both." Each point should address both subjects simultaneously.

Strong: "Both the camel and the kangaroo rat have physiological adaptations to produce concentrated urine, however only the camel can store fat in a specific location for metabolic water production." Weak: Two paragraphs describing each animal independently.
Evaluate / Assess +
Demand: Weigh evidence on both sides. Reach a supported judgement. A one-sided answer loses half the available marks.

Structure: (1) state the claim or position, (2) evidence supporting it, (3) evidence against it or limitations, (4) reasoned conclusion. Use specific named examples, not vague generalisations.

Strong structure: "D captures both richness and evenness, which is useful for comparing habitats. However, D does not measure genetic diversity, so two populations with identical D could differ in resilience. Overall, D is valuable but must be supplemented with heterozygosity data for full assessment."
Discuss +
Demand: Consider multiple aspects, perspectives, or factors. Present a balanced examination, not a single-sided argument.

"Discuss" is more open than "evaluate" — you don't need a definitive judgement, but you must present multiple angles. For 4C this often means considering ecological, genetic, and human factors together.

Example starter: "Several factors affect biodiversity in an ecosystem. The stability of environmental conditions is significant because... Productivity is also important since... Additionally, human activity has introduced new pressures..."
Justify +
Demand: Give reasons that support a stated position. Not balanced — you are defending the stated view.

Unlike "evaluate", a justify question has already assumed the position. You are building the case for it using evidence. Each supporting point should be specific and biological, not vague.

Example: "Justify the classification of the Hawaiian Islands as a biodiversity hotspot" — build the case: endemism rate, species richness, isolation, number of unique lineages. You don't argue against the classification.

Quick Check — Match Command Word to Demand

Command Word Check · Q1 MCQ

A question asks: "Explain how the variation in body temperature of a camel contributes to its survival in the desert." What is the examiner expecting?

Correct. "Explain" demands the mechanism — the chain of biological reasoning showing WHY this adaptation helps survival. A descriptive or definitional answer would score nothing on the explanation marks.
"Explain" demands the mechanism — not a definition, not a description. You need to build a chain of biological reasoning from the adaptation to its survival benefit.
Command Word Check · Q2 MCQ

A question reads: "Compare the biodiversity of tropical rainforest and temperate woodland ecosystems." Which approach scores best?

Correct. "Compare" requires paired points — each sentence addressing both subjects. Describing them separately is the most common mistake and loses structural marks.
"Compare" demands paired points, not parallel descriptions. Each sentence should address both ecosystems using linking language.

Part 2 — AO1 Practice

Knowledge and recall. Quick-fire questions across the unit.

1
Knowledge & Recall

These questions test what you know. Each should take 1–2 minutes. Write your answer, reveal the mark scheme, and self-mark honestly. A score of 10/12 or above on this section indicates solid AO1 foundations.

AO1 · Q1 · Define 2 marks

Define the term biodiversity and state the three scales at which it can be measured.



Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms and their genetic differences (1)
Measured at three scales: genetic diversity (within a species), species diversity (within an area), and ecosystem diversity (across habitats) (1)
AO1 · Q2 · State 1 mark

State the formula for the diversity index, defining every symbol used.



D = N(N−1) / Σn(n−1), where D = diversity index, N = total number of organisms of all species, n = number of organisms of each individual species, Σ = sum of all n(n−1) values (1)
AO1 · Q3 · MCQ 1 mark

Which of the following is a physiological adaptation?

Correct. Concentrated urine production is an internal biochemical/organ-level process — physiological, not anatomical or behavioural.
Physiological adaptations involve internal body processes — how the body works. The kidney function is the physiological adaptation here.
AO1 · Q4 · State 5 marks

List the five conditions required for a population to be in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium.



No mutations (1)
Random mating (1)
Large population (1)
Isolation — no immigration or emigration / no gene flow (1)
No selection pressure — all genotypes equally fertile / reproductively successful (1)
AO1 · Q5 · Define 2 marks

Define the terms niche and endemic species.



Niche: the role of an organism within the habitat in which it lives — describes how it feeds, interacts, and uses resources, not just where it lives (1)
Endemic species: a species that evolves in geographical isolation and is found in only one place (1)
AO1 · Q6 · State 3 marks

State three ecosystem services provided by biodiversity, giving a brief example for each.



Award 1 mark per valid service with example (any three of):
Food — diverse plant species support crop improvement and human diets (1)
Medicine — plant secondary metabolites provide pharmacological compounds (1)
Pollination — bees/insects enable fruit and seed production (1)
Flood regulation — root systems bind soil and slow water movement (1)
Nutrient cycling — bacteria/fungi decompose matter, releasing nitrates (1)
Carbon storage — plants/phytoplankton fix atmospheric CO₂ (1)
AO1 · Q7 · State 1 mark

State the formula for the heterozygosity index.



Heterozygosity index = number of heterozygotes ÷ number of individuals in the population (1)
AO1 · Q8 · Describe 3 marks

Describe the difference between species richness and relative species abundance, and explain why both are needed to measure biodiversity.



Species richness = number of different species in an area (1)
Relative species abundance = how evenly the species are distributed / proportional representation (1)
Both are needed because two areas can have identical richness but very different biodiversity if one is dominated by a single species (1)

AO1 total: 18 marks

Add up your self-scores. 14+ marks: strong foundations — move confidently to AO2. 10–13: some recall gaps to revisit. Below 10: go back to the relevant teaching lessons before continuing.

Part 3 — AO2 Practice

Applying knowledge. Calculations and unfamiliar scenarios.

2
Application

AO2 is where most exam marks live. These questions require you to apply what you know to new situations — calculations, novel data, unfamiliar organisms. Before the calculation questions, review the structure template.

Structure Template — Calculation Questions

USE FOR ALL CALCS
1
Write the formula

Write the formula at the top of your working. This alone can score a method mark.

2
Substitute values

Show each value being put into the formula. Don't skip steps.

3
Calculate step by step

Show intermediate working. For D index, always present a table of n(n−1) values.

4
State your answer with correct units/decimal places

D has no units. Heterozygosity index is decimal (0–1). H-W: give to 2–3 d.p.

5
Sanity check

H-W: p² + 2pq + q² should equal 1. D: should be between 1 and N. Heterozygosity: should be between 0 and 1. If not, find the error.

AO2 · Q1 · Calculate + Interpret 5 marks

A biologist samples invertebrates in a stream. She collects 12 mayfly larvae, 8 caddisfly larvae, 15 freshwater shrimp, 5 flatworms, and 10 stonefly larvae. Calculate the diversity index (D) and state what this value suggests about the stream.



Method (method marks awarded even if final answer has error):

n(n−1) calculated for each species: mayfly 12×11=132, caddisfly 8×7=56, shrimp 15×14=210, flatworm 5×4=20, stonefly 10×9=90 (1)
Σn(n−1) = 132+56+210+20+90 = 508 (1)
N = 50, N(N−1) = 50×49 = 2450 (1)
D = 2450/508 = 4.82 (accept 4.8–4.9) (1)
Interpretation: relatively high biodiversity — the stream supports several species with reasonable evenness; stonefly and mayfly presence also indicates unpolluted water (indicator species) (1)
AO2 · Q2 · Calculate + Explain 4 marks

A population of 75 Iberian lynx is analysed for a key gene. 6 individuals are heterozygous. Calculate the heterozygosity index and explain what this value suggests about the conservation status of this population.



Heterozygosity index = 6/75 = 0.08 (1)
This is a very low heterozygosity index / most individuals are homozygous (1)
The population has low genetic diversity — this makes it highly vulnerable to disease, as few individuals may carry resistance alleles (1)
The population is likely small and/or has experienced a bottleneck — conservation priority is warranted (1)
AO2 · Q3 · Calculate 5 marks

In a population of 2,500 mice, 400 show the recessive phenotype for short tail. Calculate the frequencies of all three genotypes in the population, and the expected number of heterozygous individuals.



q² = 400/2500 = 0.16 (1)
q = √0.16 = 0.4, so p = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6 (1)
p² = 0.36, 2pq = 2×0.6×0.4 = 0.48, q² = 0.16 (1)
Check: 0.36 + 0.48 + 0.16 = 1.00 ✓ (1)
Expected number of heterozygotes = 0.48 × 2500 = 1,200 mice (1)
AO2 · Q4 · Apply / Suggest 4 marks

The fennec fox (Vulpes zerda) lives in the Sahara Desert. It has unusually large ears, a pale sandy-coloured coat, thick fur on the soles of its feet, and is active mainly at dusk and dawn.

For each of the four features above, identify the type of adaptation and explain how it aids survival in the fennec fox's niche.



Large ears: Anatomical — large surface area increases heat loss by convection/radiation, helping thermoregulation in extreme desert heat (1)
Pale sandy coat: Anatomical — camouflage against desert sand reduces detection by predators and prey, and reflects solar radiation reducing heat absorption (1)
Thick fur on feet: Anatomical — insulates against scorching sand, allowing movement across hot surfaces without burn damage (1)
Active at dusk/dawn (crepuscular): Behavioural — avoids extreme midday temperatures, reducing water loss through panting and increasing survival (1)
AO2 · Q5 · Explain 4 marks

A population of insects is exposed to a new pesticide. After five generations, the frequency of the pesticide-resistance allele has increased from 0.05 to 0.42. Explain how this change has occurred.



The pesticide acts as a selection pressure — insects without the resistance allele die before reproducing (1)
Insects that carry the resistance allele (either homozygous or heterozygous) survive and reproduce, passing the allele to their offspring (1)
Over successive generations, the proportion of resistant insects in the population increases — the resistance allele frequency rises (1)
The population is not in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium — directional natural selection is driving change in allele frequency (1)

AO2 total: 22 marks

This is where most exam marks live. 17+ marks: strong application skills. 12–16: accurate knowledge but struggling to apply it — practise more novel scenarios. Below 12: revisit the structure template and worked examples from the teaching lessons.

Part 4 — AO3 Practice

Analysis, evaluation, and synthesis. Extended responses with structure templates.

3
Analysis & Evaluation

AO3 questions separate strong candidates from average ones. They require you to build an argument, weigh evidence, and reach a supported judgement. Before attempting, study the structure template and annotated model answer below.

Structure Template — Extended AO3 Response (PEEL+)

USE FOR EVALUATE/DISCUSS
P
Point — your position or claim

Open with a clear statement of what you will argue. No hedging.

E
Evidence — specific biological facts

Use named examples, values, mechanisms. Not "lots of species" — "178 species per hectare in Amazonian rainforest."

E
Explain — link evidence to your point

Show the biological reasoning. Don't assume the examiner will fill in the gap.

L
Link — connect back to the question and counter-consider

Address limitations or alternative views. This signals AO3 quality.

+
Judgement — a reasoned conclusion

State your overall position based on the balance of evidence. "Overall, X is more persuasive because..."

Annotated Model Answer — see the structure in action

QUESTION (6 marks)

Evaluate the usefulness of the diversity index (D) as a measure of biodiversity for conservation decision-making.

Model Answer with Annotation

The diversity index is a useful but incomplete measure of biodiversity and should be used alongside other indicators for conservation decisions.
P — Point Clear, balanced position stated upfront. Not a hedged "it depends" — commits to a view.
D captures both species richness and relative abundance in a single comparable value using N(N−1)/Σn(n−1). For example, two meadows each with 5 species can produce very different D values if one is dominated by grass at 95% while the other has even distribution.
E — Evidence Uses the formula and a specific scenario. The 95% grass example is the type of biological precision that scores marks.
This is useful for conservation because it quantifies both dimensions of species-level biodiversity simultaneously, enabling habitats to be ranked objectively and changes to be tracked over time — a polluted habitat will show a falling D.
E — Explain Links the evidence to the conservation purpose. Shows why the mathematical feature matters practically.
However, D has significant limitations. It does not measure genetic diversity — two populations with identical D could differ radically in heterozygosity and therefore in disease resilience. A cheetah-like population with low H would appear healthy on D alone.
L — Counter-consideration Directly addresses the weakness with a specific biological example. This is the AO3 move that lifts grades.
D also treats all species equally — a rare endemic species carries no more weight than a common one, yet its loss has far greater evolutionary significance. For decisions involving endangered species protection, the heterozygosity index and endemic species count are essential additions.
L — Extended counter Second limitation with direct conservation relevance. Goes beyond surface-level critique.
Overall, D is a valuable tool as part of a suite of measures — particularly for comparing habitats over time — but relying on D alone for conservation priorities would miss the genetic and evolutionary dimensions that ultimately determine long-term species survival.
+ Judgement Reasoned conclusion that returns to the original position with nuance. Not just a restated opening.
AO3 · Q1 · Evaluate 6 marks

"For any species, physiological adaptations are more important to survival than either anatomical or behavioural adaptations." Evaluate this statement, using named examples to support your answer.



Award marks for structured, evidenced evaluation:
Clear position stated (agree / disagree / partially) at opening (1)
Evidence supporting importance of physiological adaptations with named example — e.g. camel's ability to vary body temperature 6°C, produce concentrated urine, tolerate 30% water loss; seal bradycardia (1)
Explanation of mechanism — e.g. these are uncommon in other mammals and directly enable survival in conditions other adaptations alone could not manage (1)
Counter-evidence with example — anatomical (camel's hump provides the fat substrate, without which physiological metabolic water production is impossible) (1)
Further counter — behavioural adaptations (camels grouping together, sitting to reduce heat absorption) reduce the physiological load and therefore the survival challenge (1)
Reasoned judgement — all three types are interdependent; physiological adaptations may be most remarkable but the camel's survival relies on the combination, not any single type (1)
AO3 · Q2 · Discuss 6 marks

Discuss why biodiversity hotspots, despite their ecological value, are particularly difficult to conserve in practice.



Award marks for multi-perspective discussion:
Hotspots are often rich in natural resources (timber, minerals, agricultural land) creating direct economic conflict with conservation (1)
15.7% of land surface hosts 77% of terrestrial vertebrates — high value per area means high pressure per area (1)
Many hotspots are in developing regions where economic development priorities may override conservation (1)
Endemic species in hotspots cannot be conserved ex-situ easily / cannot be relocated because they require specific niche conditions (1)
Narrow niches of endemic species make them particularly vulnerable to even small habitat changes — partial conservation may not be enough (1)
Small populations in hotspots often have low genetic diversity, making conservation efforts vulnerable to disease and climate change even when habitat is preserved (1)
AO3 · Q3 · Analyse 5 marks

A conservation team studies two endangered bird populations on different islands. Population A has D = 5.8 and heterozygosity index = 0.08. Population B has D = 3.2 and heterozygosity index = 0.42. Both populations face habitat destruction. Analyse which population is in greater conservation danger, justifying your answer with biological reasoning.



Recognises both populations face risks but from different biological causes (1)
Population A: high D indicates diverse species-level community BUT very low heterozygosity (0.08) means genetic uniformity and extreme vulnerability to disease / environmental change (1)
Population B: moderate D (less species-diverse community) BUT high heterozygosity (0.42) means high genetic diversity and strong resilience (1)
Population A is in greater immediate danger because a single pathogen could devastate a genetically uniform population / low genetic diversity cannot be recovered once lost (1)
Justification references that species-level diversity (D) can be rebuilt through recolonisation, but genetic diversity lost through bottleneck cannot — making Population A's threat more existential (1)

AO3 total: 17 marks

These questions are the mark differentiators at the top end. 13+ marks: strong analytical writing. 9–12: argument present but lacking depth or structure. Below 9: revisit the PEEL+ template and model answer, then retry.

Part 5 — Anti-Patterns

Three weak answers. Spot why they fail before revealing the strong answer.

Why learn from weak answers?

Mark schemes reward specific things. Understanding how a superficially "reasonable" answer can still score zero is a skill examiners actively test for. Read each weak answer. Before revealing the feedback, ask yourself: what mark would I give this? Why?

EXAM QUESTION (5 marks)

Explain how the camel is adapted to survive in its desert niche.

Weak Answer 1 — "The list without mechanism" 1 / 5
"Camels have humps for storing water. They have long eyelashes to keep sand out of their eyes. They have big feet for walking on sand. They sit in groups in the sun. Their kidneys make concentrated urine."
What's wrong:
Factual error — the hump stores fat, not water (automatic mark deduction)
Every adaptation is described but none is explained — the command word is "explain" and this is a "describe" answer
No mention of adaptation types (anatomical/physiological/behavioural)
No mechanism — why do big feet help? Why does concentrated urine matter?
Examiner verdict: Shows factual knowledge but misses the command word entirely. Even with accurate recall, "explain" questions need biological mechanism chains.
Weak Answer 2 — "The right words, wrong depth" 2 / 5
"Camels have anatomical, physiological, and behavioural adaptations. Their anatomical adaptations include the hump and flat feet. Their physiological adaptations include being able to survive in the heat. Their behavioural adaptations include sitting down. All of these help them survive in the desert because the desert is hot and dry."
What's wrong:
Uses the correct terminology but without biological precision — "being able to survive in the heat" is circular, not a mechanism
No specific physiological processes named — no mention of variable body temperature, concentrated urine, metabolic water, blood cell expansion
Final sentence states the conclusion without connecting mechanism to outcome
Recognises the framework but doesn't apply it — marks are awarded for biological specificity, not terminology alone
Examiner verdict: This student has read the textbook's chapter structure but hasn't absorbed the biological content. Correct category labels with empty content don't score.
Weak Answer 3 — "Detail in the wrong places" 3 / 5
"Camels have a hump on their back which contains fat tissue. The fat is a lipid made of glycerol and fatty acids joined by ester bonds through condensation reactions. When the fat is broken down it releases water, so the camel can survive without drinking. Camels also have closable nostrils."
What's wrong:
Two marks awarded — correctly identifies fat in hump and links to metabolic water (1), mentions closable nostrils (1)
But the detail about glycerol/fatty acids/ester bonds is irrelevant to this question — it shows knowledge from another topic but wastes time and shows lack of focus
Covers only two adaptations — with 5 marks available, at least 4–5 different adaptations with mechanisms are needed
No mention of physiological or behavioural adaptations at all
Examiner verdict: Shows promising biological detail but poor exam technique. Students often over-invest in the first point and run out of time/space. Cover breadth then depth.
Strong Answer — Mechanism + breadth + command word 5 / 5
"The camel has anatomical adaptations including a hump containing fat — when fat is broken down by aerobic respiration, metabolic water is produced as a by-product, allowing survival without drinking for up to 10 days. Its large, flat feet spread weight across loose sand, preventing the camel from sinking. Physiologically, the camel can allow its body temperature to vary by 6°C, delaying the onset of sweating and conserving water in the heat. Its kidneys produce highly concentrated urine, minimising water loss through excretion. Red blood cells can expand to 240% of normal volume, allowing the camel to drink up to 180L in 24 hours without cell lysis. Behaviourally, camels sit with legs tucked under them in the morning to reduce heat absorption from the hot ground, and group together to reduce exposed surface area — both reduce sweating and conserve water."
Why this scores full marks: ✓ Addresses all three adaptation types ✓ Each adaptation has a mechanism (not just a name) ✓ Links each mechanism back to the desert survival challenge (heat/water) ✓ No factual errors — hump correctly identified as fat ✓ Uses specific data (240%, 180L, 6°C) ✓ Command word "explain" is directly addressed in every clause.

Now you try — spot the weaknesses yourself

EXAM QUESTION (4 marks)

In a population of 1,600 beetles, 576 are homozygous recessive for wing colour. Calculate the expected number of heterozygous individuals.

Student answer ? / 4
"576 ÷ 1600 = 0.36. 2pq = 0.48. Answer: 768 beetles."

Before revealing — how many marks would you award this answer? Where did the student lose marks (or potentially gain them)?



This answer scores 2/4.
✓ Correct q² = 576/1600 = 0.36 (1 mark — though method isn't shown, the final value is correct)
✗ Missing: the crucial step of q = √0.36 = 0.6 (0 marks — no working shown for this step)
✗ Missing: p = 1 − 0.6 = 0.4 (0 marks — not stated)
✓ The 2pq = 0.48 is correct, and 0.48 × 1600 = 768 (1 mark — correct final calculation)
✗ No check that p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (no mark available for this but best practice)

Examiner comment: "The student gets the right final answer but loses method marks for not showing the chain of working. Had they worked through q and p explicitly and shown the substitution into 2pq = 2 × 0.4 × 0.6, all 4 marks would have been awarded. The lesson: always show every step in calculation questions. Correct final answers with missing method typically score only 50% of available marks."
