Question 1 · From 4C.1
What is biodiversity and at which three scales can it be assessed?


Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms and their genetic differences. It can be assessed at three scales: genetic diversity (variation between individuals within a species), species diversity (variety of different species within an area), and ecosystem diversity (variety of different habitat types and ecological communities).
Question 2 · Prior knowledge
In biology, what is natural selection? State the four conditions required for it to occur.


Natural selection: the process by which individuals with advantageous heritable characteristics are more likely to survive, reproduce, and pass those characteristics on. Four conditions: (1) variation exists between individuals; (2) some variation is heritable; (3) there is competition (struggle for survival); (4) individuals with advantageous traits have greater reproductive success.
Question 3 · From 3B / 2C
What is the difference between an organism's genotype and its phenotype? How do both contribute to an organism's ability to survive?


Genotype: the genetic makeup of an organism — the specific alleles it carries. Phenotype: the observable characteristics of an organism, produced by the interaction of genotype and environment. Genotype provides the inherited blueprint; phenotype is the expressed form that natural selection acts on. An organism with a phenotype well-matched to its environment (e.g. thick blubber in cold water) has a survival advantage.
Question 4 · Thinking ahead
A camel can survive for up to 10 days in the desert without water. What types of biological adaptation (physical structure, body chemistry, or behaviour) might allow this? Have a go before the lesson covers it.


No single answer expected here. The lesson will cover three categories: anatomical (physical features — e.g. hump stores fat, slit-shaped nostrils), physiological (internal body processes — e.g. producing very concentrated urine, tolerating large water loss without osmotic damage) and behavioural (actions — e.g. sitting with legs tucked to minimise ground heat absorption, grouping together). The most remarkable adaptations are physiological.

Task A — Key Vocabulary

Complete each definition in your own words, then reveal the precise scientific version to check.

Term: Ecology


Ecology: the study of the relationships of organisms to one another and to their physical environment. It examines how living things interact with each other and with abiotic factors such as temperature, light, and water availability.
Term: Niche


Niche: the role of an organism within the habitat in which it lives. It describes not just where an organism lives but how it feeds, when it is active, how it reproduces, and how it interacts with other species — rather like a job description. Niches can be broad (eating grass) or very narrow (cleaning the teeth of larger animals).
Term: Anatomical adaptation


Anatomical adaptation: an adaptation involving the form and structure of an organism — its physical shape, body parts, or surface features. Examples: thick blubber in seals, sticky hairs on a sundew leaf, large flat feet on a camel.
Term: Physiological adaptation


Physiological adaptation: an adaptation involving the way the body works, including differences in biochemical pathways or enzymes. These are internal processes that cannot be seen directly. Examples: the mammalian diving response (bradycardia), production of highly concentrated urine in camels, anaerobic respiration in diving mammals' muscles.
Term: Behavioural adaptation


Behavioural adaptation: an adaptation involving programmed or instinctive behaviour making organisms better adapted for survival. Examples: lizards orientating towards sunlight in the morning, camels sitting with legs tucked to minimise heat absorption, birds migrating to avoid harsh conditions, social hunting in predators.

(c) The pre-dive heart rate is approximately 150 beats/min. During the dive it drops to approximately 20 beats/min. Based on this, how long might the dive have lasted without the bradycardia?


Without bradycardia, the heart would beat at ~150 beats/min, consuming oxygen at a much higher rate. During bradycardia the rate drops to ~20 beats/min — approximately 1/7th of the normal rate. Oxygen is therefore used ~7× more slowly, so the seal could theoretically sustain approximately 7× longer underwater than without this response. This is a rough calculation — the actual extension depends on other factors such as muscle oxygen store (myoglobin) and anaerobic respiration.

This is a physiological adaptation — it involves the way the body works (a change in heart rate controlled by the autonomic nervous system and diving reflex) (1)

Bradycardia reduces the rate at which oxygen is consumed by the heart and non-essential organs — oxygen-carrying blood is redirected to the brain and heart only (1)
This allows the seal to remain underwater far longer than non-diving mammals, directly enabling its predatory feeding behaviour and survival in its marine niche (1)

Task C — Fungal Carnivores: Reading for Mechanism

Arthrobotrys anchonia — the lasso fungus

Some fungi that usually break down dead matter have evolved to become active carnivores. Arthrobotrys anchonia actively lassos nematode worms as they pass through loops of its hyphae called constriction rings. When a nematode moves into a ring, a combination of wall changes and osmotic potential of the cells result in water moving in fast — within 0.1 seconds the ring inflates and holds the nematode. The fungus then grows hyphae that penetrate and digest the nematode, absorbing nutrients and transporting them within the fungus. These fungi provide an unusual source of nitrates in high-carbon, low-nitrogen environments such as bogs.

Identify one anatomical, one physiological, and one behavioural adaptation in Arthrobotrys anchonia and explain how each helps it survive in its niche.


Anatomical: Constriction rings made of hyphal loops — the physical structure of the ring allows it to encircle and trap nematodes, giving the fungus access to a nitrogen source in an otherwise nitrogen-poor environment.

Physiological: Osmotic changes in ring cells causing rapid water uptake — the biochemical change in osmotic potential causes water to move in by osmosis in under 0.1 seconds, inflating the ring faster than the nematode can escape. This is an internal biochemical process.

Behavioural: Actively positioning hyphae in nematode paths / growing hyphae that penetrate the nematode body — the active growth toward and into prey represents a form of programmed biological behaviour enabling nutrient acquisition.

Task A — Compare Adaptation Strategies

The camel and the lasso fungus occupy very different niches, but both face the challenge of obtaining nutrients in a resource-poor environment. Study the comparison below, then answer the analytical questions.

Camel — Desert Herbivore

Resource challenge: extreme heat, almost no water, sparse vegetation. Survival strategy: physiological conservation (storing water, tolerating dehydration), anatomical insulation, behavioural heat avoidance. Obtains food by browsing widely on diverse desert plants.

Arthrobotrys — Bog Carnivore

Resource challenge: high carbon, almost no nitrogen, saturated anaerobic soil. Survival strategy: evolved carnivory to access nitrogen from nematode bodies. Uses anatomical traps, physiological osmotic response, and active hyphal growth behaviour.

Question 1 · Compare
Both organisms face extreme resource limitation. Compare how each organism's physiological adaptations solve its specific resource problem. What does this tell us about the relationship between niche and adaptation type?


The camel's physiological adaptations are primarily conservative — they focus on retaining a scarce resource (water) rather than acquiring a new one. Variable body temperature, concentrated urine, and tolerant blood cells all reduce water loss. In contrast, Arthrobotrys's physiological adaptation (osmotic ring inflation) is acquisitive — it enables the organism to capture a resource (nitrogen) that would otherwise be unavailable. Both strategies are physiological responses to resource scarcity, but they reflect fundamentally different niches: the camel's niche is built around conservation; the fungus's niche is built around predation. This suggests that the type of adaptation that evolves is shaped by the specific nature of the resource limitation — what is scarce and how it can be obtained.
Question 2 · Apply to an unfamiliar organism
The Arctic fox (Vulpes lagopus) lives in tundra environments where temperatures drop below −50°C and prey is scarce for months. It has white fur in winter (brown in summer), stores food by caching it in frozen ground, has thickly furred paws, and can lower its metabolic rate significantly during cold periods.

For each of the four features above, state whether it is anatomical, physiological, or behavioural, and explain how it helps survival in the Arctic niche.


White fur in winter: Anatomical (seasonal colouration) — provides camouflage against snow, reducing detection by prey and predators; improves hunting success and reduces predation risk (1)
Caching food: Behavioural — storing excess food when prey is available allows the fox to access nutrition during lean winter months when prey is almost absent; extends survival without active hunting (1)
Thickly furred paws: Anatomical — insulates the extremities from frozen ground, preventing frostbite and maintaining blood circulation to limbs essential for locomotion and hunting (1)
Lowering metabolic rate: Physiological — reduces the rate of energy expenditure during cold, food-scarce periods; allows the fox to survive on fat reserves for longer without starving (1)
Question 3 · Reason about niches
The textbook states that niches can be "very large and general" or "very small and specific." Using examples from this lesson, explain why very narrow niches carry a higher extinction risk than broad niches.


An organism with a narrow niche depends on very specific conditions or resources. If those conditions change — through climate change, habitat loss, or the loss of a key prey species — the organism has no alternative strategy and faces extinction. For example, the lasso fungus occupies a very specific niche (predating nematodes in nitrogen-poor bogs). If nematode populations collapsed, the fungus would have no alternative nitrogen source. A camel, by contrast, is a browser — it eats a wide variety of vegetation and can tolerate a wide range of temperatures and water availability. This broad niche gives it more flexibility. More broadly: endemic species (from 4C.1) often occupy narrow niches on islands — their specificity is exactly why they are so vulnerable to introduced species or environmental change. Broad niches act as a buffer against change; narrow niches provide precision but increase vulnerability.

Task A — Make the Case

A biologist claims: "For the camel, physiological adaptations are more important to survival than either anatomical or behavioural ones." Build a structured argument — agree, disagree, or qualify. Use evidence from this lesson.

Argument Builder

Complete each section. Your position must be supported by specific biological evidence, not general statements.

Model position: Partially agree — physiological adaptations are most remarkable, but the combination is what makes the camel the "ultimate survivor."

For: The physiological adaptations are the most unusual and most directly linked to desert survival. The ability to allow body temperature to vary 6°C before sweating begins, to produce metabolic water from fat oxidation (~21L from 20kg of fat), to produce highly concentrated urine, and to rehydrate rapidly without osmotic damage — none of these could be substituted by behaviour or anatomy. They represent fundamental biochemical differences from most mammals.

Counter: Anatomical adaptations (flat feet, hump as fat store, protective lashes) provide the physical substrate that makes everything else possible. Without the hump, there is no fat to oxidise; without flat feet, movement on sand is compromised. Behavioural adaptations (grouping together, sitting in the morning, browsing widely) reduce heat load and extend food access — without them, the physiological mechanisms would be working much harder.

Conclusion: The camel is best understood as a system where all three types are interdependent. The physiological adaptations are the most impressive and least common in other mammals — so the biologist's claim has merit — but "most important" is a false distinction. An organism without any one category of adaptation would be less fit for its niche. The power of the camel's survival strategy is the coherence of all three working together.

Command Words for this Topic

DESCRIBE

For adaptations: state what the adaptation is (name the feature), state what it does, but do not explain the mechanism. "The camel has slit-shaped nostrils that can be closed" is a description. "This prevents sand entering the airways" crosses into explanation — save that for EXPLAIN questions.

EXPLAIN

You must give the biological mechanism — the why. For adaptation questions: name the feature → state what it does physiologically/anatomically/behaviourally → link directly to survival advantage in the niche. Every step in the chain must be stated. "The camel allows its body temperature to vary 6°C — this delays sweating — sweating uses water — conserving water extends survival without drinking" is a full explanation chain.

EVALUATE

State a position. Support it with evidence. Provide a counter-argument with evidence. Draw a conclusion that makes a judgement. For adaptation evaluation questions: use specific named adaptations as evidence — do not make vague generalisations about "lots of different adaptations."

SUGGEST

Apply your biological understanding to an unfamiliar context. There may be no single right answer — marks go to biologically sound reasoning. State what type of adaptation it is, describe the feature, and link to the specific environmental challenge. Use what you know from the studied examples as a framework.

Common Mistakes on this Topic

✗

Classifying physiological adaptations as anatomical

The most common error. "The camel can produce concentrated urine" is physiological — it's a biochemical/organ function. Students frequently call this anatomical. Ask yourself: can you see it? If not, it's likely physiological.

✗

Describing adaptations without explaining how they help survival

Exam hint from the textbook is explicit: "If you describe an adaptation be sure you can explain how it helps survival." Saying "camels have a hump" is worth nothing without "the fat in the hump is broken down in aerobic respiration, releasing metabolic water."

✗

Saying the camel's hump stores water

It does not. The hump stores fat. The metabolic water is produced when that fat is broken down by aerobic respiration. This is a very common misconception that will cost marks in any exam.

✗

Confusing niche with habitat

The habitat is where an organism lives (desert, pond, bog). The niche is its role in that habitat — what it eats, when it feeds, how it interacts. Two species can share a habitat but not a niche. Using the words interchangeably will lose marks.

Worked Example Answer

EXAM QUESTION (5 marks)

How do the anatomical and physiological adaptations of camels help them survive in their hot, dry, plant-browsing niche?

Model Answer (5/5)

Camels have several anatomical adaptations for desert survival. Their large, flat feet spread their weight across a greater surface area, preventing them from sinking into sand and allowing efficient movement. The hump is an anatomical fat store — importantly, it stores fat, not water. When this fat is broken down by aerobic respiration, metabolic water is released as a by-product, which can sustain the camel for extended periods without drinking. Long eyelashes and slit-shaped closable nostrils protect the eyes and airways from sand — essential for an animal that must keep moving to find food.

Physiologically, the camel can allow its core body temperature to vary over 6°C. This delays the onset of sweating during the morning and evening, conserving water. When fully dehydrated, a camel can lose up to 30% of its body water without cellular damage — most mammals' cells would experience fatal osmotic disruption at far lower losses. The kidneys produce highly concentrated urine, minimising excretory water loss. Dromedary red blood cells can expand to 240% of their normal volume without bursting, allowing the camel to drink up to 180 litres in 24 hours and rapidly restore blood volume after dehydration without lysing its own cells.

Why this scores full marks:

✓ Multiple anatomical adaptations named with mechanisms ✓ Hump correctly identified as fat store (not water) ✓ Multiple physiological adaptations with mechanisms ✓ Each adaptation linked to the survival challenge ✓ No confusion between anatomical and physiological
