⭐ Task 1: My Carbon, My Choice

⏱ 15–20 minutes 👥 Group work 📚 Y7 Science: Fossil Fuels

The Big Idea

Fossil fuels power almost everything in modern life — but burning them releases CO₂, which drives climate change. Who is responsible for that? Big companies? Governments? Or each of us individually?

In this task, you will reflect honestly on your own values and take a personal position — something no AI can do for you.

Step by Step
  1. 1

    On your own (3 mins): Think back to this morning. In the box below, list 3 things you did that required fossil fuels — for example: being driven to school, heating at home, using a device that was charged overnight. Be honest with yourself.

  2. 2

    As a group (5 mins): Share your lists. Whose morning used the most fossil fuels? Why might that be? Is it fair that some people's lives use more fossil fuels than others?

  3. 3

    As a group (6 mins): Read the statement below, then each person must give their own view. You cannot simply agree with the person before you.

"The biggest responsibility for climate change lies with individuals, not corporations."
Think about: Who makes decisions about which fuels are used? Who profits from fossil fuels? Who pays the environmental cost of climate change?
  1. 4

    As a group (5 mins): Write one Group Position Statement (2–3 sentences) that everyone can honestly stand behind. If you genuinely disagree, your statement must acknowledge both views rather than pretending there is consensus.

  • Each person's list of 3 fossil fuel uses from their morning (written individually first)
  • Each person's individual position on the statement — 1–2 sentences each, in their own words
  • One agreed Group Position Statement (2–3 sentences) written together

An AI chatbot could generate a position statement about climate responsibility in seconds. But here is the question: would it reflect your values, based on your morning?

The Big Idea

Fossil fuels have powered economic development for 200 years — but the harm they cause is not shared equally. Some countries burn the most; others suffer the worst consequences.

In this task, your group will debate global responsibility — and discover how even the AI tools you might use at home are part of this story.

🤝 Task 3: Formation Chain Challenge

⏱ 15–20 minutes 👥 Group work 📚 Y7 Science: Fossil Fuels

The Big Idea

Coal, oil, and natural gas all formed over millions of years through a similar process: organic remains buried under pressure and heat. Today you are going to reconstruct that story as a team.

The key rule: every single person in the group must genuinely understand their part. You cannot copy from each other. You cannot look it up. Each person has to build their own understanding — and teach it to the group.

The Rules
  1. 1

    Your teacher will give your group a set of shuffled Event Cards describing how a fossil fuel formed. Your task is to arrange them in the correct order.

  2. 2

    Work together to sequence the cards — but no one person can move more than half the cards. Everyone must contribute to the ordering, and you must agree as a group before locking in the sequence.

  3. 3

    Once your sequence is agreed, each person is assigned 2–3 cards from the sequence. You have 5 minutes to prepare a spoken explanation of your section in your own words.

  4. 4

    Your group presents the Formation Story aloud — each person explaining their section in order, with no gaps and no repetitions. It must flow as one continuous story.

  5. 5

    After the full story, each person must answer one question from a group member about someone else's section. So listen carefully to everyone, not just your own part!

Key Science to Include
Fossil Fuel Key Stages in Formation
Coal Dead land plants (trees, ferns) → fall into swampy water → mud burial prevents rotting → millions of years of pressure and heat → coal
Oil & Natural Gas Dead sea creatures and algae → sink to sea floor → buried under mud and sand → millions of years of pressure and heat → crude oil and natural gas
Why "non-renewable"? Formation takes hundreds of millions of years — far longer than any human lifetime. Once burned, the fuel is gone forever. We are using fossil fuels millions of times faster than they can form.

📝 Your Group Must Produce

  • A correctly sequenced set of Event Cards (ready to show your teacher)
  • An uninterrupted spoken Formation Story — each person explains their assigned section
  • Each person answers at least one question from a group member about a different section
🤖 AI Literacy Moment

An AI can produce a description of coal formation — but it doesn't understand it. AI predicts the next most statistically likely word, based on patterns in the data it was trained on. It has no mental model of pressure, time, or buried swamps.

Today, you built understanding differently: by physically working with the ideas, discussing and disagreeing with your group, and having to explain them to real people who will ask you real questions.

Q: What is the difference between an AI generating a correct answer and a person genuinely understanding something? How did this task help you tell the difference — in yourself and in your group members?

💬 Task 4: 60-Second Science Broadcast

⏱ 15–20 minutes 👥 Group work 📚 Y7 Science: Fossil Fuels

The Big Idea

Scientists and science journalists must explain complex ideas clearly — without reading from notes, without jargon, and without getting it wrong. That requires genuine understanding, not just knowing the right words.

Today your group runs a live 60-second science broadcast about fossil fuels. Each person covers a different story. After each broadcast, the group asks at least one real question.

Your Broadcast Roles
Role Your 60-Second Story
Reporter A What are fossil fuels and how did they form? Cover both coal and oil/gas — include the key similarities and differences in their formation processes.
Reporter B How are fossil fuels used in power stations to generate electricity? Describe the sequence of energy transfers: what form is the energy in at each stage?
Reporter C What are the environmental problems caused by burning fossil fuels? Name specific gases, explain what they do to the atmosphere and environment.
Reporter D What does "non-renewable" mean and why does it matter? Explain how quickly we are using fossil fuels compared to how long they took to form.
Step by Step
  1. 1

    Prepare (5 mins): Use only your science knowledge and class notes. No phones, no AI tools. Prepare to speak for 60 seconds — not read aloud. Aim to explain, not recite.

  2. 2

    Broadcast (8 mins): Each Reporter delivers their piece while the rest of the group listens. Time it — exactly 60 seconds!

  3. 3

    Questions (5 mins): After each broadcast, at least one group member asks a genuine question — not "that was great", but something they actually want to know. The Reporter must answer from memory.

  4. 4

    AI Fact-Check Challenge (last 4 mins): Your teacher will give you an "AI Transcript" — a real AI response about power stations. Your group's job is to find the scientific mistake and write a correction.

💡 Task 5: Power Station 2075

⏱ 15–20 minutes 👥 Group work 📚 Y7 Science: Fossil Fuels

The Scenario

It is 2075. After decades of climate warnings, the world's last accessible fossil fuel reserves were exhausted in 2068. Your school needs a new energy solution — and your group has been hired to design it.

This is a design challenge. Your solution must be grounded in real science, reflect your group's genuine values, and show you understand energy transfers — not just energy sources.

The Brief

Your school energy system must:

  • Power lights, heating, computers, and a kitchen
  • Use realistic renewable energy sources (solar, wind, wave, geothermal, biomass, hydroelectric — or a combination)
  • Show at least one energy transfer chain — e.g. light energy → electrical energy → heat energy
  • Acknowledge at least one trade-off or limitation of your chosen source(s)
  • Be something your whole group genuinely believes in, not just what sounds impressive
Step by Step
  1. 1

    Brainstorm (4 mins): Each person suggests at least one renewable energy source. List them all — no judgment yet. What are their advantages? What are their problems?

  2. 2

    Select and justify (5 mins): As a group, agree on your solution. Everyone must voice their opinion. Record why you chose it — not just what it is. If there was disagreement, record that too.

  3. 3

    Design (8 mins): On paper, sketch your power system for the school. Label it with: the energy source, the energy transfer chain, at least one advantage, and at least one limitation. Divide the drawing — each person contributes part of it.

  4. 4

    Present (3 mins): Each person explains one part of the design — what they drew and why.

📝 Your Group Must Produce

  • A labelled sketch of your school energy system — drawn on paper by the group together
  • Annotations showing: energy source, energy transfer chain, one advantage, one limitation
  • One sentence per person: "I argued for _____ because..."

🤖 AI Literacy Moment

If you asked an AI to design your school's energy system, it would generate something — probably a well-structured, sensible-sounding answer using solar panels and wind turbines.

But ask yourself: would it care whether it actually works for your school's location, budget, or community? Would it reflect what your group genuinely values? Would it weigh up the same trade-offs you debated?

🔍 Task 6: Fact, Flaw, or Fiction?

⏱ 15–20 minutes 👥 Group work 📚 Y7 Science: Fossil Fuels

The Big Idea

AI tools can produce text that sounds completely confident — even when it is wrong. This is called "hallucination": the AI generates a plausible-sounding answer that is not accurate, because it is pattern-matching rather than genuinely reasoning.

Your science knowledge is your best tool for evaluating claims — whether they come from a textbook, a website, or an AI chatbot. Today you will use it.

The Claims — Classify Each One

For each statement: (1) classify it as TRUE / PARTIALLY TRUE / FALSE, and (2) write the scientific knowledge that justifies your classification. Work through them together but everyone must contribute.

# Statement Classification Scientific justification (use your knowledge!)
1 Coal forms when dead sea creatures sink to the ocean floor and are compressed by layers of sand and mud over millions of years. TRUE / PARTIALLY / FALSE
2 Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, which traps heat in the atmosphere and contributes to global warming. TRUE / PARTIALLY / FALSE
3 Fossil fuels are called "non-renewable" because they take millions of years to form and cannot be replenished within a human lifetime. TRUE / PARTIALLY / FALSE
4 Sulphur dioxide released from burning fossil fuels dissolves in rainwater to form acid rain, which can harm aquatic life, forests, and buildings. TRUE / PARTIALLY / FALSE
5 Oil and coal are formed from the same types of organic material — mainly land-based trees and plants buried in bogs. TRUE / PARTIALLY / FALSE

🤖 AI Literacy Moment

AI tools learn to produce text by finding statistical patterns in huge amounts of human writing. They do not check facts — they predict what a plausible answer looks like. This means AI can be confidently wrong.

It can mix up coal and oil formation, get cause and effect backwards, or add detail that sounds scientific but is inaccurate. It cannot tell the difference between a correct science explanation and a plausible-sounding wrong one — because it doesn't actually understand the science.

The OECD's AI literacy framework states that people who understand AI know three things: (1) AI can be wrong, (2) AI outputs must be verified by humans with domain knowledge, and (3) the ability to evaluate AI claims is a critical skill for everyone — not just scientists or engineers.

Q: Which piece of science knowledge from this unit was most useful for catching the AI's mistakes? What does that tell you about why learning science matters in an age of AI?
