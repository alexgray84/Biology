01Mon

Ecosystems, Quadrats, Food Chains & Energy Transfer

Mini Poster & Fieldwork Simulation
What you need to know

A population is all the organisms of one species in a habitat. A community is all the populations of different species in a habitat. A habitat is where an organism lives. An ecosystem includes the community and its physical (abiotic) environment. Abiotic factors include temperature, light, water, pH, and soil minerals. Biotic factors include competition, predation and disease. You must describe how to use quadrats to estimate population size. For feeding relationships: producers → primary consumers → secondary consumers → tertiary consumers, plus decomposers. Energy transfer between trophic levels is about 10% because energy is lost as heat from respiration, in movement, and in undigested material.

Tasks
  1. "Ecosystem in a box" mini poster (20 min): On one A4 page, create a colourful mini poster of an ecosystem (e.g. a pond, a woodland, or a coral reef). Draw organisms from at least 4 trophic levels. Label each organism with its trophic level name (producer, primary consumer, etc.). Draw arrows showing the direction of energy flow. Include at least one decomposer. Around the border, list 3 abiotic factors and 3 biotic factors that affect this ecosystem. This poster becomes a revision tool you can stick on your wall.
  2. Quadrat fieldwork simulation (10 min): Imagine your bedroom floor is a habitat. Draw a grid representing 10 quadrat positions. "Count" an imaginary organism in each quadrat (make up reasonable numbers: 3, 5, 2, 7, 4, 6, 3, 5, 8, 2). Now calculate the estimated population size:

    Mean per quadrat = total count ÷ number of quadrats
    Estimated population = mean per quadrat × (total area ÷ quadrat area)

    Practise this calculation — it appears regularly in the exam. Then write one sentence on why quadrats should be placed randomly (to avoid bias).
  3. 10% energy rule explanation (10 min): Draw a simple food chain (grass → rabbit → fox). Draw energy bars next to each organism, getting smaller at each level. Annotate where the energy goes at each step:
    • Lost as heat from respiration
    • Lost in movement
    • Lost in undigested material (faeces)
    • Only ~10% passed to the next trophic level
    Then answer: why are food chains rarely longer than 4–5 trophic levels? (Not enough energy left to sustain another level.)
  4. Pyramid quick-draw (5 min): Sketch an example of a pyramid of numbers, a pyramid of biomass, and a pyramid of energy transfer. Note: pyramids of numbers can be inverted (e.g. one tree supporting many insects), but pyramids of energy are always upright.
  5. Spaced retrieval (5 min): From Week 4: define "allele" and "genotype." From Week 3: name the four blood components.
Spec precision: Arrows in food chains show the direction of energy transfer, not "what eats what." The arrow points from the organism being eaten to the organism eating it. E.g. grass → rabbit means "energy flows from grass to rabbit."

02Tue

Carbon Cycle & Pyramids

Jigsaw Diagram & Memory Walk
What you need to know

The carbon cycle describes how carbon is recycled through ecosystems via four key processes: photosynthesis (removes CO2 from the atmosphere), respiration (returns CO2 to the atmosphere — all living things), decomposition (decomposers break down dead organisms, releasing CO2 through respiration), and combustion (burning fossil fuels releases CO2). You also need to be confident with pyramids of number, biomass and energy transfer.

Tasks
  1. Carbon cycle "jigsaw" challenge (20 min): Take a blank A4 sheet. In the centre, draw a large circle and write "CO2 in atmosphere." Around it, draw four boxes labelled: "Plants," "Animals," "Dead organisms/waste," "Fossil fuels." Now draw arrows between them and label each arrow with the correct process:
    • CO2 → Plants: Photosynthesis
    • Plants → CO2: Respiration
    • Plants → Animals: Feeding
    • Animals → CO2: Respiration
    • Plants/Animals → Dead organisms: Death/excretion
    • Dead organisms → CO2: Decomposition (decomposers respire)
    • Dead organisms → Fossil fuels: Fossilisation (over millions of years)
    • Fossil fuels → CO2: Combustion
    Now cover the labels and try to re-label every arrow from memory. Repeat until perfect.
  2. "Memory walk" — carbon cycle narration (10 min): Stand up and walk around your room. As you walk, narrate the journey of a single carbon atom through the entire cycle, starting from CO2 in the air: "I'm absorbed by a plant during photosynthesis, becoming part of a glucose molecule. The plant is eaten by a rabbit, so I'm now in the rabbit's body. The rabbit respires, and I'm released back into the atmosphere as CO2..." Continue through decomposition and combustion routes. The physical movement helps encode the sequence in a different way.
  3. Pyramid practice (10 min): For this food chain: oak tree → caterpillars → blue tits → sparrowhawk — draw:
    • A pyramid of numbers (inverted at the base — one tree supports many caterpillars)
    • A pyramid of biomass (upright — the tree has the most biomass)
    • A pyramid of energy transfer (always upright)
    Annotate why the pyramid of numbers is inverted but the others are not.
  4. Past paper question (5 min): Complete one carbon cycle exam question from the resources below.
  5. Spaced retrieval (5 min): From Week 4: describe what happens during meiosis in one sentence. From Week 2: name the enzyme that digests proteins in the stomach.
Exam tip: The carbon cycle diagram appears almost every series. The most commonly missed arrow is decomposition — students forget that decomposers (bacteria and fungi) respire, which is how they release CO2. Always write "decomposers release CO2 through respiration" not just "decomposition releases CO2."

03Wed

Pollution, Greenhouse Effect & Eutrophication

Cause-&-Effect Chain & Debate Prep
What you need to know

Air pollution: SO2 causes acid rain (damages plants, acidifies lakes). CO reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen. Greenhouse gases: water vapour, CO2, nitrous oxide, methane, CFCs. Human activities (burning fossil fuels, deforestation, farming) increase greenhouse gas levels, enhancing the greenhouse effect, potentially leading to global warming and its consequences (rising sea levels, changing weather patterns, habitat loss). Water pollution: sewage causes deoxygenation of water and death of aquatic organisms. Eutrophication: excess fertiliser leaches into water → algal bloom → blocks light → plants die → bacteria decompose dead organisms → bacteria use up O2 → aquatic organisms die.

Tasks
  1. Cause-and-effect chains (15 min): For each of the following, write a step-by-step chain using arrows, with each step on a new line. This is the exact format 6-mark answers need:

    Chain 1 — Eutrophication:
    Fertilisers leach into waterways →
    Excess nitrates/phosphates stimulate rapid algal growth (algal bloom) →
    Algae block sunlight reaching underwater plants →
    Plants cannot photosynthesise and die →
    Decomposing bacteria break down dead organisms →
    Bacteria use up dissolved oxygen through aerobic respiration →
    Aquatic organisms (e.g. fish) die due to lack of oxygen

    Chain 2 — Enhanced greenhouse effect:
    Humans burn fossil fuels / deforestation / farming →
    Increased CO2, methane, N2O in atmosphere →
    More heat energy trapped by greenhouse gases →
    Global temperatures increase (global warming) →
    Consequences: ice caps melt → sea levels rise → habitats lost → changing weather patterns

    Now cover your chains and rewrite them from memory.
  2. "60-second debate" — for or against (10 min): Set a timer for 60 seconds. Argue for the statement: "Farmers should be banned from using artificial fertilisers." Then reset the timer and argue against the same statement for 60 seconds. This forces you to understand both the environmental consequences (eutrophication) and the agricultural benefits (increased crop yield to feed growing populations). Write down two key points from each side.
  3. Pollutant-disease match-up (10 min): On scraps of paper, write these pollutants on one set: SO2, CO, excess CO2, sewage, fertiliser run-off. On another set, write the consequences: acid rain, reduced oxygen transport in blood, enhanced greenhouse effect, deoxygenation of water, eutrophication/algal bloom. Shuffle both sets face down and try to match each pollutant with its consequence. Time yourself — can you do it in under 30 seconds?
  4. Greenhouse gases list (5 min): Without notes, list all five greenhouse gases named in the spec (water vapour, CO2, nitrous oxide, methane, CFCs) and one human activity that increases each.
  5. Spaced retrieval (5 min): From Week 3: describe how the body responds when it is too hot (two mechanisms). From Week 1: name two food tests and their positive results.
Common exam error: Students lose marks on eutrophication by writing "the algae use up the oxygen." No — the algae block light, causing plants to die. It is the decomposing bacteria that use up the oxygen. Getting this causal sequence correct is essential for full marks.

04Thu

Food Production — Crops, Micro-organisms & Fermenters

Annotated Diagram & Recipe Method
What you need to know

Glasshouses/polythene tunnels increase crop yield by controlling temperature and CO2. Fertilisers provide minerals for growth. Pest control: pesticides (chemical — quick, but may harm other organisms and accumulate in food chains) vs biological control (using natural predators — no chemicals, but slower and predator may become a pest itself). For micro-organisms: yeast in bread (anaerobic respiration produces CO2 which makes dough rise). Lactobacillus in yoghurt production (ferments lactose in milk, producing lactic acid which causes milk protein to coagulate). You must know the features of an industrial fermenter: aseptic conditions, nutrient supply, optimal temperature and pH, oxygenation, and agitation.

Tasks
  1. Glasshouse "before and after" (10 min): Draw two side-by-side sketches: a crop field outdoors vs a glasshouse. Annotate the glasshouse with: controlled temperature (heaters/ventilation), increased CO2 (from gas burners or cylinders), protection from pests, water supply. For each, write one sentence explaining how it increases yield. Link back to photosynthesis: higher temperature and CO2 increase the rate of photosynthesis → more glucose → more growth.
  2. Pesticides vs biological control — "pros and cons" speed challenge (10 min): Set a 5-minute timer. Write as many advantages and disadvantages as you can for pesticides. Then reset and do the same for biological control. Compare your lists:
    • Pesticides: + Fast, effective, wide coverage. – May harm beneficial insects, can accumulate in food chains, pests may develop resistance, ongoing cost.
    • Biological control: + No chemicals, specific to pest, self-sustaining. – Slow to establish, introduced organism may become a pest itself, may not eliminate pest entirely.
  3. "Recipe card" — yoghurt production (10 min): Write the yoghurt-making process as a recipe card with numbered steps, as if writing instructions for a food science class:
    1. Heat milk to 72°C to kill unwanted bacteria (pasteurisation)
    2. Cool to ~40°C (optimum temperature for Lactobacillus)
    3. Add Lactobacillus bulgaricus starter culture
    4. Incubate at ~40°C for several hours
    5. Lactobacillus ferments lactose (milk sugar) → produces lactic acid
    6. Lactic acid causes milk proteins to coagulate/solidify → yoghurt forms
    7. Cool and store to prevent further bacterial activity
  4. Fermenter diagram annotation (10 min): Draw a simple industrial fermenter from memory (a large vessel with an input pipe, output pipe, stirrer, and monitoring probes). Label and explain why each feature is needed:
    • Aseptic conditions: Sterilised before use to prevent contamination by unwanted microorganisms
    • Nutrient supply: Provides food for microorganism growth
    • Temperature control (water jacket): Maintains optimum temperature for enzymes
    • pH monitoring: Kept at optimum for enzyme activity
    • Oxygenation (air supply): Needed for aerobic respiration of microorganisms
    • Agitation (stirrer/paddle): Ensures even distribution of nutrients, oxygen, and temperature
  5. Spaced retrieval (5 min): From Week 4: explain how antibiotic resistance develops (3 key steps). From Week 2: write the word equation for anaerobic respiration in animals.
Spec precision: The spec says yeast is used in the production of food, including bread. In bread, yeast carries out anaerobic respiration: glucose → ethanol + CO2. The CO2 makes the dough rise. The ethanol evaporates during baking.

05Fri

Selective Breeding & Genetic Modification

Flowchart Build & Role-Play Pitch
What you need to know

Selective breeding (artificial selection): choose organisms with desired characteristics → breed them together → select offspring with desired traits → repeat over many generations. Works for both plants (e.g. disease resistance, higher yield) and animals (e.g. increased milk production, meat quality). Genetic modification: restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific sites → desired gene is cut out → ligase enzymes join the gene into a vector (plasmid or virus) → vector inserts recombinant DNA into a host organism (e.g. bacterium). You must know how GM bacteria produce human insulin in a fermenter, how GM plants improve food production, and that "transgenic" means transferring genetic material from one species to a different species.

Tasks
  1. Selective breeding flowchart (10 min): Draw a flowchart showing the selective breeding process. Use a specific example (e.g. breeding cows for higher milk yield). Your flowchart should have at least 5 boxes connected by arrows:

    Choose cows with highest milk yield → Breed them with a bull from a high-yield line → Select offspring that produce the most milk → Breed these selected offspring together → Repeat over many generations → New breed reliably produces high milk yield

  2. GM insulin "assembly line" diagram (15 min): Draw the step-by-step process for producing human insulin from GM bacteria as a numbered sequence with diagrams at each step:
    1. Identify the human insulin gene
    2. Use a restriction enzyme to cut the gene from human DNA
    3. Use the same restriction enzyme to cut open a bacterial plasmid (creates matching "sticky ends")
    4. Insert the human gene into the plasmid using ligase enzyme to join them
    5. The plasmid is now recombinant DNA
    6. Insert the recombinant plasmid into a bacterium
    7. Grow the GM bacteria in a fermenter at scale
    8. Bacteria produce human insulin, which is extracted and purified
    Draw a simple sketch for each step — even stick figures of enzymes "cutting" and "gluing" DNA.
  3. "Dragon's Den" pitch — role play (10 min): Imagine you are a scientist pitching a GM crop to a panel of investors. Write and deliver (aloud) a 90-second pitch explaining: what the GM crop does (e.g. pest-resistant maize), how it was made (gene transfer using restriction enzymes, ligase, and a vector), why it's beneficial (higher yield, less pesticide use), and one potential concern you'd address (e.g. effects on biodiversity, unknown health effects). This forces you to use all the key terminology in a connected argument.
  4. "Transgenic" definition drill (5 min): Write the definition of "transgenic" from memory three times. It must include: transfer of genetic material, from one species, to a different species. Then give one example (e.g. human insulin gene inserted into bacteria).
  5. Spaced retrieval (5 min): From Week 3: draw a quick sketch of a reflex arc with all 5 components labelled. From Week 1: name the cell organelle where aerobic respiration occurs.
Common exam error: Students confuse restriction enzymes and ligase. Remember: restriction enzymes cut DNA (think "restrict = cut/limit"). Ligase joins DNA (think "ligate = tie together"). Both appear in virtually every GM question.

Weekly Consolidation — Topics 4 & 5 Combined Review

Retrieval Grid & Full Paper Section
What this session is for

This is your final consolidation before Week 6's full-syllabus review. Today you'll pull together everything from Topics 4 and 5 and identify any remaining gaps before the final week of timed practice.

Tasks
  1. "Blurt it" retrieval grid (15 min): Draw a 3×4 grid. Label the 12 cells with these topics: Ecosystem terms, Quadrats, Food chains/webs, Pyramids, Carbon cycle, Air pollution, Greenhouse effect, Eutrophication, Glasshouses & fertilisers, Yoghurt & bread, Selective breeding, Genetic modification. Set a 15-minute timer. Write everything you can remember in each cell without any notes. Use keywords, mini diagrams, equations — whatever comes to mind. When the timer stops, check against your notes and fill gaps in a different colour.
  2. Traffic-light spec checklist (5 min): Go through 4.1–4.17 and 5.1–5.16. Mark green/amber/red.
  3. Re-revise red/amber (5 min): Focus on your weakest two topics only. Re-test yourself on these immediately.
  4. Timed mini-test (20 min): Under strict exam conditions:
    1. Define the terms "population" and "community." (2 marks)
    2. A student counted 8 daisies in a 0.5 m² quadrat. The total field area is 200 m². Estimate the total daisy population. Show your working. (2 marks)
    3. Explain why only about 10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next. (3 marks)
    4. Describe the stages of the carbon cycle, starting with CO2 in the atmosphere. (4 marks)
    5. Explain the process of eutrophication caused by fertiliser leaching into a river. (4 marks)
    6. Describe the steps involved in the genetic modification of bacteria to produce human insulin. (4 marks)
    7. Compare pesticides and biological control as methods of pest management. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of each. (4 marks)
  5. Error analysis (5 min): Mark your test and categorise each error as: K (knowledge gap), R (misread question), I (incomplete answer), P (imprecise terminology). Note which categories dominate — this tells you what to focus on in Week 6.
Looking ahead to Week 6: Next week is your final week — full syllabus consolidation, exam technique, and timed full paper practice. There is no new content. Use Sunday to gather all your red/amber topics from Weeks 1–5 into one list. That list becomes your priority for Monday.
