01Mon

Weak-Area Blitz — Target Your Red & Amber Topics

Spaced Retrieval + Targeted Practice
What this session is for

Over the past five weeks, you have been traffic-lighting your spec checklist. Today is about honestly confronting the topics you still find difficult and attacking them head-on. This is the highest-value revision session of the entire plan — working on weaknesses has a far bigger impact than re-reading topics you already know.

Tasks
  1. Compile your "hit list" (10 min): Go through every traffic-light checklist from Weeks 1–5. Write a single list of all your red and amber topics. Group them by theme:
    • Diagrams I can't draw: e.g. heart, reflex arc, eye, leaf cross-section, carbon cycle
    • Processes I can't sequence: e.g. eutrophication, GM insulin production, ventilation mechanism
    • Definitions I'm fuzzy on: e.g. osmosis, denaturation, transgenic, excretion vs egestion
    • Calculations I avoid: e.g. quadrat population estimates, Punnett square probabilities
  2. "3-2-1 blitz" for each weak topic (30 min): For every topic on your hit list, do this rapid cycle:
    • 3 minutes: Re-read the relevant notes or watch a short video clip
    • 2 minutes: Close your notes and write/draw everything you can remember
    • 1 minute: Check what you missed and correct in a different colour
    Aim to get through at least 5 weak topics in 30 minutes. This is intense but extremely effective.
  3. Re-make flashcards for persistent gaps (10 min): For any facts or definitions that keep slipping, make new flashcards. Keep them with you all week — test yourself in spare moments (waiting for the bus, between lessons, before bed).
Mindset note: Finding topics you still don't know is not a sign of failure — it's a sign that your self-assessment is working. Every red topic you convert to green this week is marks gained in the exam. The students who perform best are the ones who are honest about their gaps.

02Tue

Exam Technique Workshop — Command Words & Extended Responses

Metacognition & Model Answers
What this session is for

Knowing the content is only half the battle. The other half is knowing how to answer the question. Today is about understanding command words, structuring 6-mark extended responses, and avoiding the mistakes that cost marks even when you know the biology.

Tasks
  1. Command word card sort (10 min): Cut or tear pieces of paper. On each, write one command word from the specification. On separate pieces, write the definition. Shuffle and match them. The key ones to master:
    Command WordWhat It MeansWhat You Must Do
    State / Name / GiveRecall a factShort answer, no explanation needed
    DescribeGive an account ofSay what happens — step by step if a process
    ExplainGive reasonsSay what happens AND why — every point needs a reason
    CompareIdentify similarities and differencesMust do BOTH — use "whereas" or "but" to link
    SuggestApply knowledge to an unfamiliar contextUse what you know to make a reasonable proposal
    CalculateWork out a numerical answerShow your working — even if the answer is obvious
    EvaluateWeigh up evidenceDiscuss strengths AND weaknesses, then reach a conclusion
  2. 6-mark answer structure drill (20 min): The extended response is worth 6 marks and appears at the end of the paper. Practise this structure using three different questions:

    PEE chains: Point → Evidence → Explain. Aim for 3 PEE chains = 6 marks.
    Example question: "Explain how the small intestine is adapted for absorption." (6 marks)
    PEE 1: Villi increase the surface area (P) → they are finger-like projections lining the intestine wall (E) → more surface available for absorption of digested food (E)
    PEE 2: Villi have thin walls, only one cell thick (P) → this minimises the diffusion distance (E) → so nutrients pass quickly into the blood (E)
    PEE 3: Each villus has a rich network of blood capillaries (P) → absorbed nutrients are carried away quickly (E) → maintaining a steep concentration gradient for continued diffusion (E)

    Now write your own 6-mark answers to:
    • "Explain the effect of temperature on enzyme activity." (6 marks)
    • "Explain how a population of bacteria can become resistant to antibiotics." (6 marks)
  3. "Spot the lost mark" activity (10 min): Read these deliberately flawed answers and identify what mark(s) would be lost:
    • "Osmosis is the movement of water from high to low concentration." → Missing: "through a partially permeable membrane" AND should specify "water concentration" not just "concentration"
    • "Mitochondria produce energy." → Should say "release energy through aerobic respiration." Energy cannot be produced.
    • "The bacteria became resistant because of the antibiotic." → Wrong causal direction. The mutation was random and happened before the antibiotic.
    • "Bile digests fat." → Bile emulsifies fat (breaks large droplets into smaller droplets). It does not chemically digest it — lipase does.
  4. Mark your own 6-mark answers (10 min): Go back to your two 6-mark answers above. Be a strict examiner. Award 1 mark per valid scientific point, up to 6. Did you hit 6? If not, add the missing points.
Golden rule for "explain" questions: Every sentence should contain a because, so, which means, or therefore. If your sentence doesn't link cause to effect, it's a "describe" answer, not an "explain" answer — and you'll lose marks.

03Wed

Practical Skills & Data Analysis Bootcamp

Applied Practice
What this session is for

Around 19–21% of the paper (AO3) tests experimental skills, data analysis, and evaluation of methods. These marks are available on every topic — and students who practise them specifically pick up marks that others leave behind. Today is entirely focused on practical contexts, graph interpretation, and experimental design.

Tasks
  1. Variables quick-fire (5 min): For each investigation below, identify the independent variable (what you change), dependent variable (what you measure) and two control variables (what you keep the same):
    • Investigating the effect of temperature on enzyme activity
    • Investigating the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis
    • Investigating osmosis using potato cylinders in different sugar concentrations
  2. Graph interpretation challenge (15 min): Find or sketch the following graph types, then practise answering questions about them:
    • Enzyme activity vs temperature graph: Read off the optimum temperature. Explain why the rate falls above the optimum. Calculate the rate of increase between two points.
    • Limiting factor graph (photosynthesis): Identify the limiting factor at different points on the graph. Explain what causes the plateau.
    • Population growth curve: Describe the trend. Suggest reasons for any plateau (limiting factors — food, space, predation, disease).
    For each graph, practise the skill of describing the trend before explaining it. Examiners award separate marks for description and explanation.
  3. Practical methods recall (15 min): Without notes, write the method for each of these spec-required practicals in as much detail as you can. Then check:
    • Testing a leaf for starch (include safety: ethanol + water bath)
    • Investigating osmosis with potato cylinders
    • Investigating enzyme activity at different temperatures
    • Using quadrats to estimate population size
  4. "Improve this method" exercise (10 min): Read this deliberately poor method and rewrite it correctly:

    "We put some potato in sugar water and weighed it after. The potato got lighter so osmosis happened."

    Your improved version should include: specific concentrations, measuring and recording initial mass, blotting dry before re-weighing, repeating for reliability, calculating percentage change in mass, identifying the independent and dependent variables, and explaining the results in terms of water concentration.
  5. CORMS evaluation checklist (5 min): Memorise this framework for evaluating any practical:
    • C — Conclusion: does the data support the hypothesis?
    • O — Odd results: any anomalies? Should they be removed from the mean?
    • R — Reliability: did you repeat? Were results consistent?
    • M — Method: any improvements? Better equipment? More concentrations?
    • S — Safety: any hazards? How were they managed?
Common mark-loser: When asked to "describe the trend shown in the graph," many students jump straight to explanation. Always describe first: "As [x] increases, [y] increases/decreases/stays the same." Only then explain why.

04Thu

Full Timed Past Paper — Exam Simulation

Exam Simulation
What this session is for

This is your dress rehearsal. You will sit a complete Biology Paper 1B under strict exam conditions. The goal is not just to test your knowledge but to practise pacing, question selection, and managing the pressure of a 2-hour paper.

Tasks
  1. Set up your exam environment (5 min):
    • Clear your desk. Only your paper, a pen, a pencil, a ruler and a calculator
    • Put your phone in another room
    • Set a timer for exactly 2 hours
    • Have a glass of water nearby
    • No music, no breaks, no notes
  2. Sit the paper (2 hours): Use one of the following sources for a full Paper 1B:
    • Save My Exams mock papers (link below)
    • A past paper from PMT or your teacher
    • The Week 1 Q&A PDF combined with questions from your weekly mini-tests (if no full paper is available)
  3. Pacing guide:

    110 marks in 120 minutes ≈ 1 minute per mark + 10 minutes reading/checking time.
    Multiple choice: ~15 min. Short answer sections: ~60 min. Extended responses: ~25 min. Checking: ~10 min.
    If stuck on a question for more than 2 minutes, move on and come back.

During the paper: Read every question twice before writing. Underline command words. Check how many marks are available — this tells you how many points to make. For calculations, always show working. For diagrams, use a pencil and ruler where appropriate.

05Fri

Mark, Review & Targeted Re-Revision

Error Analysis + Final Gap Closure
What this session is for

Today you turn yesterday's paper into a precision revision tool. Self-marking with a mark scheme is one of the most powerful learning activities — it forces you to compare your answer with the exact wording that earns marks.

Tasks
  1. Self-mark your paper (25 min): Using the mark scheme, go through every question. Be strict. Award marks only where your answer matches the mark scheme's required points. Write your total score and the percentage.
  2. Error analysis — categorise every lost mark (10 min): For each mark you lost, write the question number and assign it a category:
    CodeError TypeWhat It Means
    KKnowledge gapDidn't know the fact or concept
    RMisread questionAnswered a different question than asked
    IIncomplete answerKnew it but didn't write enough points
    PImprecise languageUsed wrong terminology or vague wording
    CCalculation errorMaths mistake or didn't show working
    Count how many marks you lost in each category. This tells you whether you need more content revision (K) or more exam technique (R, I, P, C).
  3. Targeted re-revision (15 min): Using your error analysis:
    • For K errors: Go back to the specific topic and use the 3-2-1 blitz method from Monday
    • For R errors: Re-read the questions you misread. What tripped you up? Practise underlining command words
    • For I errors: Look at how many marks the question was worth. Practise writing one point per mark
    • For P errors: Make a "precision language" flashcard for each term you got wrong
    • For C errors: Redo the calculations, showing full working
  4. Rewrite your worst 3 answers (10 min): Pick the three questions where you lost the most marks. Rewrite each answer as a perfect model answer, using the mark scheme as a guide. Read each model answer aloud once.
Perspective check: If you scored 70+ out of 110, you're solidly in grade 7–9 territory with improvement still to come. If you scored 50–70, you're in the 5–7 range and the re-revision you're doing today could push you up a grade or more. If below 50, focus entirely on K errors — content gaps are your biggest lever.

06Sat

Final Review — Speed-Run, Teach-Back & Confidence Check

Retrieval + Self-Explanation
What this session is for

This is your last revision session. The goal is to touch every topic one final time — not deeply, but enough to activate the memory so it's fresh for the exam. Think of it as warming up before a race.

Tasks
  1. "Speed-run" through all 5 topics (25 min): Set a timer for 5 minutes per topic. For each topic, write down everything you can recall on a blank sheet — key terms, diagrams, equations, processes, named examples. Don't look anything up. Just write. When the timer goes, move to the next topic.
    • 5 min: Topic 1 — Nature & variety of living organisms
    • 5 min: Topic 2 — Structure & functions in living organisms (this is huge — focus on your weakest sub-topics)
    • 5 min: Topic 3 — Reproduction & inheritance
    • 5 min: Topic 4 — Ecology & the environment
    • 5 min: Topic 5 — Use of biological resources
    After all 5 topics, check what you missed against your notes. These are your very last gaps — learn them now.
  2. "Teach it back" challenge (15 min): Pick your 3 hardest topics from the entire course. For each one, explain it aloud as if you are teaching a Year 9 student who has never heard of it before. Speak for 2 minutes per topic. If you can explain it simply and completely, you truly understand it. If you stumble or stall, that's the gap — fill it immediately.
  3. Equations and definitions final check (5 min): Write from memory:
    • Photosynthesis: word equation + balanced symbol equation
    • Aerobic respiration: word equation + balanced symbol equation
    • Anaerobic respiration: word equations (animals AND plants/yeast)
    • Definitions: osmosis, denaturation, active transport, pathogen, excretion, transgenic, natural selection
  4. Final confidence rating (5 min): Go through the complete end-of-course checklist below. For each statement, give yourself an honest rating. Anything not ticked is something to glance at one final time tonight or tomorrow morning.
You've done the work. Six weeks of structured, evidence-informed revision. You've covered every specification point, practised under timed conditions, analysed your errors, and closed your gaps. Trust the preparation. Get a good night's sleep before the exam — it consolidates memory far better than last-minute cramming. You've got this.
