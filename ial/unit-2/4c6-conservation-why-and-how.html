Part 1 — See · Think · Wonder

A thinking routine that trains observation before interpretation

Allow 6 minutes
👁

See-Think-Wonder

Start with what you can see. Then interpret what you think it means. Then ask what you wonder. This order matters — don't skip to interpretation.

A Visible Thinking Routine · Harvard Project Zero

How to use this routine

Most people jump from "I see this" to "I think it means..." without noticing. This routine slows you down. You observe precisely, then reason from your observations, then ask questions that push your thinking further. These three steps are the foundation of scientific analysis.

The first stimulus — the human population over 2,000 years

Fig A — World human population from year 0 to 2000. Population now exceeds 7 billion.
Step 1 · See

What do you notice?

Write only what you can literally observe in the graph. No interpretation yet. Describe the shape, specific values, patterns. Use numbers.

Good observations (seeing):
The line stays close to zero from year 0 until about year 1700
Around 1800 the line starts to rise visibly
After 1900 the line rises steeply, almost vertically
By 2000, the population is over 7 billion
The shape of the curve is often called a "hockey stick"

Note how none of these include a reason or interpretation — just what's visible.
Step 2 · Think

What do you think might be going on?

Now use biological and historical reasoning to explain what you observed. What events or changes could have caused this pattern? What might the consequences be?

Good interpretations (thinking):
The flat section before 1700 suggests populations were limited by food supply, disease, and infant mortality
The steep rise from around 1800 aligns with the Industrial Revolution — mechanised farming, better food supply, improved medicine
The vertical climb after 1900 is probably driven by antibiotics, vaccines, and further agricultural advances
This rapid growth must put increasing pressure on natural resources, habitats, and other species
Biodiversity loss seen today is likely to be a direct consequence of this population explosion
Step 3 · Wonder

What do you wonder?

Ask genuine questions that the graph raises. Questions without obvious answers. Good wonderings lead to further investigation.

Good wonderings:
I wonder what happened to other species populations during this same period
I wonder if there's a limit to how big the human population can grow
I wonder how many species have gone extinct since 1800 compared to before
I wonder if any human action could slow this curve
I wonder if the relationship between human population and biodiversity loss is inevitable, or if it's the way we live, not how many of us there are

Notice how wonderings are open questions that drive further inquiry — they are the foundation of good science.

Part 2 — The Human Threat to Biodiversity

Active reading with expert-level questions

Allow 10 minutes
Read like an expert

As you read the content below, hold this question in your mind: "What specifically about modern human life drives biodiversity loss — is it our numbers, our behaviour, or both?" This question has no single correct answer. Your task is to reach your own view by the end.

The human population explosion

As people learned to farm plants and animals to provide a reliable supply of food, more children survived and populations grew. But this was on a fairly small and local scale. Later, tools and then machines enabled us to farm on a much bigger scale. Now we can reshape the environment with reservoirs, roads, canals, towns, and cities. We have developed medicines that keep us and our children alive — allowing many of us to survive to great age. We have developed engines that burn fossil fuels and release large quantities of exhaust gases into the atmosphere. We cut down huge areas of rainforest to grow crops or rear cattle. We have developed floating factories that can clear fish from whole areas of the oceans — very different from local fishing boats that provide for a family or a village market.

All of these activities have been driven by basic desires to provide for ourselves with food, shelter, and goods. We appear to be having a major impact on many of the ecosystems of the Earth as an unintended consequence. Scientists have increasing evidence that we are affecting the global climate, biological resources, and biodiversity around the world.

Climate change

From individual observations to major scientific studies, there is growing agreement that the Earth's climate is changing. Global temperatures are rising. This is seen in overall climate patterns and in the increasing number and frequency of "extreme weather events" all over the world. The world's climate has changed regularly over time — the fossil record shows us how often the world has gone through ice ages and periods of tropical heat or desertification. The difference is that this time, it is happening quickly and there is a growing body of evidence to suggest the changes are the direct result of human activities. These changes mean that many plants and animals will no longer survive in their current ranges.

Depletion of biological resources

A growing world population means an increase in the demand for resources such as food, firewood, and land. In more economically developed parts of the world, people have gone way beyond simply fulfilling the basic needs of life. They eat far more food than they need and want a great variety of different foods, which may need to be imported from far away. As a result, biological resources are being depleted and ecosystems destroyed, both on land and in the oceans. Biodiversity is destroyed along with ecosystems.

Conservation as a response

Biodiversity is being reduced through activities such as overfishing, habitat reduction, and climate change. However, many people around the world are working to reduce these trends and conserve the biodiversity we still have. This can be tackled in many different ways.

Conservation
Maintaining and protecting a living and changing environment. It is an active process involving an enormous range of projects — reclaiming land after industrial use, setting up sustainable agriculture, protecting single threatened species, global legislation on pollution and greenhouse gases, and more.

Examples of conservation projects include:

  • Reclaiming land after industrial use
  • Helping set up sustainable agriculture systems in the developed world
  • Protection of a single threatened species
  • Global legislation on pollution levels and greenhouse gas emissions

Key distinction — preservation vs conservation

Preservation means keeping something exactly as it is now. Conservation recognises that living environments constantly change — so conservation aims to maintain and protect while allowing natural change. A forest conservation project, for example, does not try to freeze the forest; it works to allow natural regeneration, manage human use, and support biodiversity over time.

Pause and synthesise
Come back to the expert question: is it our numbers, our behaviour, or both that drives biodiversity loss?


A strong answer would argue it is both — but differently weighted. Absolute numbers matter because even basic needs (food, shelter) scaled across 7+ billion people put enormous pressure on ecosystems. But behaviour matters more in developed countries — eating far more than needed, shipping food from far away, and clearing rainforest for cattle cause disproportionate harm per person. A smaller population behaving sustainably would cause less damage than a slightly smaller population behaving as we do now. Both factors interact: population size amplifies the effect of any harmful behaviour.

Part 3 — Frayer Model: Ex-situ vs In-situ

Active vocabulary construction — build understanding, don't just read it

Allow 8 minutes

Why a Frayer Model?

The Frayer Model is a vocabulary strategy that builds deep understanding by forcing you to think about a term from four angles: its definition, its characteristics, examples of it, and — critically — non-examples (things it is NOT). This final step is the one most students skip, and it's the one that prevents confusion in exams.

The two key terms

Around the world, there are many animals and plants threatened with extinction — often as a result of human activities causing habitat loss or climate change. There are two main ways of conserving animals and plants:

Ex-situ conservation (ex = outside)
The conservation of components of biological diversity (living organisms) outside their natural habitats. Examples: seed banks, zoos, botanic gardens, captive breeding programmes.
In-situ conservation (in = in place)
The conservation of ecosystems and natural habitats, and maintenance and recovery of viable populations of species in their natural surroundings. Examples: nature reserves, national parks, marine protected areas.

Ex-situ conservation is always seen as a complementary approach to in-situ conservation — not a replacement. Ideally, ex-situ conservation occurs in the country where the threatened species originates, so the genetic material can be reintroduced to the natural habitat.

Part 4 — Seed Banks and Captive Breeding

The two main ex-situ methods in depth

Allow 12 minutes
Read like an expert

Hold this question: "Plant conservation looks much easier than animal conservation — is that true, and why?" You'll be asked to answer this formally in Part 7.

Ex-situ conservation of plants — seed banks

It has been estimated that 25% of the world's flowering plant species could disappear within the next 50 years. There are about 242,000 known flowering plant species — so this could mean 60,500 species disappearing in less than one human lifetime.

Plants are of vital importance to our lives. The genetic material of extinct species would be lost forever — a disaster for the plants and potentially for human survival. Cross-breeding crop plants back to wild plants, or using wild plants for genetic engineering, are ways to maintain the long-term health of our crop plants.

Botanic gardens (zoos for plants) maintain collections of many of the world's most interesting and unusual plants. In the 1960s, the Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew (UK) set up a seed bank that is now home to the seeds of around 80,000 plants — the Millennium Seed Bank. Its two aims: (1) collect and conserve the seeds of the entire UK native flora (completed 2009 — of the 1442 native UK plants, over 300 are already threatened with extinction); (2) conserve seeds of an additional 25% of the flora of the whole world by 2020, focussing on the drylands which are experiencing rapid habitat loss. Similar seed banks now exist in Sri Lanka and the UAE.

A seed bank can preserve many plants in a state of effective suspended animation. Live seeds are collected, checked by X-ray for fully developed embryos, then dried and stored between −20°C and −40°C. Many will survive and remain capable of germinating for up to 200 years. In general, the lifespan of a seed doubles for every 5°C drop in temperature or 2% fall in relative humidity. Some stored seeds may even germinate in several thousand years.

Most plants make huge numbers of seeds, so they can be collected without damaging the natural population. Seeds are usually quite small, so large numbers can be stored cheaply in small space. They contain all the genetic material of the plant. There are now over 1,000 seed banks around the world.

But seed banks have limits

About 80% of known plant species could be stored in seed banks, but the seeds of some species don't store well — including many important crop plants: mango, rubber, oak, avocado, cacao, and coconut. These plants need to be conserved differently, often in field gene banks (plantations, orchards, arboretums) or as tissue cultures. The International Potato Centre in Peru contains around 4,100 clones of potato, all of which need to be planted annually.

Ex-situ conservation of animals — captive breeding

It is not always possible to conserve animal species in the wild because the conditions that threatened them continue. Zoos and wildlife parks used to exist just for people to look at animals, but today they are very important in animal conservation.

Captive breeding programmes
Programmes where individuals of an endangered species are bred in zoos and parks in an attempt to save the species from extinction, and if possible to reintroduce them to their natural wild environment.

Reintroduction does not always work, but it can succeed in national parks or protected areas. Species saved by captive breeding and successfully reintroduced include Californian condors and Przewalski's horses. Captive breeding programmes for the white and black rhino, along with much conservation work in East Africa, give hope these creatures may also be saved.

The problems with captive breeding

❖ There's not enough space or resources in zoos and parks for all the endangered species.
❖ It's often difficult to provide the right conditions for breeding, even if scientists know what those conditions are. The giant panda, for example, is notoriously difficult to breed even in ideal conditions.
❖ Reintroduction will be unsuccessful unless the original reason for the species being pushed toward extinction is removed first.
❖ Animals bred in captivity may have great problems adjusting to unsupported life in the wild.
❖ When the population is small, the gene pool is reduced — this can cause serious problems. Zoos try to overcome this by keeping detailed records of genetic data and swapping sperm between zoos for artificial insemination to maximise genetic variation in offspring.
❖ Reintroduction programmes can be very expensive and time-consuming — and they may fail.

Did you know?

The Frozen Ark Project

Set up in 1996, the Frozen Ark aims to conserve genetic resources of animals by storing their DNA and viable cells — the animal equivalent of the Millennium Seed Bank. DNA is very stable and can be stored frozen for hundreds of years. The 10 million samples take up very little room. This gives scientists the possibility of increasing genetic variation in critically endangered species, or even cloning extinct species once their habitat has been restored. The Frozen Ark does not replace in-situ and ex-situ conservation — it supports them.

Part 5 — Claim · Support · Question

A thinking routine for evaluating complex ideas

Allow 10 minutes
💡

Claim-Support-Question

Make a claim about the topic. Find support for your claim — specific examples, evidence, reasons. Then generate questions that challenge or extend your claim. This routine builds evaluation skills you need for AO3 exam questions.

A Visible Thinking Routine · Harvard Project Zero

The focus for this CSQ

You'll apply Claim-Support-Question to this statement: "Captive breeding programmes in zoos are an effective way to save endangered species." This is contested — there are strong views on both sides. Your task is to take a position and support it properly.

How to do CSQ well

Claim: take a clear position — don't hedge. Support: use specific evidence from the reading (named examples, specific problems, real case studies). Generic statements don't count. Question: genuine questions that would challenge or test your claim further — not rhetorical ones.

Step 1 · Claim

Take a clear position. You might agree, disagree, or give a qualified view ("agree, but only when...").



Sentence stems if you're stuck
  • Captive breeding is effective when...
  • Captive breeding is NOT effective because...
  • Captive breeding can be effective but only if...
  • The effectiveness of captive breeding depends on whether...
Step 2 · Support

Give 2–3 pieces of specific evidence that supports your claim. Name actual species, programmes, case studies, or problems from the reading. Avoid generic statements like "it can work sometimes".



Evidence you might use
  • The successful reintroduction of Californian condors and Przewalski's horses shows that...
  • Captive breeding programmes for black and white rhinos in East Africa demonstrate that...
  • The well-known difficulty of breeding giant pandas in captivity suggests that...
  • The problem of small gene pools — zoos manage by swapping sperm between zoos — shows that...
  • The fact that animals bred in captivity often struggle to adjust to the wild suggests that...
  • If the original threat (poaching, habitat loss) is not removed, reintroduction fails — this shows that...
Step 3 · Question

Generate 2–3 questions that probe your own claim — things you don't yet know, challenges to your position, or what would change your mind. Good questions show strong thinking.



Question starters
  • Is captive breeding really the best use of conservation funding — what about...?
  • Would my claim still hold if...?
  • How do we know that reintroduced populations are truly "saved" long-term?
  • What would need to change for captive breeding to work for species it currently can't save?
  • At what point does continuing to breed a critically low-diversity species in captivity become pointless?
Model response:

Claim: Captive breeding is effective only when it is combined with in-situ habitat protection and when the species biology allows controlled breeding. As a stand-alone strategy, it often fails.

Support: (1) The Californian condor and Przewalski's horse show captive breeding can work — both were successfully reintroduced into protected areas where the original threat had been removed. (2) However, the giant panda demonstrates a critical limit — despite decades of captive breeding attempts, reproductive success remains low even in ideal conditions, suggesting that biology itself can block ex-situ success. (3) The gene pool problem — zoos must now swap sperm between institutions to maintain genetic variation — shows that without active management, captive populations will inbreed and become genetically vulnerable over time, like the cheetahs studied earlier in 4C.

Questions: (1) How much funding does captive breeding absorb that could otherwise protect wild habitats — is there an opportunity cost? (2) If a species is reintroduced but the population remains very small and genetically narrow, have we actually "saved" it, or just delayed extinction? (3) The Frozen Ark suggests we might be able to revive extinct species in future — does that change how we should think about captive breeding today?

Part 6 — Sustainability and Education

Prevention is better than cure

Allow 8 minutes
Read like an expert

Hold this question: "Why might sustainability and education matter more in the long run than seed banks and captive breeding?"

Sustainability

In an ideal world, we wouldn't need seed banks, zoos, or other methods of conserving biodiversity. People are increasingly trying to find ways to prevent the loss of biodiversity in the first place. Habitats and ecosystems can be conserved with less conflict by encouraging sustainable methods of land use.

For example, illegal logging operations in rainforests use "slash and burn" techniques — cutting down all the trees and burning the ground afterwards to harvest wood and clear soil for farming. The soil is soon exhausted and biodiversity lost. However, if we harvest trees selectively and replant for the future, biodiversity can be maintained while people continue to use the forest for income. This is sustainable forestry.

Sustainable agriculture includes farming methods that minimise environmental damage and avoid monoculture — using organic fertilisers where possible, minimising artificial fertilisers and chemical pesticides, using biological pest control, and planting in rotation to avoid soil exhaustion. Large-scale farming is essential for food supply, but sustainable methods like biological pest control can actually increase yields and improve profits while being cheaper long-term than expensive manufactured chemicals.

Getting priorities right

People and politicians often have limited sympathy for ecology, so it's important to target spending accurately. Around the world, there is a growing understanding of the need for sustainable agriculture and sustainable tourism to conserve biodiversity while still providing food and income.

Research continues into how food and other resources can be produced in a way that minimises biodiversity loss — and in some cases increases it again. Tourism can be developed sustainably — providing jobs and money for local people while conserving the environment.

Case Study — Costa Rica

Costa Rica — reversing habitat loss

Costa Rica is a good example of a country reversing its habitat loss. More than 25% of the country is now protected in some way. Most of its electricity is from renewable resources. Costa Rica earns a considerable proportion of its national income from sustainable ecotourism — from the president to children in schools, people in Costa Rica have become passionate about conserving the country's rich biodiversity. The ecotourism industry now provides many jobs and contributes around 6% of national GDP. This small country covers only 0.03% of the Earth's surface but contains more than 4% of the world's biodiversity — proving that it's possible to use natural resources to make money AND conserve biodiversity at the same time.

Case Study — Ethiopia

Restoring forests in Ethiopia

Professor Legesse Negash is a pioneer in the propagation of Ethiopia's native trees. Ethiopia has tremendous natural biodiversity threatened by both climate change and deforestation. By propagating indigenous trees and storing germlines in seed and tissue banks, Negash aims to protect soil against erosion and save the country's biodiversity. His Center for Indigenous Trees Propagation and Biodiversity Development produces thousands of young trees and trains new young biologists. Local farmers are taught why and how to plant native trees — so new trees can be planted when older ones are harvested. This combines ex-situ conservation (seed banks), in-situ restoration (forests), and education into one integrated approach.

The importance of education

People have to work hard to reduce pollution and support conservation. To help them understand why conservation matters, they need to learn about:

  • The impact of human activities on the natural world
  • Ways in which people can act to protect animals, plants, and habitats

When people learn about the damage they are doing to the environment, they often want to change. When they discover the range of biodiversity in the world and how it can be conserved, they usually want to help. Zoos, national parks, and seed banks are all important in education — allowing people to see animals and plants from other parts of the world, explaining different environments, showing how threatened species can be protected.

Maintaining biodiversity is a major issue for the 21st century. In a global economy, we can all contribute to maintaining biodiversity by the choices we make now.

Expert question check
Back to the question: why might sustainability and education matter more than seed banks and captive breeding in the long run?


Seed banks and captive breeding are responses to damage already done — they treat the symptoms of biodiversity loss. Sustainability and education address the root causes. A seed bank can preserve the genetic material of a plant species, but if its habitat is destroyed it may never return to the wild. Costa Rica's approach shows that changing how people use land can reverse habitat loss without relying on emergency preservation. Education matters because conservation requires sustained political and personal support — and people only act when they understand. In the long run, preventing biodiversity loss is vastly cheaper and more effective than trying to recover species one at a time. Ex-situ conservation buys time; sustainability buys the future.

Checkpoint Q1 · 3 marks

Preservation means preserving something exactly as it is now. Explain how conservation differs from preservation.

Sentence stems to help structure
  • Preservation aims to keep something exactly as it is, whereas conservation...
  • Conservation recognises that living environments...
  • A practical example: in a forest, preservation would..., but conservation would...


Preservation aims to keep something exactly as it is now, whereas conservation is an active process that maintains and protects living environments while allowing natural change (1)
Conservation recognises that ecosystems are dynamic and constantly changing — species evolve, communities shift, climates change — so preserving something unchanged is neither possible nor biologically desirable (1)
Example — a forest conservation project would manage human use, support regeneration, and allow natural succession, rather than trying to freeze the forest in time (1)
Checkpoint Q2 · 3 marks

Explain the difference between ex-situ and in-situ conservation.

Sentence stems
  • Ex-situ conservation refers to..., whereas in-situ conservation refers to...
  • An example of ex-situ conservation is..., while an example of in-situ conservation is...
  • These approaches are complementary because...


Ex-situ conservation is the conservation of living organisms outside their natural habitat, whereas in-situ conservation is the conservation of species within their natural habitats and ecosystems (1)
Examples — ex-situ: the Millennium Seed Bank at Kew storing 80,000 plant species, or captive breeding of Californian condors in zoos. In-situ: the Mkomazi Rhino Sanctuary in Tanzania, or Costa Rica's protected rainforests (1)
Ex-situ and in-situ are complementary — ex-situ acts as a backup when habitats are at risk or species are critically endangered, while in-situ maintains the natural ecosystem and evolutionary processes. Both are needed — neither alone is sufficient (1)
Checkpoint Q3 · 4 marks

Explain why it is much easier to conserve plant biodiversity than animal biodiversity.

Sentence stems
  • Plant conservation is easier because seeds...
  • The genetic material of plants...
  • Animal conservation faces the challenge that...
  • A key specific example is...


Most plants produce huge numbers of seeds, so large samples can be collected without damaging the wild population, whereas removing animals from the wild can threaten already-small populations (1)
Seeds are small and contain all the genetic material of the plant — they can be stored cheaply in large numbers at low temperatures, effectively in suspended animation for up to 200 years. Animals require living conditions, space, food, veterinary care, and appropriate social structures (1)
80% of known plant species can be stored in seed banks — 80,000 species are already at Kew. In contrast, zoos have limited space and resources; not all endangered animals can be bred in captivity (the giant panda is a notorious example) (1)
Animal captive breeding faces challenges that plant conservation does not: small gene pools causing inbreeding, animals struggling to adjust to wild life after captivity, the difficulty of providing the right breeding conditions, and the expense and time demand of reintroduction programmes (1)

Seeds are dried and stored at low temperatures (−20°C to −40°C), reducing metabolic activity inside the seed to near-zero (1)
Low temperature and low humidity prevent seed deterioration — for every 5°C drop in temperature or 2% fall in humidity, lifespan doubles (1)
The seed's embryo remains viable but dormant — it can be germinated and grow into a living plant when returned to suitable conditions, even after 200+ years (1)
Q4 · 4 marks

Explain two problems that can occur in captive breeding programmes, and how zoos try to overcome them.



Award 1 mark per valid problem + 1 mark per valid mitigation (any two problems):

Problem: small gene pool when the captive population is small — causes inbreeding, reduced genetic diversity, and greater vulnerability to disease (1)
Mitigation: zoos keep detailed records of genetic data of breeding individuals, and swap sperm between zoos for artificial insemination, maximising genetic variation in offspring (1)
Problem: animals bred in captivity may have problems adjusting to the wild on reintroduction — they may not know how to find food or avoid predators (1)
Mitigation: gradual reintroduction via protected areas and "soft release" programmes / training captive animals in wild-like conditions before release (1)
Q5 · 4 marks

Using Costa Rica as an example, explain how a country can use its natural resources to generate income while conserving biodiversity.



Costa Rica has developed sustainable ecotourism — people travel to see biodiversity, providing income without destroying the environment (1)
Over 25% of the country is protected and most electricity comes from renewable sources — so economic activity does not drive habitat destruction (1)
Ecotourism provides jobs for local people and contributes around 6% of national GDP — this gives communities an economic incentive to conserve biodiversity rather than exploit it destructively (1)
The country covers 0.03% of Earth's surface but hosts 4% of world biodiversity — showing that sustainability can preserve disproportionately rich biodiversity when national commitment is strong (1)
Q6 · 5 marks · AO3

Evaluate the statement: "Ex-situ conservation such as seed banks and zoos is now more important than in-situ conservation for protecting biodiversity."



Award marks for balanced evaluation with specific evidence:
Ex-situ has important roles — 80,000 plant species at Kew, captive breeding successes like Californian condors and Przewalski's horses, and the Frozen Ark enabling potential future genetic rescue (1)
Ex-situ acts as a safety net when habitats are destroyed — genetic material can be preserved even if the wild species is lost (1)
However, ex-situ cannot replace in-situ — it cannot conserve ecosystem relationships, natural selection pressures, or the evolutionary processes that maintain long-term biodiversity (1)
In-situ is ultimately necessary because successful reintroduction requires an intact habitat — and captive breeding often fails when the original threat is not addressed (1)
Reasoned judgement — the two are complementary, not competing. Ex-situ is a critical backup, but in-situ conservation is fundamental to long-term biodiversity preservation. Costa Rica demonstrates this with its in-situ-led approach achieving disproportionate biodiversity protection (1)

Command Words for this Topic

EXPLAIN

For conservation questions, you must give biological reasons. "Seed banks work because seeds stay alive" isn't enough — give the mechanism: low temperature + low humidity → reduced metabolic activity → seeds viable for 200 years.

COMPARE

Ex-situ vs in-situ is a classic compare question. Use paired points with linking words ("Ex-situ happens outside the natural habitat, whereas in-situ happens within it..."). Never two separate paragraphs.

DISCUSS

Present multiple angles — advantages AND disadvantages. For conservation questions like captive breeding, you must cover both sides. A one-sided answer caps at half marks.

EVALUATE

Like discuss, but with a stronger requirement for a judgement at the end. "Overall, captive breeding is most effective when..." — the conclusion must follow from the evidence, not just restate the question.

Common Mistakes

✗

Confusing conservation with preservation

Preservation = keep exactly as it is. Conservation = active, dynamic, allows change. Mixing these up is the single most common error in this topic.

✗

Giving one-sided answers to "discuss" or "evaluate" questions

These command words specifically require both advantages AND disadvantages. Captive breeding has real strengths (Californian condors) AND real weaknesses (gene pool problems, giant panda). Both must appear.

✗

Vague named examples or no examples at all

"Seed banks store seeds" scores little. "The Millennium Seed Bank at Kew holds 80,000 plant species, including all of the UK's 1,442 native flowering plants" scores marks. Specifics pay.

✗

Forgetting that ex-situ and in-situ are COMPLEMENTARY

Examiners reward students who understand conservation isn't either/or. The textbook explicitly states: "Ex-situ conservation is always seen as a complementary approach to in-situ conservation". Use this phrase.

Worked Example Answer

EXAM QUESTION (6 marks)

Discuss why seed banks have become a vital tool in conserving plant biodiversity, including any limitations of this approach.

Model Answer (6/6)

Seed banks are an effective form of ex-situ conservation because they preserve the genetic material of plants outside their natural habitat. The Millennium Seed Bank at Kew holds seeds of around 80,000 plant species, including the UK's entire native flora — over 300 of which are already threatened with extinction. Seeds are a practical choice for conservation because plants produce huge numbers of seeds that can be collected without damaging wild populations, and seeds are small enough that large numbers can be stored cheaply in small spaces. Stored at −20°C to −40°C with low humidity, metabolic activity is reduced almost to zero — for every 5°C drop in temperature or 2% fall in humidity, lifespan doubles, allowing many seeds to remain viable for 200 years or more.

However, seed banks have significant limitations. About 20% of known plant species have seeds that do not store well — including crucial crops such as mango, avocado, rubber, cacao, and coconut. These require alternative approaches like field gene banks (the International Potato Centre in Peru maintains 4,100 clones of potato requiring annual replanting), which take much more space, labour, and funding than seed storage. Seed banks also cannot preserve ecological relationships — a stored seed retains the plant's genetics, but it cannot preserve the pollinators, soil microbes, or fungi the plant depends on in its natural ecosystem. This is why seed banks are always seen as complementary to in-situ conservation, never a replacement.

Why this scores full marks:

✓ Specific named examples (Kew, Peru potato centre, specific crop species) ✓ Specific mechanisms (temperature, humidity, 200-year lifespan) ✓ Balanced — covers benefits AND limitations ✓ Uses the key phrase "complementary to in-situ" ✓ Acknowledges ecological relationships beyond the genetics
