Question 1 · From 4C.1
Distinguish between species richness and relative species abundance. Which one alone gives an incomplete picture of biodiversity?


Species richness = the number of different species in an area. Relative species abundance = how evenly the different species are distributed. Species richness alone gives an incomplete picture — two areas can have identical richness but very different biodiversity if one is dominated by a single species. Both measures are needed together.
Question 2 · From 4C.1
Define the term "endemic species" and give one example of a location with high endemism. Explain why that location has so many endemic species.


An endemic species evolves in geographical isolation and is found in only one place. Example: Madagascar — almost all its species are endemic because it is an island, so ocean barriers prevent gene flow with mainland Africa. Isolated populations evolve independently over time, eventually becoming distinct species found nowhere else.
Question 3 · From 2C
What is an allele? What is the difference between a homozygote and a heterozygote?


An allele is a different version of the same gene (different base sequence at the same locus). A homozygote has two identical alleles for a gene (e.g. AA or aa) — when DNA is analysed, only one band appears per allele. A heterozygote has two different alleles (e.g. Aa) — two bands appear on DNA analysis, one for each allele.
Question 4 · From 3B/2C
What is allele frequency? How can a mutation increase the allele frequency of a particular allele in a population?


Allele frequency = the relative frequency with which a particular allele appears within a population. A mutation creates a new allele. If that allele confers an advantage, natural selection favours organisms carrying it — they survive and reproduce more successfully, passing the allele to offspring. Over generations, the allele becomes more common in the gene pool, increasing its frequency.
Question 5 · Thinking ahead
If you wanted to compare the biodiversity of two ponds, what information would you need to collect? How might you turn that into a single number?


You would need: the total number of organisms (all species combined) and the number of organisms of each individual species. To generate a single number, a mathematical formula can combine both species richness and abundance into a diversity index — higher values indicate greater biodiversity. This is exactly what you will calculate in Station 1.

The Diversity Index: Understanding the Formula

Decode each component, then work through the calculation step by step

Why do we need a formula?

Species richness and relative abundance together capture biodiversity — but comparing two habitats is difficult when both measures vary. The diversity index (D) combines both into a single comparable number. The higher the value, the greater the biodiversity. Big is always better.

The Formula

D =
N(N − 1)
Σn(n − 1)
where D = diversity index · N = total number of all organisms · n = number of organisms of each species · Σ = sum of all values

Decode the components

Before calculating, make sure you understand each part. Match each term to its meaning by clicking the reveal:

N

Total number of organisms of all species combined — you count every individual organism in your sample, regardless of species. This goes in the numerator.

n

Number of organisms of each individual species — you calculate n(n−1) for each species separately, then add all those values together using Σ.

Σ

Sum (add together) — Σn(n−1) means: calculate n(n−1) for species 1, then species 2, then species 3... and add all the results together. This gives you the denominator.

D

The diversity index — the final answer. A higher D means greater biodiversity. D has no units. When comparing two habitats, the one with the higher D is more biodiverse.

On its own, a single D value is hard to interpret. Its power comes from comparison — if you calculated D = 1.20 for a nearby polluted pond, you could conclude the natural pond (D = 3.60) has nearly three times the biodiversity. Higher D always means more biodiverse. There is no fixed scale — D values are only meaningful relative to each other.

Learning Tip

Big is better — a higher D value always indicates greater biodiversity. If a habitat becomes polluted or degraded, D will decrease as fewer species survive and/or one species comes to dominate. Tracking D over time is a useful conservation monitoring tool.

How Biodiversity Varies

Why biodiversity isn't constant — and what drives the differences

Biodiversity is not fixed

The D value for a habitat will change depending on when you measure it, the condition of the environment, and the stability of the ecosystem. Understanding why biodiversity varies helps us decide how to protect it.

Three key ideas

1. Biodiversity varies with time

Animal species in an area vary with time of day — bats at dusk, birds at dawn. Seasonality changes everything: wetland feeding sites for migratory birds are packed in some months and empty in others. Plant species on a meadow floor are different in spring vs autumn. This means when you measure matters — a single survey may not capture true biodiversity.

A single survey of a coastal wetland in July might count 50 wading bird species. The same survey in January might count 5. If conservation funding is allocated on the basis of a winter survey, the site's importance as a summer feeding ground may be missed entirely. Biodiversity assessment should ideally use repeated surveys across seasons.

2. Extreme environments = lower biodiversity

In extreme environments (desert, deep ocean vents, Arctic tundra), biodiversity is typically low because few species can tolerate the conditions. The species that do survive are highly specialised. Any change — a severe frost, a flood, a new pathogen — has a disproportionate impact because there are so few species to absorb the shock.

In extreme environments, you would expect a low D value. Few species means low species richness (numerator N is small relative to what it could be). And often one or two species dominate the few survivors — so Σn(n−1) is large relative to N(N−1), pushing D down further. This mathematical relationship mirrors the ecological reality.

3. Stable ecosystems = higher biodiversity

In stable, less hostile environments — tropical rainforests, temperate woodland, coral reefs — biodiversity can be very high. A stable ecosystem allows complex food webs and ecological relationships to develop. A new species moving in or out will have almost no effect on overall function because there are many alternative species filling similar roles.

The current best-supported model suggests three factors: (1) very stable ecosystems with many complex relationships between species; (2) areas with high levels of productivity — when photosynthesis rates are very high, more energy is available to support more species at each trophic level; (3) areas where organisms can grow and reproduce rapidly, increasing mutation rates and the chance of adaptations that allow exploitation of new niches.

Genetic Biodiversity and the Heterozygosity Index

Measuring biodiversity within a species using allele frequency

A different scale of measurement

The diversity index D measures biodiversity at the species level within a habitat. But biodiversity also exists within a species — in the genetic variation between individuals. The heterozygosity index measures this. Without genetic variation, a population is vulnerable to disease, climate change, and environmental shifts.

The Formula

Heterozygosity index =
number of heterozygotes
number of individuals in the population
A higher index = greater genetic diversity = healthier, more resilient population
?

How do we identify heterozygotes? Modern DNA analysis — when the DNA of an organism is analysed for a particular gene, homozygotes show one band (both alleles identical), while heterozygotes show two bands (two different alleles). Scientists use this to count heterozygotes across a sample population.

Worked Example: European Toad

DNA analysis of 23 European toads for a single gene shows: 5 individuals are heterozygous, 18 are homozygous.

Heterozygosity index = number of heterozygotes / total individuals
= 5 / 23
= 0.217

Interpreting 0.217

For comparison, healthy populations of Siberian apricot trees in China have heterozygosity indices of 0.639 and 0.774. The toad's 0.217 is considerably lower, suggesting limited genetic diversity — the population may be vulnerable. However, interpretation requires context: what is the species' typical range? Has the population recently declined? Single-locus measurements may not represent genome-wide diversity.

Practice Calculations

Practice 1 — Guided
A conservation team analyses DNA from 50 individuals of a rare bat species. They find that 32 individuals are heterozygous for a key immune-response gene. Calculate the heterozygosity index and interpret the result.


Heterozygosity index = 32 / 50 = 0.64

This is a relatively high heterozygosity index, suggesting good genetic diversity in this population for the immune-response gene. The population has a reasonable level of variation, which should provide some resilience to disease — a healthy sign for a rare species. However, a single gene does not represent total genome diversity; further analysis across multiple loci would be needed for a full picture.
Practice 2 — Independent
A researcher studies two isolated populations of mountain gorillas. In Population A (n=40), 6 individuals are heterozygous. In Population B (n=35), 4 individuals are heterozygous.

Calculate the heterozygosity index for each population and state which is more genetically diverse. Then explain what the lower index suggests about conservation risk.


Population A: 6/40 = 0.15
Population B: 4/35 = 0.114

Population A (0.15) has slightly greater genetic diversity than Population B (0.114), though both values are very low.

The low heterozygosity in both populations suggests limited genetic variation. This means: (1) both populations are potentially vulnerable to a single pathogen — if one allele confers resistance and few individuals have it, disease could devastate the population; (2) the populations may have experienced a bottleneck event — a dramatic reduction in numbers that eliminated most genetic variation; (3) conservation priority should be considered for both, but Population B's lower index makes it particularly at risk.

Case Study: Cheetahs and Dangerously Low Genetic Diversity

Cheetahs are the fastest land animals on Earth. They are also among the most genetically uniform large mammals alive. The heterozygosity index for different cheetah populations ranges from just 0.0004 to 0.014 — extraordinarily low values. Scientists believe this is because the species was reduced to a tiny surviving group approximately 10,000–20,000 years ago — a severe population bottleneck. Modern cheetahs are so genetically similar that unrelated individuals can accept skin grafts from each other without rejection. The conservation implications are serious: a single epidemic could potentially destroy the entire species. Habitat destruction is accelerating the decline.

A population bottleneck dramatically reduces the number of individuals — and therefore the number of different alleles in the gene pool. Many alleles are simply lost by chance when most individuals die. The surviving individuals — and all their descendants — carry only the alleles that happened to be present in that tiny founding population. Even if the population later recovers in number, it cannot recover the alleles it lost. This is why cheetahs look superficially healthy in numbers but remain genetically fragile.

Command Words for this Topic

CALCULATE

Work through the formula step by step and show all working. Always present your table of n(n−1) values before giving D. Include units if asked (D has none). Round to 2 decimal places unless told otherwise. Do not just write the final answer — method marks are available for each step.

INTERPRET

Go beyond the number — explain what it means. For D: is it high or low relative to a comparison? What does this suggest about the habitat condition? For heterozygosity index: is the population genetically diverse or uniform? What does that mean for resilience?

EXPLAIN

For allele frequency questions: state the mechanism clearly — mutation creates the allele, natural selection determines whether it increases or decreases in frequency, and the chain of reasoning must link to survival and reproduction. Each link needs to be stated.

EVALUATE

For evaluation questions about D or the heterozygosity index, you need both sides — what the measure does capture AND what it misses. "D is useful because... however, it is limited because..." is the structure. Avoid evaluating only one side.

Common Calculation Mistakes

✗

Confusing N and n in the formula

N (capital) = total of ALL organisms. n (lower case) = total for ONE species. The numerator uses N. The denominator uses n (one per species, then summed). Getting these mixed up is the most common error — the table structure helps you keep them separate.

✗

Forgetting the Σ (sum) step

You must calculate n(n−1) for every species and then add all those values together. Students frequently calculate only one or two n(n−1) values and use those in the denominator. The denominator is the sum of ALL species' n(n−1) values.

✗

Not applying the (n−1) correctly for n=1

If a species has only 1 individual, n(n−1) = 1×0 = 0. That species contributes nothing to the denominator. Students sometimes write n(n−1) = 1 for this case — always subtract 1 from n before multiplying.

✗

Heterozygosity index inverted

The formula is heterozygotes ÷ total. Students sometimes invert this (total ÷ heterozygotes). The index must be between 0 and 1. If you get a number greater than 1, you have inverted the fraction — check immediately.

✗

Saying "higher D = more species"

A higher D means greater biodiversity, which combines species richness AND evenness. Two habitats with the same species richness can have very different D values. Never conflate D with species count alone.

Worked Example Answer

EXAM QUESTION (6 marks)

A river ecologist surveys invertebrates at two sites — Site 1 (upstream, unpolluted) and Site 2 (downstream, affected by agricultural runoff). Site 1 has D = 6.4. At Site 2, the following organisms were collected: bloodworms (n=34), tubifex worms (n=29), rat-tailed maggots (n=18). Calculate D for Site 2, compare the two sites, and suggest what the data indicate about the effect of pollution on biodiversity.

Model Answer (6/6)

Step 1 — Complete the table:
Bloodworms: n=34, n(n−1) = 34×33 = 1122
Tubifex worms: n=29, n(n−1) = 29×28 = 812
Rat-tailed maggots: n=18, n(n−1) = 18×17 = 306
N = 81 · Σn(n−1) = 1122 + 812 + 306 = 2240

Step 2 — Apply the formula:
D = N(N−1) / Σn(n−1) = 81 × 80 / 2240 = 6480 / 2240 = D = 2.89

Step 3 — Compare and interpret:
Site 2 (D = 2.89) has substantially lower biodiversity than Site 1 (D = 6.4). Only three species were recorded at Site 2 compared with presumably more at Site 1. Agricultural runoff typically introduces excess nitrates and phosphates, causing algal blooms that deplete oxygen levels. Most invertebrate species cannot survive in low-oxygen conditions, so only pollution-tolerant species (bloodworms, tubifex worms, rat-tailed maggots — all of which can survive very low dissolved oxygen) persist. The data suggest that pollution has significantly reduced both species richness and — since three species are still relatively abundant — also reduced the evenness of distribution compared to the unpolluted site.
