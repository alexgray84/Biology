Core Content

Molecular Evidence & Biochemical Relationships

For centuries, classification was based on detailed observations of morphology — counting hairs on a fly's foreleg or petals on a flower. Now, biochemical relationships are increasingly being used to support or clarify relationships based on morphology. Scientists analyse the structures of many different chemicals as well as the DNA to identify inter-relationships between groups of organisms. This analysis is called molecular phylogeny.

The evidence from biochemical analysis may support or conflict with relationships based on morphology. All green plants share similar complex pathways for making glucose — so they likely developed from a common ancestor. But American and African porcupines occupy similar niches and look almost identical, yet biochemical analysis shows they are only very distantly related.

Key Technique

Gel Electrophoresis

A variation of chromatography that separates DNA and RNA fragments, proteins, or amino acids according to their size and electrical charge. Patterns in DNA, RNA, or protein fragments can be compared between species to identify relationships and determine which species they belong to.

Biochemical Evidence in Practice

  • 🌿

    Photosynthesis pathways: All green plants share similar complex chlorophyll-based glucose synthesis pathways — strong evidence of common ancestry. In contrast, agaves and aloes share environmental adaptations but are biochemically very different.

  • ⭐

    Phosphagens (ATP synthesis): These molecules provide the phosphate group for ATP synthesis in muscles. Phosphocreatine occurs almost exclusively in vertebrates and echinoderms (starfish, sea urchins); phosphoarginine in other groups. This unexpected shared biochemistry suggests vertebrates and echinoderms share a closer common ancestor than anatomy alone would suggest.

  • 🩸

    Blood pigments: Any group contains only one type of blood pigment. All vertebrates and many polychaete worms have haemoglobin; all molluscs and crustaceans have haemocyanin; all annelid worms and arthropods have chlorocruorin. This allows scientists to build a detailed picture of relationships between animal groups.

  • 🔗

    Amino acid sequences in proteins: The sequence of amino acids in specific proteins (like fibrinogen, involved in blood clotting in mammals) reveals how closely different groups are related. Single amino acid changes are used to plot relationships within a phylum.

  • 🦛

    Surprising results: A combination of DNA analysis, protein analysis and anatomical observations can reveal unexpected relationships. For example, the closest living relative to the hippopotamus appears to be the whale — a result impossible to reach from morphology alone.

Core Content

Phylogenetic Trees

A phylogenetic tree is a model used to show the relationships between different groups of organisms. They may show relationships between all living organisms, or the relationships within a particular group. Phylogenetic trees were originally built using morphology — now scientists use DNA and amino acid evidence as a major tool.

A phylogenetic tree shows a common ancestor which may no longer exist, with the tree branching as different species appear through natural selection. The tree branches are not time-lines — they show the sequence of divergence from common ancestors, not how long ago.

Core Content · The Scientific Debate

Two Domains or Three?

For many years biologists divided living organisms into two large groups: the eukaryotes (cells with a complex cell structure — membrane-enclosed nucleus and organelles) and the prokaryotes (which included all bacteria). The theory was that eukaryotes developed from prokaryotes billions of years ago.

Endosymbiont Theory

How did eukaryotic organelles arise?

Ancient eukaryotic cells engulfed aerobic prokaryotes — which, instead of being digested, became mitochondria (permanent aerobic respiration factories). Similarly, photosynthetic prokaryotes were engulfed and became chloroplasts. Organisms living inside other cells like this are called endosymbionts. Evidence: mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own circular DNA, replicate independently, and have 70S ribosomes (like bacteria, not eukaryotes).

Scientists used molecular phylogeny to investigate further — comparing internal structures of prokaryotes and eukaryotes, and analysing the biochemistry of proteins in their ribosomes and enzymes. This revealed something unexpected: a new theory that there are, in fact, three domains.

The Archaea and Bacteria are as different from each other as they are from the Eukaryota. Genetic studies show all three groups probably shared a single common ancestor about three billion years ago. Some evidence suggests Archaea are more closely related to Eukaryota than to Bacteria — their last common ancestor with eukaryotes was probably about two billion years ago.

Two-domain view

Prokaryote + Eukaryote

Based on cell structure alone — nucleus present or absent
Archaea and Bacteria are grouped together as "prokaryotes"
Simple and visible — can be assessed under a microscope
Ignores fundamental biochemical differences between Archaea and Bacteria
Three-domain view (modern consensus)

Archaea + Bacteria + Eukaryota

Based on molecular and biochemical evidence
Archaea are as different from Bacteria as Eukaryota are
Supported by ribosome structure, membrane lipids, DNA replication
Most biologists now accept this, but debate continues about origins and horizontal gene transfer
🔬

Horizontal gene transfer: Evidence is growing that genes can be transferred between organisms of different groups — not just passed from parent to offspring. This complicates the simple "tree of life" model and suggests a complex interwoven network of ancestry. This is an area of ongoing scientific debate.

⚡ Learning Tip from the Textbook

Most scientists now believe there are three domains rather than two. Learn two or three specific pieces of evidence that justify the three-domain argument. The best ones to know: ribosome sensitivity differences (Bacteria sensitive to chloramphenicol, Archaea are not) and membrane lipid structure (Archaea have unique branched lipids). These are specific, testable, and memorable.

Core Content · Classification History

How Many Kingdoms?

The number of kingdoms we use has changed dramatically as scientific knowledge has grown. When Linnaeus first worked out his system, he proposed just two kingdoms: plants and animals — and fungi counted as plants because they didn't move. As technology improved, the picture became more complex.

The five-kingdom system is still used but increasingly biologists use a six-kingdom classification. This results from the three-domain evidence: Archaea and Eubacteria are now recognised as two very different groups with very different biochemistry. The prokaryotes are no longer regarded as a single group — splitting the old "Monera" kingdom into two.

The Six Kingdoms

Archaeabacteria
Prokaryotic · Domain: Archaea
Ancient bacteria with a wide variety of lifestyles. Include the extremophiles — can survive extreme heat, cold, pH, salinity and pressure. Reproduce asexually.
Eubacteria
Prokaryotic · Domain: Bacteria
The true bacteria and cyanobacteria (formerly called blue-green algae). Reproduce asexually. Include pathogens, decomposers, nitrogen fixers, and photosynthesisers.
Protista
Eukaryotic · Domain: Eukaryota
All single-celled eukaryotes, green and brown algae, slime moulds. A catch-all for organisms that don't fit other kingdoms. Mainly reproduce asexually.
Fungi
Eukaryotic · Domain: Eukaryota
Includes unicellular yeasts and multicellular toadstools and moulds. All heterotrophs. Chitin in cell walls. Reproduce both asexually and sexually.
Plantae
Eukaryotic · Domain: Eukaryota
Almost all multicellular autotrophs — make their own food by photosynthesis using chlorophyll. Mosses, liverworts, ferns, gymnosperms, angiosperms.
Animalia
Eukaryotic · Domain: Eukaryota
Multicellular heterotrophs. No cell walls. Invertebrates and vertebrates. Sexual reproduction is common but some reproduce asexually.
⚡ Exam Hint

The move from five to six kingdoms is directly caused by accepting the three-domain evidence: once Archaea are recognised as a separate domain from Bacteria, the old Monera kingdom must be split in two. Make sure you can explain this causal link — not just list the kingdoms.

T3 · Evaluate Longer response — structure your argument
Q3.1
Evaluate the use of molecular analysis compared to morphological differences in the classification of organisms. In your answer, consider the advantages and limitations of each approach and refer to specific examples. (6 marks)
Morphological — advantage: quick, cheap, accessible, no specialist equipment; works for fossils (1); limitation: convergent evolution can cause unrelated organisms to look similar, e.g. American and African porcupines, agaves and aloes (1); sexual dimorphism can cause the same species to be classified differently (1). Molecular — advantage: reveals actual genetic relationships not obscured by environmental pressures or convergent evolution, e.g. hippopotamus and whale as closest relatives (1); more objective and can be quantified (1); limitation: expensive historically (though getting cheaper); doesn't work on most fossils; scientists must still make a judgement about how much genetic difference = different species (1). Overall: molecular is increasingly preferred but morphology remains useful and both approaches complement each other (credit any well-supported conclusion).

Subject Vocabulary

Key Terms

Click any term to reveal its definition.

gel electrophoresis
A method of separating fragments of proteins or nucleic acids based on their electrical charge and size.
phylogenetic tree
A model used to show the relationships between different groups of organisms. Shows common ancestors and the divergence of species over time.
molecular phylogeny
The analysis of the genetic material of organisms (DNA, RNA, proteins) to establish their evolutionary relationships.
endosymbionts
Organisms that live inside the cells or body of another organism. The theory proposes that mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living prokaryotes that became permanent endosymbionts of early eukaryotic cells.
Monera
A kingdom in the five-kingdom classification system that contains the Archaea and Eubacteria — all prokaryotes grouped together. No longer used in the six-kingdom system.
extremophiles
Bacteria (specifically Archaeabacteria) that can survive extreme conditions of heat, cold, pH, salinity and pressure.
convergent evolution
When unrelated organisms independently evolve similar features due to similar environmental pressures. Can mislead morphological classification — e.g. American and African porcupines, agaves and aloes.
bioinformatics
The development of software and computing tools needed to organise and analyse the enormous quantities of raw biological data generated by DNA and protein analysis.
phosphagen
A molecule that provides the phosphate group for the synthesis of ATP in muscles. Phosphocreatine occurs in vertebrates and echinoderms; phosphoarginine in other groups — used as biochemical evidence of evolutionary relationships.
horizontal gene transfer
The transfer of genes between organisms of different groups — not just from parent to offspring. Complicates the simple tree of life model and suggests ancestry is more of an interwoven network than a simple branching tree.
three-domain system
The modern classification system that divides all life into three domains — Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukaryota — based on molecular and biochemical evidence. Archaea and Bacteria are as different from each other as they both are from Eukaryota.
common ancestor
An ancestor from which two or more different species evolved. Shown as branching points on a phylogenetic tree. May no longer exist as a living organism.
