Learn 1 — Gene Pool and Allele Frequency

Read carefully. Answer the Pause and Check questions before moving on.

Allow 10 minutes

Population and Gene Pool

In biology, a population is a group of individuals of the same species occupying a particular habitat and a particular niche. The habitat is the place where an organism lives.

In population genetics, we take the gene as the unit of evolution. The sum total of all the alleles in a population at a given time is called the gene pool. At any point in time, a population will have a particular gene pool — with different alleles occurring with varying frequencies. Think of it like a massive bag of alleles from every individual in the population. Evolution is a permanent change in those allele frequencies over time.

Gene pool
The sum total of all the genes in a population at a given time — it includes every copy of every allele carried by every individual in the population.

Allele Frequency

The frequency of alleles in a population is not fixed. As the environment changes, so does the frequency of different alleles through natural selection and adaptation.

A classic example: warfarin is a rat poison that prevents blood clotting. When first introduced, some rats already carried a mutation giving them resistance. The poison acted as a powerful selection pressure — it killed susceptible rats and left resistant ones to reproduce. Over generations, the resistance allele increased in frequency across the entire population until most rats became resistant.

Allele frequency
The relative frequency with which a particular allele appears within a population. It is expressed as a decimal fraction between 0 and 1. An allele with a frequency of 0.5 is carried by 50% of individuals in the population.
Selection pressure
The effect of one or more environmental factors that determine whether an organism will be more or less successful at surviving and reproducing. Selection pressure drives changes in allele frequency and ultimately speciation.

The Foundation: p + q = 1

Imagine a single gene with just two possible alleles: A (dominant) and a (recessive). In a population, every copy of this gene must be either A or a — there are no other options.

We use p to represent the frequency of the dominant allele (A) and q to represent the frequency of the recessive allele (a). Because every allele must be one or the other:

p + q = 1

where p = frequency of dominant allele (A) · q = frequency of recessive allele (a)

So if you know p, you can always find q: q = 1 − p. And if you know q, you can find p: p = 1 − q.

Example: if 30% of alleles in a population are the recessive allele (q = 0.30), then the dominant allele must make up the remaining 70% (p = 0.70). Check: 0.30 + 0.70 = 1. ✓

Important limitation

The equation p + q = 1 is not very useful on its own — it is almost impossible to distinguish heterozygotes (Aa) from dominant homozygotes (AA) just by looking at the phenotype. However, we can easily observe and count recessive phenotypes (aa). This is what makes the Hardy-Weinberg equation so powerful, as you will see in Learn 2.

p + q = 1 → p = 1 − q = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6
The dominant allele has a frequency of 0.6 (i.e. 60% of alleles in the population are the dominant form).

Learn 2 — The Hardy-Weinberg Equation

Read all content and both worked examples before moving to Practice.

Allow 15 minutes

Why we need the Hardy-Weinberg equation

In 1908, G.N. Hardy (British mathematician) and W. Weinberg (German physician) independently developed an equation to describe the mathematical relationship between the frequencies of alleles and genotypes within a stable theoretical population. It gives us a powerful tool: if we can observe the frequency of the recessive phenotype (aa), we can calculate all the allele and genotype frequencies in the population.

The key insight: recessive phenotypes are easily observable because they can only arise from the genotype aa (two recessive alleles). From that single observable frequency, we can unlock everything else.

The Full Equation

p² + 2pq + q² = 1

This must always equal 1 — because all genotype frequencies in a population must add up to 100%

p²
Homozygous dominant
Frequency of AA genotype in the population
2pq
Heterozygous
Frequency of Aa genotype in the population (×2 because Aa and aA are the same)
q²
Homozygous recessive
Frequency of aa genotype — this is the one we can directly observe from the recessive phenotype

The key strategy — always start with q²

Since recessive phenotypes are easily observable, we always begin by measuring the frequency of the recessive phenotype in the population. This gives us q². From q², we find q (square root). From q, we find p (p = 1 − q). Then we can calculate p², 2pq, and q² for all genotype frequencies. The order is: q² → q → p → p², 2pq. Always follow this order.

Worked Example 1 — Albinism in North Americans
Follow every step

Tests on a sample of North Americans showed that the frequency of albinos in the population was 1 in 20,000. Albinism is a recessive phenotype. Calculate the frequencies of all three genotypes.

1
Identify q² — the frequency of the recessive phenotype (albinism = aa)
Frequency of albinos = 1/20,000 = 0.00005
Therefore q² = 0.00005
2
Find q — take the square root of q²
q = √0.00005 = 0.007
3
Find p — using p + q = 1
p = 1 − q = 1 − 0.007 = 0.993
4
Calculate all three genotype frequencies
Frequency of AA (homozygous dominant) = p² = 0.993² = 0.986
Frequency of Aa (heterozygous) = 2pq = 2 × 0.993 × 0.007 = 0.014
Frequency of aa (homozygous recessive) = q² = 0.00005
5
Check your answer — p² + 2pq + q² must equal 1
0.986 + 0.014 + 0.00005 ≈ 1.000 ✓

Interpretation: 98.6% of the North American population are homozygous for the dominant allele, 1.4% are heterozygous carriers, and only 0.005% are albino. Despite albinism being very rare as a phenotype, the recessive allele is carried by 1.4% of the population — far more than the phenotype suggests.

Worked Example 2 — Cystic Fibrosis in a UK Population
Follow every step

In the UK, approximately 1 in 2,500 babies are born with cystic fibrosis (CF), which is caused by a recessive allele. Calculate the expected frequency of carriers (heterozygotes) in the UK population.

1
Identify q²
Frequency of CF = 1/2500 = 0.0004
Therefore q² = 0.0004
2
Find q
q = √0.0004 = 0.02
3
Find p
p = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98
4
Calculate heterozygote (carrier) frequency
Frequency of carriers (Aa) = 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.0392
This means approximately 1 in 25 people in the UK is a carrier of the CF allele.
5
Check
p² = 0.98² = 0.9604 · 2pq = 0.0392 · q² = 0.0004
0.9604 + 0.0392 + 0.0004 = 1.000 ✓

Key insight: CF appears in only 1 in 2,500 births, yet approximately 1 in 25 people carry the allele. The Hardy-Weinberg equation reveals a hidden reservoir of genetic disease in the population that phenotype data alone would massively underestimate.

Learn 3 — Conditions for Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

Five conditions. Learn them all. Understand what happens when each is broken.

Allow 8 minutes

What is Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium?

The Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium theory states that in a population that is not evolving, allele frequencies will remain stable from one generation to the next — as long as there are no other evolutionary influences. This describes a theoretical steady state where the gene pool is not changing.

If the population is evolving, allele frequencies will change from generation to generation and the population is said to be not in equilibrium. This is the normal situation in real populations — the conditions for true equilibrium are almost never fully met in nature.

The Five Conditions

For Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium to hold, all five of the following must be true. Learn each condition AND what happens if it is violated.

ConditionWhat it meansWhat happens if it's broken?
1. No mutations No new alleles are created and no alleles are lost through mutation New mutations create new alleles or change existing ones, directly altering allele frequencies in the gene pool
2. Random mating Any individual is equally likely to mate with any other individual — mate choice is not based on genotype Non-random mating (mate selection based on phenotype) means some alleles are preferentially passed on — e.g. sexual selection in peacocks means showy-tail alleles are passed on more
3. Large population The population must be large enough that random sampling effects do not significantly alter allele frequencies In small populations, genetic drift can cause random changes in allele frequency — alleles can be lost or fixed purely by chance, not by selection
4. Isolation (no immigration or emigration) No organisms move into or out of the population, bringing or removing alleles Gene flow — migration of organisms or genetic material in or out — changes allele frequencies. Immigration can introduce new alleles; emigration can remove them
5. No selection pressure All genotypes are equally fit — no genotype has a survival or reproductive advantage over another If some genotypes are more successful, natural selection increases the frequency of advantageous alleles and decreases the frequency of disadvantageous ones — allele frequencies change

The critical point

In the real world, these conditions are almost never all met simultaneously. Mutations occur continuously; mating is rarely truly random; all populations have finite size; gene flow occurs constantly; and selection pressures are always present to some degree. This is why real populations are always changing — always evolving. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is a theoretical baseline, not a description of nature. Its value is as a null model: if allele frequencies are changing, something is driving that change.

⏸ Pause and Check
A farmer notices that the frequency of a recessive allele for disease susceptibility in his sheep flock has increased significantly over three years. Suggest two conditions of Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium that may have been violated. Explain each.


Selection pressure: if a disease recently killed a large proportion of sheep, the surviving individuals (who passed on their alleles) may have been those that happened to carry the susceptibility allele alongside other advantageous alleles — indirect selection could increase the frequency (1)
Non-random mating: farmers typically select which animals breed — this is artificial selection, not random mating. If rams carrying the susceptibility allele happen to be selected as studs, the allele frequency increases in the next generation (1)
Small population / genetic drift: farm flocks are often small — random chance could cause the frequency of any allele, including the susceptibility allele, to drift upwards without any selection advantage (1) [any two conditions with valid explanations]

Apply — Why Equilibrium Breaks Down

Read each mechanism, then answer the application question.

Allow 12 minutes

Mutations

Although each gene mutates very rarely (between 1 in 10⁴ and 1 in 10⁹ per generation), across a population of millions and a genome of billions of genes, mutations are occurring continuously. Most mutations are recessive and will never be expressed. Occasionally, a mutation arises that confers a benefit — and this is when natural selection begins to act.

In a single generation, mutations alone rarely have a large effect on allele frequencies. But over many generations, they are the ultimate source of all new genetic variation. Without mutation, there would be nothing for natural selection to act on.

⏸ Apply
The textbook mentions ruby red grapefruit — a naturally occurring mutation in growing grapefruit cells produced a new dominant phenotype. Explain why a single dominant mutation can affect allele frequencies in a population more rapidly than a single recessive mutation.


A dominant mutation is expressed in the phenotype immediately, even in heterozygotes — natural selection can therefore act on it in the very first generation it appears (1)
A recessive mutation is hidden in heterozygotes (carriers) — it can only be expressed and selected for when an individual is homozygous recessive (aa), which may take many generations to occur at significant frequency (1)
Therefore a beneficial dominant mutation increases in frequency much more rapidly under selection than an equally beneficial recessive mutation (1)

Non-Random Mating

For Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, any individual must be equally likely to mate with any other. But in almost all real populations, mating is non-random. If a feature of the phenotype affects the probability of mating — this is natural selection in action.

Examples: the male peacock with the most impressive tail is preferred by females; the male deer with the largest antlers wins more fights; the butterfly with the brightest wings attracts the most mates. In all these cases, the alleles coding for these attractive features are disproportionately passed on. This is sexual selection — a specific type of natural selection.

In human populations, non-random mating is universal. Cultural, social, and family factors all influence mate choice. In the Kuna people of Panama, albinism is considered desirable — the frequency of the albinism allele is approximately 1 in 200, compared to a global average of 1 in 17,000. This striking difference demonstrates the power of non-random mating on allele frequencies across populations.

⏸ Apply
Explain why sexual selection (non-random mating) can cause allele frequencies to change even if the selected alleles confer no survival advantage to the organism.


Sexual selection increases the reproductive success of individuals with certain phenotypic traits — even if those traits do not improve survival (1)
Individuals with the preferred phenotype mate more frequently and produce more offspring, passing on the alleles for that phenotype more often (1)
Over generations, the alleles coding for the preferred traits increase in frequency in the gene pool — not because they aid survival, but because they increase mating success / the peacock's tail may actually reduce survival by making the bird more conspicuous to predators (1)

Population Size and Isolation

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium requires a large population. In small populations, genetic drift — random fluctuations in allele frequencies — can have a large effect. An allele present in only a few individuals could be lost entirely if those individuals die before reproducing, even if the allele is neutral or beneficial.

Isolation is required to prevent gene flow — the migration of genetic material into or out of a population. When organisms move between populations, they bring their alleles with them, changing the allele frequencies in both the source and destination populations. Gene flow tends to make geographically separated populations more similar to each other over time, and reduces the likelihood of speciation. When gene flow between two populations stops — through isolation — their allele frequencies can diverge, eventually leading to new species.

Gene flow
The migration of either whole organisms or genetic material into or out of a population and into another population, tending to make different populations more alike, but changing the allele frequencies within each individual population all the time.

Selection Pressure

The most powerful disruptor of Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is natural selection. Any environmental factor that makes some genotypes more successful than others is a selection pressure — and selection pressures are always present to some degree.

When conditions change, alleles that were previously neutral can become advantageous or disadvantageous. The warfarin example from Learn 1 shows this clearly: before warfarin was introduced, the resistance allele was rare and neutral. Once warfarin was applied, it became strongly advantageous — and its frequency increased rapidly through selection.

This is evolution in real time. Tracking changes in allele frequency using the Hardy-Weinberg equation allows scientists to detect selection pressure acting on a population — even before the morphological effects are visible.

If a population is in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, allele and genotype frequencies will remain constant from one generation to the next (1)

Command Words

CALCULATE

Always show every step — q² → q → p → p², 2pq, q². Method marks are available at each step. Even if your final answer is wrong, you can score 3 out of 4 marks by showing correct method. Never just write the final answer.

EXPLAIN

For H-W questions: state the biological mechanism, not just the outcome. "Selection pressure increases the frequency of the resistance allele" is incomplete. "Organisms with the resistance allele survive the selection pressure, reproduce, and pass the allele to offspring — over generations the frequency increases" is complete.

SUGGEST

For "suggest why a population is not in equilibrium" questions: name a specific condition that is likely violated, explain the mechanism, and link to the given context. Multiple valid answers are usually accepted.

Common Mistakes — Read these carefully

✗

Starting with p instead of q

Always start with q² (the recessive phenotype frequency) — never start with p. You cannot directly observe AA vs Aa individuals, so p² cannot be measured from the phenotype alone. If you start with p, the whole calculation is invalid.

✗

Using percentage instead of decimal frequency

If 9% of the population is recessive, q² = 0.09, not 9. All values must be decimals between 0 and 1. If your q² is greater than 1, you have made this error.

✗

Forgetting to square root q² to get q

q² gives you the genotype frequency. To get the allele frequency q, you must take the square root. A very common error is to use q² as if it were q in subsequent calculations.

✗

Not checking your answer sums to 1

Always check p² + 2pq + q² = 1 at the end. If it doesn't, you have a rounding error or a calculation mistake — go back and find it. This check takes 10 seconds and can save marks.

✗

Saying "the population is evolving" without saying why

If asked why allele frequencies are changing, you must identify the specific condition of H-W equilibrium that is being violated and explain the mechanism. "The population is evolving" is a description, not an explanation.

Frequency of homozygous recessive (aa) = q² = 0.09
Frequency of heterozygous (Aa) = 2pq = 2 × 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.42
Frequency of homozygous dominant (AA) = p² = 0.7² = 0.49

Check: 0.49 + 0.42 + 0.09 = 1.00 ✓

This population is unlikely to be in true Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. In the real world, the five conditions required — no mutations, random mating, large population, no gene flow, and no selection pressure — are almost never simultaneously met. Mutations occur continuously, mating is rarely fully random, and some selection pressure will be acting on coat colour (e.g. camouflage from predators). This calculation gives us a theoretical expectation; monitoring allele frequencies over time would reveal whether the population is evolving.
