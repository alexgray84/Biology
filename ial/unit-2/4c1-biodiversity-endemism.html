Question 1 · From 4B
What is the name of the system used to classify and name all living organisms, and who developed it?


The binomial nomenclature system, developed by Carl Linnaeus. Organisms are given a two-part Latin name: genus then species (e.g. Homo sapiens). The hierarchy of classification runs: Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.
Question 2 · From 3B
Explain how meiosis and random fertilisation together produce genetic variation between individuals of the same species.


Meiosis produces variation through crossing over (exchange of segments between homologous chromosomes in prophase I) and independent assortment (random orientation of homologous pairs at metaphase I, producing 223 possible combinations in humans). Random fertilisation then combines two unique gametes, dramatically increasing variation in offspring.
Question 3 · From 2C / 3B
What is a gene mutation? Give one example of a condition caused by a gene mutation.


A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA. Mutations can be substitutions, insertions, or deletions. Example: sickle cell anaemia — a single base substitution changes one amino acid in haemoglobin, altering the shape of red blood cells. Mutations are the ultimate source of all genetic variation.
Question 4 · Ecosystems
What is a trophic level? Name the trophic level of a primary producer and explain how energy enters that level.


A trophic level is a feeding level in a food chain or web. Primary producers (plants, algae) occupy trophic level 1. Energy enters at this level via photosynthesis — light energy from the sun is converted into chemical energy stored in organic molecules such as glucose.
Question 5 · Thinking ahead
In your own words, what do you think "biodiversity" means? Have a go before the lesson defines it.


There is no single wrong answer here — this is a thinking question. The formal definition: biodiversity is the variety of living organisms and their genetic differences. It can be measured at different scales: genetic diversity within a species, species diversity within an area, and the variety of different ecosystems.

Why does biodiversity matter to scientists?

Read the following, then use the reveal to check your understanding before moving on.

The Convention on Biological Diversity defines biodiversity as the variety of life on Earth, from the smallest microbes to the largest animals and plants. This includes genetic diversity between individuals within a species and between different species, as well as the variety of different ecosystems.

A precise definition matters because different types of biodiversity require different conservation strategies. You cannot protect ecosystem biodiversity simply by listing species — you need to understand habitat range, genetic health within populations, and the ecological relationships between species. A definition that captures all three scales enables more effective scientific and policy decisions.

The bigger picture

Biodiversity loss doesn't affect ecosystem services one at a time. Because organisms are interdependent, losing one species can cascade through an ecosystem. Microorganisms in soil and water convert ammonia to nitrate ions for plant uptake. Land plants absorb a third of all CO₂ produced by burning fossil fuels. These systems only work at scale — and they all depend on rich biodiversity.

The loss of a keystone species — one that has a disproportionately large effect on its ecosystem — can trigger trophic cascades. Example: the removal of wolves from Yellowstone caused deer populations to overgraze, which eroded riverbanks, which changed river flow patterns. Reintroducing wolves reversed this. This illustrates why even apparently "minor" losses of biodiversity can have far-reaching consequences.

Work through these questions

Question 1
Both plots contain exactly 5 species. Does this mean they have equal biodiversity? Explain your thinking.


No. Although both plots have the same species richness (5 species each), Plot A is dominated by one species (grass at 95%), meaning the other four are barely present. Plot B has a much more even distribution. Biodiversity also depends on how evenly species are distributed — this is called relative species abundance.
Question 2
Which plot is more biodiverse — A or B? Use the data to justify your answer in biological terms.


Plot B is more biodiverse. Both have equal species richness (5 species), but Plot B has a far more even relative species abundance — no single species dominates. An area where different species are more evenly represented is considered more biodiverse because more species are meaningfully contributing to the community. Plot A, dominated by grass at 95%, functions ecologically more like a single-species environment.
Question 3 · Apply it
Plot A might be a well-maintained city park lawn. Suggest what Plot B might represent, and explain why these two habitats differ so much in species abundance.


Plot B might represent a traditional hay meadow or a managed wildflower meadow. City park lawns are regularly mowed, treated with herbicides and fertilisers, and often sown with a single grass cultivar — all of which actively reduce species diversity. A traditional meadow has low soil fertility (which prevents any one species dominating) and is cut infrequently, allowing many species to flower and set seed. Human management is the key driver of the difference.

Endemism and Hotspots

Two case studies — identify the conditions that produce endemic species

What is endemism?

An endemic species is one that evolves in geographical isolation and is found in only one place. Your task is to read the two case studies below and identify the conditions that lead to high endemism in each location.

Case Study 1

Madagascar

Madagascar is a large island off the coast of East Africa. Almost all species found there are endemic — from giant baobab trees to ring-tailed lemurs, from the bizarre elephant's foot plant to the tailless tenrec (a small mammal that can have over 30 babies at a time). The only species on the island that are not endemic have been introduced by humans in recent times. Introduced species can cause significant problems for endemic species.

Case Study 2

The Desert Pupfish

The Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge in Nevada was once covered in springs and rivers. Around 50,000 years ago the climate changed and the area became arid. Water retreated to small isolated ponds. Fish in each pond could no longer interbreed with others and evolved independently, producing four completely separate species of desert pupfish — each endemic to one pond. Water levels there still rise and fall when earthquakes occur in Mexico.

Work through these questions

Question 1 · Compare
What do Madagascar and the Desert Pupfish populations have in common that explains their high levels of endemism?


Both involved geographical isolation. Madagascar is an island — the ocean acts as a barrier to gene flow between its species and those on mainland Africa. The pupfish populations became isolated in separate ponds when water retreated and the surrounding desert became an impassable barrier. In both cases, isolated populations evolved independently through natural selection and genetic drift, eventually producing distinct species found nowhere else.
Question 2 · Checkpoint (from textbook)
Using the maps of bird species richness and bird endemism, describe how the areas of high bird biodiversity and high bird endemism differ, and explain why this might be.


Areas of high species richness are broadly distributed across tropical regions (e.g. Amazon, central Africa, Southeast Asia), while areas of high endemism tend to be more restricted and often correspond to islands or geographically isolated regions (1)
High species richness reflects generally favourable conditions — warm climate, high productivity, stable environment — which support many species (1)
High endemism requires geographical isolation, which prevents gene flow and allows populations to evolve independently — islands, isolated mountain ranges, and fragmented habitats produce endemism even where species richness may be lower overall (1)
Question 3 · Checkpoint (from textbook)
Suggest why endemic populations often have low genetic diversity.


Endemic populations are typically small and isolated / cut off from gene flow with other populations (1)
Small populations experience genetic drift — random changes in allele frequency — which reduces the number of different alleles over time (1)
Inbreeding within small isolated populations also reduces heterozygosity / increases the frequency of homozygous genotypes, reducing genetic diversity further (1)

Biodiversity hotspots — why they're hard to protect

Hotspots cover only 15.7% of the Earth's land surface but are home to 77% of all terrestrial vertebrate species. The problem: these areas are frequently rich in other resources too — timber, minerals, agricultural land — creating direct conflict between human need and conservation. Around 125 different theories have been proposed to explain why certain areas develop such high biodiversity. The current best-supported model suggests three factors: ecosystem stability enabling complex relationships, high productivity supporting more niches, and rapid reproduction enabling faster mutation and adaptation.

Command Words for this Topic

DEFINE

Give the precise meaning of a term. For biodiversity topics, always try to include the key qualifying words (e.g. for endemic: "evolves in geographical isolation" and "found in only one place"). Missing these costs marks.

DESCRIBE

State what you can observe — usually from data, a graph, or a map. For biodiversity maps: state the pattern (e.g. "highest in tropical regions"), use data to support (name specific areas), and note any exceptions. Do not explain — that's the next command word.

EXPLAIN

Give biological reasons for a pattern or process. Use "because" as a prompt. For endemism questions, your explanation must include geographical isolation → no gene flow → independent evolution. Each link in the chain needs to be stated.

SUGGEST

Apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar situation. There may be more than one acceptable answer. Use your understanding of underlying principles — don't try to recall a specific fact that isn't there.

Common Mistakes on this Topic

✗

Confusing species richness with biodiversity

Many students write "area X has more species, so it has higher biodiversity" without considering relative abundance. Always address both dimensions in your answer.

✗

Defining endemic without mentioning isolation

Writing "found in only one place" is incomplete. The full definition requires "evolves in geographical isolation AND is found in only one place." The isolation is the mechanism — it must be in your definition.

✗

Vague ecosystem service answers

"Biodiversity is important for medicine" scores nothing without the mechanism. You need: diverse plants → varied secondary metabolites → potential pharmacological compounds. Examiners reward biological specificity.

✗

Mixing up describe and explain in map questions

If asked to describe a map, name locations and state the pattern. If asked to explain, add the biological reason. Doing both when only one is asked costs time and may introduce contradictions.

Worked Example Answer

EXAM QUESTION (4 marks)

Explain why islands tend to have a high proportion of endemic species compared with mainland regions of similar size.

Model Answer (4/4)

Islands are surrounded by ocean, which acts as a geographical barrier preventing movement of most terrestrial and freshwater species between the island and the mainland. This means there is no gene flow between island populations and mainland populations of the same species. Over time, the island population evolves independently through natural selection acting on the genetic variation present, and through genetic drift — random changes in allele frequency that are more pronounced in small, isolated populations. The combination of no gene flow and independent evolutionary pressure means that over many generations, island populations diverge sufficiently from mainland populations to become distinct species, found only on that island — i.e. endemic. The longer the isolation, the greater the divergence.

Why this scores full marks:

✓ Identifies geographical isolation (ocean barrier) ✓ Links to no gene flow ✓ Explains independent evolution via NS and genetic drift ✓ Directly connects this to the formation of endemic species
