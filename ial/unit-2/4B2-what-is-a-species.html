Core Content · Species Model 1

The Morphological Species Concept

Definition

Morphological species model

A species defined solely on the appearance of organisms observed — their external and sometimes internal morphology. Developed by Linnaeus.

The concept of species is vital for biologists. We use species numbers to measure biodiversity, monitor environmental change, and study adaptations. The problem is that species are defined in many different ways, and the best model changes with the circumstances and type of organism.

The morphological species model was the original approach — scientists would look closely at the outer (and sometimes inner) morphology and group organisms based on similarity of physical characteristics. Much of modern classification is still based on morphology. You would never mistake a lion for a domestic cat — so in many cases it still works well.

However, appearance can be affected by many different things, and there can be huge variation within a closely related group. The clearest problem is sexual dimorphism — where males and females look so different they could be classified as separate species. A peacock and a peahen are a famous example.

A further problem: plants that look identical (like agaves and aloes) may be unrelated — they evolved similar forms in similar environments through convergent evolution. The morphological model would incorrectly group them together.

⚡ Exam Hint

Sexual dimorphism is the most commonly tested limitation of the morphological model. Make sure you can name it, define it, and give an example. The peacock/peahen pairing is perfect — they're the same species but look so different they could be mistaken for two.

Core Content · Species Model 2

The Biological (Reproductive) Species Concept

Definition

Biological species model

A group of organisms with similar characteristics that interbreed to produce fertile offspring. Sometimes refined as: a group in which genes can flow between individuals.

Over time, biologists moved towards defining species based on reproductive behaviour. This overcomes the problem of sexual dimorphism — a peacock and peahen clearly belong together because they interbreed and produce fertile offspring.

In this model, if two individuals from different populations mate and produce fertile offspring, genes can "flow" between them — so they're the same species. This is why a very large horse and a very small pony are the same species: they produce fertile foals. But a horse and donkey produce a mule — which is sterile. Genes cannot flow to the next generation, so they are different species.

The limitations: Lions and tigers are clearly different species — but they can occasionally produce fertile offspring (ligers/tigons). The reproductive model struggles here. It also fails entirely for organisms that don't reproduce sexually: bacteria, many protists, and fungi reproduce asexually. And populations in different geographic locations may not interbreed simply because they never meet — not because they're different species.

Core Content · Species Models 3–5

Other Species Models

The definition of species is constantly evolving. Scientists now use molecular phylogeny — the analysis of DNA, RNA, and proteins — to build new models of species and their relationships. Some of these are more sophisticated than simple observation, though some are no better than the morphological model.

Ecological Species Model
A species defined by the ecological niche it occupies — its role in the environment.
Works when reproductive data is unavailable
Niche definitions vary widely between ecologists
Many species occupy more than one niche
Not a robust method
Mate-Recognition Model
A concept based on unique fertilisation systems, including mating behaviour and signals.
Accounts for behavioural differences between species
Many species will mate with or cross-pollinate others
May produce fertile offspring yet still be different species
Genetic Species Model
A species model based on DNA evidence — how much genetic difference is needed to be a different species?
Potentially the most objective and reliable method
DNA analysis is getting faster and cheaper
Will likely become the main classification method in future
Scientists still have to decide how much genetic difference = different species
Historically expensive and time-consuming to analyse
Cannot be used for fossils (usually no accessible DNA)

Core Content

Limitations of All Species Models

All ways of defining species have significant limitations. No single model solves the species problem for all organisms in all situations. Biologists currently use a combination of the biological definition with basic morphology — but this is imperfect and being refined by molecular techniques.

  • 1

    Finding the evidence: Many living species have never been observed mating. Setting up a breeding programme is time-consuming, expensive, and may not prove anything definitive.

  • 2

    Fertile hybrids in plants: Plants of different but closely related species frequently interbreed and produce fertile hybrids. When should the hybrid itself be regarded as a new separate species?

  • 3

    Asexual reproduction: Many organisms — bacteria, many protists, fungi — reproduce mainly or entirely asexually. Any definition involving reproductive behaviour is simply irrelevant for these.

  • 4

    Fossil organisms: Fossils cannot reproduce and usually don't have accessible DNA. They still need to be classified — but the most useful modern methods don't work on them.

The Importance of DNA in Classification

In recent years scientists have developed techniques that allow them to analyse the DNA and proteins of different organisms. Two key techniques are especially important for classification:

DNA Sequencing
The base sequences of all or part of the genome of an organism are revealed. Gives the complete genetic 'fingerprint' of the organism.
Reveals precise evolutionary relationships
Getting faster and cheaper rapidly
Requires accessible DNA (fails for most fossils)
DNA Profiling
Looks at the non-coding areas of DNA to identify patterns. These patterns are unique to individuals, but similarity of patterns reveals relationships between individuals and species.
Can identify individuals within a species
Shows relatedness between species
Generates enormous volumes of data

DNA sequencing and profiling generates so much data that it would be impossible for individual scientists to manually analyse it all. This has given rise to bioinformatics — the development of software and computing tools needed to organise and analyse enormous quantities of raw biological data. Bioinformatics allows us to make sense of DNA sequencing results to identify species and their evolutionary relationships.

Case Study: Fusarium — The Same But Different

When DNA revealed eight species where one was assumed

The plant disease scab destroys wheat and barley crops. For many years it was thought to be caused by a single fungus, Fusarium graminearum. DNA evidence shows there are at least eight different species of Fusarium pathogens — based on divergence of six different genes. Morphologically they look identical. This has major implications for disease control worldwide.

Case Study: Red Deer — Different But the Same

When DNA revealed one species where two were assumed

Red deer stags that live in woods have much longer and broader antlers than stags on highland mountainsides — they look different enough to be mistaken for different species. But DNA evidence confirms they are the same species. Phenotype alone was misleading; environment was causing the difference.

State the biological (reproductive) species definition. (2 marks)
A group of organisms with similar characteristics that are all potentially capable of breeding to produce fertile offspring (1) / a group of organisms in which genes can flow between individuals (1). Either definition accepted — both are valid phrasings.
Q1.2
Define sexual dimorphism and explain why it is a problem for the morphological species model. (3 marks)
Sexual dimorphism = where there is a great deal of difference between the appearance of the male and female of a species (1). Problem: morphological model classifies based on appearance alone (1); so males and females of the same species with very different appearances could be incorrectly classified as different species (1). T2 · Apply Use your understanding in a new context
Q2.1
A lion and a tiger mate in captivity and produce a liger. The liger is fertile. Explain whether this means lions and tigers are the same species, using the biological species concept. (3 marks)
The biological species concept defines a species as organisms that interbreed to produce fertile offspring (1). If the liger is fertile, this would suggest they could be the same species under this definition (1). However, this is a limitation of the model — lions and tigers are widely accepted as different species and would not mate in the wild; the biological model breaks down in this case (1).
Q2.2
Explain why the biological species model cannot be used to classify bacteria. (2 marks)
Bacteria reproduce asexually (1); any definition based on reproductive behaviour or interbreeding is irrelevant / cannot be applied to organisms that don't reproduce sexually (1).
T3 · Evaluate Longer response — structure your argument clearly
Q3.1
Compare the advantages and limitations of using morphological features, reproductive capability, and DNA analysis to decide if an organism belongs to a particular species. (6 marks)
Morphological — advantage: can be applied to any organism including fossils and asexual species (1); limitation: sexual dimorphism and convergent evolution can lead to incorrect classification (1). Reproductive — advantage: overcomes sexual dimorphism problem; genes can flow between members of the same species (1); limitation: cannot be applied to asexually reproducing organisms or fossils; populations may not interbreed simply due to geography (1). DNA analysis — advantage: most objective and precise method; can be used for any organism with accessible DNA including asexual reproducers (1); limitation: historically expensive; cannot usually be applied to fossils; scientists still must decide how much genetic difference = different species (1).

Subject Vocabulary

Key Terms

Click any term to reveal its definition.

morphological species model
A species definition based solely on the appearance of the organisms observed.
sexual dimorphism
Describes species where there is a great deal of difference between the appearance of the male and female.
biological species model
A group of organisms with similar characteristics that are all potentially capable of breeding to produce fertile offspring — or in which genes can flow between individuals.
molecular phylogeny
The analysis of the genetic material of organisms to establish their evolutionary relationships.
ecological species model
A species definition based on the ecological niche occupied by an organism.
mate-recognition species model
A species definition based on unique fertilisation systems, including mating behaviour.
genetic species model
A species model based on DNA evidence — the degree of genetic similarity or difference between organisms.
DNA sequencing
The process by which the base sequences of all or part of the genome of an organism are worked out.
DNA profiling
The process by which the non-coding areas of DNA are analysed to identify patterns — unique to individuals, but similarity reveals relationships.
bioinformatics
The development of software and computing tools needed to organise and analyse enormous quantities of raw biological data generated by DNA sequencing.
convergent evolution
When unrelated organisms evolve similar features due to similar environmental pressures — e.g. agaves and aloes both adapted to desert conditions but are not closely related.
gene flow
The movement of alleles between populations through interbreeding. In the biological species model, species are groups in which genes can flow between individuals.
