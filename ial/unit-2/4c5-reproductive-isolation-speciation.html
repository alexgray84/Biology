Part 1 — Species, Speciation & Reproductive Isolation

Core content before cooperative activity

What is a species?

A species is a group of organisms sharing several features, which are capable of interbreeding to produce fertile offspring. This is the critical definition. A horse and a donkey can mate to produce a mule — but the mule is infertile, so horses and donkeys are different species.

A species is usually much bigger than a single population. It often consists of many populations spread across a country or even multiple countries. So how, from this mixing pool, do new species form?

Speciation — the formation of new species

Speciation is the formation of a new species. It happens as a result of the isolation of parts of a population. The key factor in the process is reproductive isolation — the reduced gene flow between populations that results from isolation.

Here's the logic: two isolated populations experience different conditions. Different selection pressures act on them. Natural selection pushes the two populations in different directions. Over time, both the genotype and phenotype of the isolated groups change. Eventually, even if the two populations are reunited, they can no longer interbreed — a new species has formed.

Speciation
The formation of a new species through the isolation of parts of a population, accumulation of genetic differences, and eventual inability to interbreed with the original population.

Five isolating mechanisms

For reproductive isolation to occur, gene flow between populations must be reduced. This can happen in several ways:

Geographical

Geographical isolation

A physical barrier such as a river, mountain range, or ocean separates individuals from an original population. No physical contact, no gene flow.

Ecological

Ecological isolation

Two populations inhabit the same region but develop preferences for different parts of the habitat — so they rarely encounter each other.

Seasonal / Temporal

Seasonal isolation

The timing of flowering or sexual receptiveness in some parts of a population becomes different from the usual timing. Populations reproduce months apart and never meet.

Behavioural

Behavioural isolation

Changes in courtship ritual, display, or mating patterns mean some animals no longer recognise others as potential mates — often triggered by a mutation changing colour or markings.

Mechanical

Mechanical isolation

A mutation changes the genitalia of animals so they can only mate with some members of the group. In plants, it can change the relationship between stigma and stamens, making pollination between some individuals unsuccessful.

The headline: Reproductive isolation is the most important factor in speciation. Whatever mechanism causes it, the effect is the same — gene flow stops, populations diverge, and new species can emerge.

Hybridisation — speciation without isolation

Speciation can also occur through hybridisation, particularly common in plants. Two closely related species can breed and form fertile hybrids that are better adapted to a niche than either parent.

Sometimes these hybrids don't produce fertile offspring when crossed back to the parent plants — so a new species is formed. A UK example: the hybrid plant formed between native English and imported Spanish bluebells. The new hybrid species can out-compete both parents.

Part 3 — Allopatric and Sympatric Speciation

Two different paths to new species

Allopatric speciation — speciation by separation

Allopatric speciation occurs when populations become physically or geographically separated. Scientists recognise this as the main evolutionary process for generating new species.

The physical isolation of populations continues naturally — islands form and disappear, ice floes melt, rivers change course, lakes dry up or appear. Human interventions like dams, roads, and cities also create new isolations.

Allopatric speciation (allo = different place)
Speciation that occurs when populations are physically or geographically separated, preventing all interbreeding and gene flow between the populations.

Adaptive radiation

Allopatric speciation is often followed by adaptive radiation — when one species develops rapidly to form several different species, each filling different ecological niches. Two classic examples:

Case Study 1

Australian marsupials and monotremes

Until about 5.5 million years ago, Australia was joined to the rest of the world's land mass. At that time, the only mammals were marsupials (give birth to very immature young, protect them in pouches) and monotremes (the much rarer egg-laying mammals). After Australia separated from other continents, marsupials evolved to fill an enormous range of niches — the large herbivorous kangaroo, the eucalyptus-specialising koala, the carnivorous quoll, and the Tasmanian devil. On other continents, placental mammals evolved and mostly replaced the marsupials, but these did not reach Australia until humans arrived.

Case Study 2

Darwin's finches — the textbook example

The Galapagos Islands sit over 500 miles from land. A small flock of finches was probably carried there by a storm. Within them, there was variation in alleles and beak characteristics. Different niches on the islands favoured different variations — a bird with a smaller, stronger bill could eat seeds; one with a longer, thinner bill could probe dead wood for insects. Natural selection drove specialisation in each niche. Over several million years, at least 14 different species of finch emerged from one common ancestral species. DNA analysis shows they remain remarkably similar genetically, despite looking very different.

Because food was such an important selection pressure, it became advantageous to mate with finches of similar beak shape — mating with a different-beaked finch would produce offspring less well adapted to either feeding strategy. This added a selection pressure on mate choice itself, reinforcing species boundaries. Behavioural and phenotypic changes that made choosing the right mate easier were therefore also selected for.

Sympatric speciation — speciation in the same place

Sympatric speciation (sym = same) occurs between populations living in the same place that become reproductively isolated by mechanical, behavioural, or seasonal changes. Some gene flow continues even as speciation happens — a very different model from allopatric speciation.

Sympatric species are closely related and occupy overlapping ranges. Many scientists are reluctant to classify examples of sympatric speciation — they argue that when speciation occurs, there is always some adaptive pressure (even previously unknown microhabitats) that drives the formation of two species and creates a barrier between them. DNA evidence often shows that species originally thought to be sympatric actually show evidence of cross-breeding or niche separation at some point.

Sympatric speciation
Speciation that occurs between populations of a species living in the same place. They become reproductively separated by mechanical, behavioural, or seasonal mechanisms. Gene flow continues to some extent even as speciation occurs.

Case Study

The Apple Maggot Fly

In North America, the apple maggot fly (Rhagoletis pomonella) originally laid its eggs only on hawthorn fruit. After apple trees were introduced from Europe in the 19th century, some flies began laying eggs on apples instead of hawthorns. Today, the "apple race" and the "hawthorn race" of flies coexist in the same orchards and forests. However:

  • Apples mature about 3 weeks earlier than hawthorn fruit
  • Apple-race flies emerge as adults about 3 weeks earlier than hawthorn-race flies
  • Apple-race flies prefer the smell of apples; hawthorn-race flies prefer the smell of hawthorns
  • Interbreeding between the races has decreased significantly
  • Genetic analysis shows the two races are beginning to diverge — though they are not yet considered separate species
Question 1 · Identify
Is this an example of allopatric or sympatric speciation? Justify your answer using evidence from the case study.


This is an example of sympatric speciation — the two fly populations live in the same geographical location (often the same orchards and forests). There is no physical or geographical barrier separating them. Instead, reproductive isolation is being driven by behavioural and temporal (seasonal) mechanisms within a shared habitat.
Question 2 · Analyse
Identify two different isolating mechanisms at work in this case study and explain how each is contributing to speciation.


Seasonal (temporal) isolation: apple-race flies emerge 3 weeks earlier than hawthorn-race flies. This means their reproductive windows barely overlap — they rarely meet when both are sexually mature — dramatically reducing interbreeding (1)
Behavioural isolation: apple-race flies prefer apple smell, hawthorn-race flies prefer hawthorn smell. This means they actively seek different hosts for egg-laying and mating, so the two populations encounter each other less often — reducing gene flow (1)
Either mechanism explained well scores 2 marks. Both mechanisms reduce gene flow, allowing allele frequencies to diverge between the races through natural selection (1 extra mark for this synthesis)
Question 3 · Evaluate
Some scientists are reluctant to classify this as "true" sympatric speciation. Based on the case study, suggest one reason why they might disagree.


The flies are technically in the same region, but they are in effect using different microhabitats — one race lives on apple trees, the other on hawthorn trees — which functions as a subtle form of ecological separation (1)
The 3-week timing difference means the populations rarely meet as reproductive adults — some scientists argue this is effectively temporal allopatry (separation in time rather than space) (1)
The populations are diverging but have NOT yet become separate species — gene flow is reduced but not eliminated, so this is speciation in progress, not completed speciation (1) (accept any valid reason)
Question 4 · Synthesise
Predict what might happen to these two fly races over the next million years if the current isolating mechanisms continue or strengthen. Use biological reasoning.


Over a million years, several likely outcomes: (1) With continued reduced gene flow, allele frequencies in the two races will continue to diverge through natural selection and genetic drift. (2) Selection pressures will differ — apple-race flies will be increasingly selected for apple-specific adaptations (timing, chemistry, metabolism); hawthorn-race similarly adapted to their host. (3) If interbreeding produces offspring less well-adapted to either host, selection will favour mating preferences that avoid cross-race mating — reinforcing the behavioural isolation. (4) Eventually, the two races will likely diverge sufficiently that they cannot produce fertile offspring — full speciation. (5) This may lead to further adaptive radiation if new fruits or host plants become available.

Part 5 — Population Bottlenecks & Founder Effect

Two ways a population can lose genetic diversity fast

Why population size matters for gene pools

A large population is needed to maintain a large and diverse gene pool. Even in large populations, allele frequencies can be affected by mutations and non-random mating. But when a population is greatly reduced in size, the gene pool shrinks proportionally — and allele frequencies can change dramatically. There are two main ways this happens.

Population bottlenecks

A population bottleneck occurs when a large population's size is dramatically reduced by:

  • Environmental disaster (volcanic eruption, wildfire, flood)
  • A new disease
  • Intensive hunting by humans or predators
  • Habitat destruction

The result: many gene variants present in the original population are lost. The gene pool shrinks. Allele frequencies change dramatically by chance — whichever individuals happened to survive determine what's left in the gene pool.

After a catastrophic event, the remaining small population is vulnerable to the complete loss of some alleles. A single mutation or a new individual can have a much bigger effect than usual. As the population recovers, it may become so genetically different from the original that it effectively becomes a new species.

Case Study — Cheetahs

Cheetahs: the extreme bottleneck

DNA evidence suggests cheetahs came close to extinction at the end of the last Ice Age, at the same time that woolly mammoths, cave bears, and giant deer died out. Although cheetah numbers have recovered, their genetic diversity has not. All modern cheetahs share about 99% of their alleles — extraordinarily low diversity. The heterozygosity index ranges from 0.0004 to 0.014. This causes low fertility and extreme vulnerability to disease or environmental change. In biological terms, the population has recovered in number but not in genetic diversity — and it cannot, because the alleles that were lost are gone forever.

Case Study — Elephant seals

Northern elephant seals: a human-imposed bottleneck

Heavy hunting of northern elephant seals meant that by the end of the 19th century there were only about 20 individuals left. The population has since recovered to over 30,000 — but their allele frequency remains low. They have far less genetic diversity than southern elephant seals, which were less intensively hunted. Compare this to cheetahs: in both cases, small surviving populations led to a genetic "bottleneck" through which only a tiny sample of alleles passed.

The founder effect

The founder effect is the loss of genetic variation that occurs when a small number of individuals leave the main population to set up a separate new population — producing what is essentially a voluntary population bottleneck.

The alleles carried by the founders are unlikely to include all the alleles of the original population, or at the same frequencies. Any unusual genes in the founder members may become more frequent as the population grows.

Founder effect
The loss of genetic variation that occurs when a small number of individuals become isolated, forming a new population with allele frequencies not representative of the original population.
Case Study — Amish

The Amish and Ellis-van Creveld syndrome

Mr and Mrs Samuel King moved to Pennsylvania in 1744 as members of a small group of 200 people who founded an Amish community. One of them was heterozygous for Ellis-van Creveld syndrome — a rare recessive genetic disorder causing a form of dwarfism, extra fingers and toes, and often serious heart problems. Purely by chance, at least one other founding member was also heterozygous for this allele. The Kings had many children, who had many children, and interbreeding in this isolated community raised the frequency of the allele dramatically. By 1964, there were 43 cases of Ellis-van Creveld syndrome in a population of 8,000 — roughly 1 in 14 people now carry the gene. In the general population, this condition occurs in as few as 1 in 200,000 births. This is a clear, measurable demonstration of how the founder effect can dramatically change allele frequencies.

Critical distinction — bottleneck vs founder effect

Population bottleneck: imposed on a population by the loss of most members through natural events or human intervention (disease, hunting, disaster). The population shrinks from within.

Founder effect: the result of the isolation of a small group from an original population. The population grows somewhere new from a small non-representative sample. Both produce a limited gene pool and changed allele frequencies — but the mechanism is different.

Part 6 — Cooperative: Rally Coach

Paired calculation with coaching — one works, one coaches, then swap

🔄

Rally Coach

A Kagan structure where one partner works through a calculation while the other coaches. Then you swap. Both partners end up stronger because teaching is the most powerful way to learn.

How Rally Coach works

1
Work in pairs. Face your partner. Decide who is Partner A and who is Partner B.
2
Round 1 — Partner A solves, Partner B coaches. Partner A works through the first calculation, talking aloud as they go. Partner B watches, listens, and uses the Coach Prompts below to guide without giving the answer.
3
Praise and confirm. When finished, Partner B confirms the answer is correct and praises specifically ("I loved how you checked that p² + 2pq + q² = 1"). If wrong, coach back through the error.
4
Round 2 — Swap roles. Partner B now solves the second calculation aloud. Partner A coaches using the same prompts.
5
Both check. Reveal the answers together and discuss anything unclear before moving on.

The golden rule of coaching

Never give the answer. Ask a question that helps your partner see their own path. "What does q² represent again?" "Have you checked that your answer is between 0 and 1?" "What's the next step after finding q?" Coaching is harder than doing — you have to hold back while your partner thinks.

Round 1 — Partner A solves, Partner B coaches

Scenario 1: Ellis-van Creveld in the Amish Community

A solves · B coaches
The Question (textbook checkpoint Q1) In 1964, there were 43 individuals with Ellis-van Creveld syndrome in an Amish population of 8,000. Ellis-van Creveld is recessive. Use the Hardy-Weinberg formula to calculate:

(a) the frequency of the Ellis-van Creveld allele (q) in the population
(b) the number of individuals homozygous dominant (AA)
(c) the number of individuals heterozygous (Aa) for the dominant phenotype

Partner A — work through this below (Partner B coach using the prompts)

Coach Prompts — Partner B uses these
  • "What's the first step in any Hardy-Weinberg calculation?"
  • "What does q² represent again?"
  • "What's the relationship between q² and q?"
  • "Once you have q, how do you find p?"
  • "To find the number of homozygous dominant people, what do you multiply?"
  • "For heterozygotes, which part of the equation do you use?"
  • "Does your answer make biological sense — do all the groups add up to 8,000?"

(a) Frequency of the EvC allele (q)
q² = 43/8000 = 0.005375
q = √0.005375 = 0.0733 (≈ 0.07)

(b) Number homozygous dominant (AA)
p = 1 − q = 1 − 0.0733 = 0.9267
p² = 0.9267² = 0.8588
Number AA = 0.8588 × 8000 = 6,870 individuals (≈ 6,870)

(c) Number heterozygous (Aa)
2pq = 2 × 0.9267 × 0.0733 = 0.1359
Number Aa = 0.1359 × 8000 = 1,087 individuals (≈ 1,087)

Check: 6,870 + 1,087 + 43 = 8,000 ✓

Interpretation: Around 1 in 14 people in this community now carry the EvC allele — a dramatic illustration of the founder effect raising a very rare global allele (normally ~1 in 200,000 births) to a common one in an isolated community.

Round 2 — Swap roles. Partner B solves, Partner A coaches

Scenario 2: A Remote Island Bird Population

B solves · A coaches
The Question (novel scenario) A small population of 1,200 birds on an isolated island descended from a founder population of just 15 birds that arrived after a storm. Scientists find that 108 birds show a recessive phenotype for a particular colour trait. Use the Hardy-Weinberg formula to calculate:

(a) the frequency of the recessive allele (q)
(b) the number of heterozygous individuals (Aa)
(c) the number of homozygous dominant individuals (AA)

Partner B — work through this below (Partner A coach using the prompts)

Coach Prompts — Partner A uses these
  • "What does the number 108 represent in Hardy-Weinberg terms?"
  • "What do you divide to get q²?"
  • "How do you then get q from q²?"
  • "What's the relationship between p and q?"
  • "For heterozygotes, what does 2pq give you — is that a frequency or a number?"
  • "How do you convert a frequency into a number?"
  • "Does your total add up to 1,200?"

(a) Frequency of the recessive allele (q)
q² = 108/1200 = 0.09
q = √0.09 = 0.3

(b) Number heterozygous (Aa)
p = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7
2pq = 2 × 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.42
Number Aa = 0.42 × 1200 = 504 birds

(c) Number homozygous dominant (AA)
p² = 0.7² = 0.49
Number AA = 0.49 × 1200 = 588 birds

Check: 588 + 504 + 108 = 1,200 ✓

Interpretation: A q of 0.3 is high for a recessive allele — suggesting the founder population of 15 birds likely had several carriers of the recessive allele by chance. This is the founder effect in action: unusual alleles present in a small founder group become far more common in the descendant population than they would in the original mainland gene pool.

🤝 Pair reflection before moving on

Discuss with your partner and answer together:



Both scenarios show how small founding/surviving populations lead to allele frequencies that differ dramatically from the wider population. In Scenario 1, by chance, two founders carried a very rare allele — and interbreeding in isolation raised its frequency to 1 in 14. In Scenario 2, the founder population of 15 birds carried the recessive allele at higher-than-usual frequency, giving q = 0.3 in the descendant population. The founder effect amplifies chance — whatever alleles the founders happened to carry become the new baseline for the isolated population, regardless of whether they were common in the original population.

Command Words for this Topic

DESCRIBE

For isolating mechanisms: state what the mechanism is and what happens. "Seasonal isolation occurs when the timing of flowering or mating differs between populations, preventing them from reproducing together."

EXPLAIN

Give biological reasons. Especially for speciation questions, build the full chain: isolation → reduced gene flow → different selection pressures → divergence → reproductive incompatibility → new species. Each link needs to be stated.

COMPARE

Allopatric vs sympatric is a common compare question. Use paired points ("In allopatric speciation... however, in sympatric speciation..."), not separate paragraphs for each. Linking words earn structural marks.

CALCULATE

Hardy-Weinberg questions on bottleneck and founder populations still use the same process: q² → q → p → p², 2pq. Don't let the biological context distract you from the maths. Show every step.

Common Mistakes on this Topic

✗

Confusing bottleneck with founder effect

These are related but distinct. Bottleneck = population shrinks through loss. Founder effect = new population forms through separation. The Amish example is founder effect; the elephant seals are a bottleneck.

✗

Saying "the population evolves" instead of "allele frequencies change"

Individuals don't evolve — populations do, through changes in allele frequency. Use precise language in exam answers.

✗

Not mentioning reproductive isolation in speciation answers

Reproductive isolation is THE key concept in speciation. Any answer about how new species form must explicitly reference it — just saying "the populations become different" misses the critical mechanism.

✗

Claiming cheetahs are a "founder effect" example

Cheetahs experienced a bottleneck (near-extinction through population reduction), not a founder effect. The Amish, by contrast, are a clear founder effect (small group moved to new location).

Worked Example Answer

EXAM QUESTION (6 marks)

Explain how allopatric speciation led to the diversity of Darwin's finches on the Galapagos Islands.

Model Answer (6/6)

A small flock of finches from the mainland was carried to the Galapagos Islands by a storm, establishing a founder population on islands 500 miles from land. With the islands geographically isolated from the mainland and, crucially, from each other, there was no gene flow between populations on different islands. The finches showed variation in beak shape and other characteristics — the raw material for natural selection. Different islands and different niches on each island favoured different variations: a finch with a stronger, smaller beak gained a survival advantage eating seeds, while a finch with a longer, thinner beak had an advantage probing dead wood for insects. Over many generations, natural selection on each island drove the finch populations in different directions.

Because food sources were a powerful selection pressure, finches with similar beak shapes produced better-adapted offspring when they mated with each other. This created a selection pressure on mate choice itself — favouring behavioural and phenotypic changes that kept individuals mating with their own beak type, reinforcing reproductive isolation. Over several million years, at least 14 distinct species of finch emerged from the one ancestral species — a classic example of adaptive radiation following allopatric speciation. DNA analysis shows these species remain genetically similar despite their different appearances, confirming their common ancestry and recent divergence.

EXAM QUESTION (5 marks)

The northern elephant seal population was reduced to about 20 individuals by the end of the 19th century due to heavy hunting. The population has since recovered to over 30,000 but genetic diversity remains low. Explain the genetic consequences of this event and suggest why this population may be vulnerable in the future.

The dramatic reduction from a large population to ~20 individuals is a population bottleneck — caused by human hunting (1)
Many alleles present in the original population were lost during the bottleneck — only alleles carried by the 20 surviving individuals passed through to the descendant population (1)
Although the population has recovered numerically (30,000+), genetic diversity cannot recover — the lost alleles are gone permanently, so heterozygosity remains low (1)
The population is vulnerable to disease — with low genetic variation, few individuals may carry resistance alleles, so a single pathogen could devastate the whole population (1)
The population is also vulnerable to environmental change — with limited genetic variation, natural selection has less raw material to act on, reducing the population's adaptability to new conditions (climate change, food availability) (1)
