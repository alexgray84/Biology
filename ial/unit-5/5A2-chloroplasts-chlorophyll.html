Section 01 · Hook + Do Now

Why does a leaf look green?

~8 min

A leaf reflects what it cannot use. Green light bounces off, every other colour gets caught. The leaf you see is, in a sense, a leaf made of waste light. The interesting question is not what colour leaves are, but what is happening to all the colours you cannot see.

Teacher notes, pacing & intent

Total runtime: 80–90 min (double period). 8 sections, designed to flow.

Front-loaded retrieval: the Do Now reaches back to Unit 4A.2 (cell ultrastructure) so chloroplasts arrive as a familiar object before students dissect them.

The conceptual hinge is Section 5 (absorption vs action spectra). Students routinely confuse these. Spend time there. The match game in Section 4 and the Rf calculator in Section 6 are deliberate retrieval anchors.

What this lesson does not cover: the light-dependent reactions, photophosphorylation, and the Calvin cycle. Photosystems I and II are introduced in Section 7 only as labelled features, ready for 5A.3.

Learning objectives
  • Understand the overall reaction of photosynthesis as requiring light energy to break the strong bonds in water, storing the hydrogen in glucose by combining it with carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen.
  • Understand the structure of chloroplasts in relation to their role in photosynthesis.
  • Understand what is meant by absorption spectrum and action spectrum.
  • Understand that chloroplast pigments can be separated using chromatography and identified using Rf values.

An average green plant cell contains how many chloroplasts? [1]

Apply · 3
Predict, which cells in a plant will contain no chloroplasts? Justify your answer. [2]
Root cells: they receive no light, so chloroplasts would serve no purpose and would be a metabolic waste. The same applies to inner cells of woody stems and any cell shielded from light. This is a function-follows-light principle: where there is no light energy to capture, there is no machinery to capture it.
Feynman move, frame the lesson

Think of a chloroplast as a solar panel factory. The outer walls are the envelope. The stacks of solar panels are the grana. The scaffolding holding the panels in place is the lamellae. The factory floor where the actual product gets made is the stroma. Hold this image. We will fill it in piece by piece.

Section 02 · The big picture

Photosynthesis is a bond-breaking problem

~10 min

The textbook gives you a one-step equation. The reality is dozens of enzyme-controlled steps. But there is one fact about that equation worth carrying in your head: photosynthesis exists because water is hard to break apart.

carbon dioxide  +  water light energychlorophyll glucose  +  oxygen
6CO2  +  6H2O light energychlorophyll C6H12O6  +  6O2
ΔH ≈ +2880 kJ  ·  endothermic

Read the equation again, slowly. The energy from sunlight is doing one specific job: it is breaking the H–O bonds in water. Those bonds are strong. Once broken, the hydrogen released is locked into glucose by combining with carbon dioxide. The oxygen is released as a waste product, a waste product that, incidentally, every aerobic organism on Earth depends on.

Note the value. ΔH ≈ +2880 kJ, positive. Photosynthesis is endothermic: it stores energy. That stored energy is what is released, in fragments, when a cell respires the same glucose later. Photosynthesis and respiration are mirror reactions.

Learning tip, the link to respiration

Photosynthesis takes in energy and uses CO2 and water to make glucose and oxygen, endothermic. Aerobic respiration takes glucose and oxygen and releases the energy as ATP, with CO2 and water as waste, exothermic. Same atoms. Opposite directions. You will meet aerobic respiration in detail in Chapter 7A.

Why the simplified equation is misleading

Six water molecules in. Six oxygens out. It looks neat. But the equation hides almost everything that matters. It does not tell you:

· that the oxygen released comes only from the water, not from the carbon dioxide;
, that hydrogen is shuttled around as ions and bound to carrier molecules before it ever meets carbon dioxide;
, that the reaction happens in two distinct stages, in two different parts of the chloroplast, using two different photosystems.

For the next two topics (5A.3, 5A.4) you will unpack each of these. Today we lay the foundations: the structure that makes it possible, and the pigments that make it work.

Section 03 · Chloroplast structure

The architecture of a solar factory

~15 min

Chloroplasts are large organelles. An average green plant cell contains 10 to 50 of them. Each is built around a single design problem: how do you arrange a pigment to catch the maximum amount of light, and how do you stage the chemistry that follows?

Structure to function, the ones examiners reward

Structure
Function it enables
Envelope
Two membranes (outer + inner) with a small intermembrane space.
Selectively permeable. Controls movement of substrates (CO2, water) in and products (glucose, O2) out. Isolates the chemistry inside from the rest of the cell.
Grana
Stacks of disc-shaped thylakoids, joined together. The textbook describes this as the granal membrane being covered in particles thought to be involved in ATP synthesis.
Massive surface area for chlorophyll embedded in the thylakoid membranes. Pigments are arranged at the optimal angle to absorb light. Site of the light-dependent reactions.
Thylakoids
Membrane discs found in each granum.
Hold chlorophyll and the photosystem complexes. Their stacked geometry packs more pigment into the same volume than a single flat sheet could.
Lamellae
Extensions of thylakoid membranes that connect two or more grana.
A skeleton inside the chloroplast. Maintain the working distance between grana so each receives maximum light and functions efficiently.
Stroma
The fluid matrix surrounding the grana.
Contains all the enzymes for the light-independent reactions. Site where CO2 is fixed and glucose is assembled. Glucose can then be respired, stored as starch, or built into amino acids and lipids.
Spelling matters

The matrix inside the chloroplast is the stroma. The pores on the underside of a leaf are stomata (singular: stoma). One letter different. Examiners will not give the mark if you confuse them.

Feynman move, why stack the thylakoids?

Imagine a single flat sheet of solar panel. Now stack a hundred discs of the same area on top of each other. The light has to pass through one disc, hit the next, then the next. More pigment per square millimetre of leaf. More photons caught. The stacked geometry is not decorative, it is the only way to fit a serious quantity of pigment into a 5 µm-wide organelle.

Mid-section check Progress
Recall · 1
What is the difference between a granum and a thylakoid? [2]
A thylakoid is a single membrane-bound disc. A granum is a stack of thylakoids. (Plural of granum: grana.)
Apply · 2
A student writes: "The lamellae are where chlorophyll is found." Identify and correct the error. [2]
Chlorophyll is found in the thylakoid membranes, which are stacked into grana. The lamellae are connecting extensions of these thylakoid membranes, they hold the grana in place rather than being the primary site of chlorophyll. (Note: chlorophyll is also present in the lamellae since they are continuous with the thylakoids, but the principal site is the granal thylakoids.)
Stretch · 3
The granal membranes are covered in small particles. Suggest what these particles are and what biochemical role they play. [3]
Scientists think the particles are involved in ATP synthesis. They are ATP synthase enzyme complexes embedded in the thylakoid membrane. They use a proton (H+) gradient generated by the light-dependent reactions to phosphorylate ADP, producing ATP. (You will return to this in 5A.3, chemiosmosis.)
Section 04 · The pigments

Chlorophyll is not one molecule

~10 min

When you say "chlorophyll", you are using a shorthand. Chlorophyll is a mixture of closely related photosynthetic pigments. There are five you need to know. Each one absorbs light from a slightly different part of the spectrum, which means together they harvest more of the available light than any single pigment could alone.

Chlorophyll a
Blue-green
Found in all photosynthesising plants. Present in the highest quantity of the five pigments. The principal pigment in both photosystems.
Rf 0.59
Chlorophyll b
Yellow-green
Found alongside chlorophyll a. Absorbs light at slightly different wavelengths and passes the energy to chlorophyll a.
Rf 0.42
Carotene
Orange
A carotenoid. Absorbs blue-green light and reflects orange. The pigment that makes carrots orange and autumn leaves bright before they fall.
Rf 0.98
Xanthophyll
Yellow
A carotenoid. Often runs as two distinct bands on a chromatogram (xanthophyll 1 and 2) because the group includes related compounds.
Rf 0.28 / 0.15
Phaeophytin
Grey
Not a true working pigment. A breakdown product of the other photosynthetic pigments. Its presence on a chromatogram is normal but does not contribute meaningfully to photosynthesis.
Rf 0.81
Feynman move, why have five?

Imagine your phone could only receive one radio frequency. You would miss every other station. Multiple pigments are the leaf's way of tuning into multiple frequencies of sunlight at once. Different pigments cover different parts of the spectrum, so the energy from light falling on the plant is captured rather than wasted. The textbook calls this an adaptive advantage, and that is exactly what it is.

Exam hint, leaf colour as adaptation

Different proportions of these pigments give plants different leaf colours, and that can be a major adaptation to habitat. Many aquatic plants are red or brown, those colours absorb the blue light that penetrates deepest through water. A green leaf at depth would be a poorly adapted leaf.

Section 05 · Absorption & action spectra

What the pigment grabs vs what the plant does

~12 min

This is the section students most often confuse. Two graphs. Two y-axes. They look similar. They tell you different things. Get the distinction crisp now and the rest of photosynthesis follows easily.

Definitions, plain

Absorption spectrum, a graph showing the amount of light a pigment absorbs at each wavelength. Y-axis: relative absorption of light.

Action spectrum, a graph showing the rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength. Y-axis: rate of photosynthesis (measured by oxygen production, CO2 uptake, or, in Engelmann's case, the movement of bacteria towards oxygen).

Combined absorption spectra of the three main pigment groups, with the action spectrum (dashed) overlaid.

Why this graph is the most important graph in 5A.2

Look at the dashed action spectrum. Now look at the coloured absorption spectra below it. They match. The wavelengths the pigments absorb are the same wavelengths at which photosynthesis runs fastest. The dip in the middle (around 550 nm, green) appears in both: green light is poorly absorbed and produces little photosynthesis. The peaks at the blue end (around 430-470 nm) and the red end (around 660-680 nm) appear in both.

This is the evidence that the pigments are doing the work. If the absorption and action spectra did not line up, you would have to conclude that something other than chlorophyll was capturing the light energy. They line up almost perfectly. They line up better when you include all the pigments than when you just plot chlorophyll a. That is the adaptive advantage of having multiple pigments.

Engelmann's elegant 1880s experiment

T.W. Engelmann produced the first action spectrum without any electronic kit. He shone light of different wavelengths along a strand of filamentous algae and added bacteria that swim towards oxygen. Wherever the bacteria clustered, oxygen was being released, and where oxygen was being released, photosynthesis was running fastest. The bacteria themselves were the rate sensor. Modern action spectra use electronic data logging instead of bacteria, but the principle is identical.

Spectrum check Progress
Recall · 1
Define absorption spectrum and action spectrum in your own words. [2]
Absorption spectrum: a graph showing the amount of light absorbed by a pigment at different wavelengths.
Action spectrum: a graph showing the rate of photosynthesis at different wavelengths of light.
Apply · 2
Use the data on the graph to explain, fully, why plant leaves usually appear green. [3]
The photosynthetic pigments absorb light strongly at the blue end (around 430-470 nm) and the red end (around 660-680 nm) of the visible spectrum. They absorb very little light in the green region (around 500-570 nm), there is a clear trough in the absorption spectrum here. Green light is therefore reflected and transmitted rather than absorbed, so the leaf appears green to the observer. (This is textbook Checkpoint Q2.)
Stretch · 3
A botanist measures an action spectrum for a plant and finds it does not match the absorption spectrum of the chlorophyll extracted from its leaves. Suggest two possible explanations. [4]
Possible explanations:
(1) The plant contains additional pigments not captured in the extraction (perhaps an unusual carotenoid or phycobilin), so the absorption spectrum is incomplete.
(2) Some absorbed light energy may not be passed efficiently to the photosystems, fluorescence or heat loss could mean absorbed light does not all drive photosynthesis.
(3) The action spectrum could have been measured at a wavelength range outside the absorption peaks, or under saturating light where the rate is limited by another factor (e.g. CO2, temperature). Any two well-explained.
Section 06 · Chromatography & Rf values

Pulling the pigments apart

~15 min

A leaf extract looks dark green. To prove that it is actually a mixture of five pigments, you need to separate them. Paper or thin-layer chromatography does this elegantly, different pigments travel up the medium at different speeds in a suitable solvent, and after a few minutes you have a column of bands instead of a smear of green.

The method, briefly

Grind leaves with propanone to dissolve out the pigments. Filter. Spot a small concentrated drop of the green filtrate onto a baseline near the bottom of a strip of chromatography paper or silica gel. Stand the strip in a small amount of solvent (the textbook example uses a 3:1:1 petroleum ether–propanone–chloroform mix) so the solvent is below the spot. Cover the chamber. The solvent rises up the strip by capillary action, carrying the pigments with it. Pigments that interact more weakly with the paper travel further; those that interact more strongly travel less.

The Rf formula

Rf = distance travelled by the pigmentdistance travelled by the solvent

The Rf value is always between 0 and 1. It is unitless. Compare Rf values only if the same solvent and stationary phase were used, in different solvents the same pigment can give very different values.

Rf practice, textbook checkpoint Q3 Apply & Stretch
Apply · 1, Worked example
On a chromatogram, pigment A travelled 4.7 cm. The solvent travelled 5.0 cm. Calculate Rf and identify the pigment. [3]
Rf = 4.7 / 5.0 = 0.94 (to 2 d.p.). The closest match in table A is carotene (0.98). The slight difference is within experimental error, pigments may give slightly different Rf values from chromatogram to chromatogram depending on temperature, solvent purity and how the spot was loaded. Visible colour on the chromatogram should be orange to confirm.
Apply · 2, Independent
On the same chromatogram, pigment B travelled 2.1 cm and the solvent again travelled 5.0 cm. Calculate Rf and identify the pigment. [3]
Rf = 2.1 / 5.0 = 0.42. From table A this matches chlorophyll b (yellow-green band).
Stretch · 3
A student repeats the experiment using a different solvent and the chlorophyll a band gives an Rf of 0.71 instead of 0.59. Explain why this is not a problem for the experiment, but is a problem for using table A to identify the pigment. [3]
Rf values are solvent-specific. The same pigment can give very different Rf values in different solvents because solubility, and therefore travel distance, depends on the solvent's polarity. The experiment itself is fine: you can still separate and identify pigments in any suitable solvent. However, table A's values were measured in a specific 3:1:1 petroleum ether–propanone–chloroform mix on silica gel. Comparing a chlorophyll a Rf of 0.71 to that table will lead to misidentification. The student must use a reference table for their specific solvent system, or run a known sample of chlorophyll a alongside as a comparison.
Section 07 · Photosystems I and II

Two complexes, two jobs

~8 min

The pigments do not float around freely. They are organised into two distinct chlorophyll complexes called photosystem I and photosystem II. You will not need their full mechanism today, that is 5A.3. But you do need to know what they are, where they are, and how they differ.

Feature
Photosystem I (PSI)
Photosystem II (PSII)
Peak absorption wavelength
700 nm (red end)
680 nm (red end, slightly shorter)
Location in chloroplast
Mainly on the intergranal lamellae (the membranes connecting grana)
On the grana themselves (the stacked thylakoid membranes)
Pigment composition
A combination of chlorophylls, different from PSII, which is why peak absorption differs slightly
A different combination of chlorophylls, tuned to 680 nm
Role in photosynthesis
Involved in both cyclic and non-cyclic photophosphorylation (you will meet this in 5A.3)
Involved only in non-cyclic photophosphorylation
Why the names are confusing

Photosystem II was discovered second but, in the non-cyclic light-dependent reaction, electrons flow from PSII to PSI. The numbering reflects discovery order, not the order of action. This trips students up. When you read the textbook diagrams in 5A.3, expect arrows to flow II → I.

Feynman move, why two photosystems?

One pigment complex on its own would behave like a single battery, it would discharge once and stop. The plant needs to keep electrons flowing through a chain that ends with hydrogen attached to a carrier (NADPH). Splitting the work across two photosystems lets the cell re-energise the electrons twice, once at PSII and again at PSI, like a relay race. Each photosystem is a runner, and the baton is the electron. Two runners can carry that baton further than one ever could.

Photosystem check Progress
Recall · 1
State the peak absorption wavelength of PSI and PSII. [2]
PSI peaks at 700 nm. PSII peaks at 680 nm. Both are at the red end of the visible spectrum.
Apply · 2
Explain why PSI and PSII absorb at slightly different wavelengths despite both containing chlorophyll. [2]
Each photosystem contains a different combination of chlorophyll pigments. The exact mix of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and accessory pigments is different in each, and the local protein environment also shifts the absorption peak slightly. The two photosystems are tuned to slightly different wavelengths, which broadens the range of light the chloroplast can use.

Exam-style synoptic question

6-mark response, modelled Exam technique
Stretch · synoptic
Describe how the structure of a chloroplast is adapted to its function in photosynthesis. [6]

Model answer:

A chloroplast is bounded by an envelope of two membranes that selectively control the movement of substrates and products. Inside, stacks of thylakoid membranes called grana hold chlorophyll molecules in the optimal position to capture light energy. The granal stacking provides a large surface area for pigment, allowing the chloroplast to absorb a maximum proportion of incident light despite its small volume.

Lamellae extend between grana and act as a structural skeleton, holding the grana at a working distance so that each receives maximum light and functions efficiently. Multiple photosynthetic pigments, chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids, are arranged on the thylakoid membranes; together they absorb across a wider range of wavelengths than any single pigment, so more of the available light is captured.

The stroma surrounds the grana and contains all the enzymes for the light-independent reactions, so the products of the light-dependent stage (ATP and reduced NADP) can be used immediately to fix CO2 into glucose. Particles on the granal membranes are involved in ATP synthesis, coupling light capture to chemical energy production.

Band descriptors:

5–6 marks: All major structures named with correct location AND linked function. Refers to multiple pigments and the role of stacking. Uses correct technical vocabulary throughout.

3–4 marks: Most structures named with some function. May lack the link between structure and adaptation, or miss surface area / pigment variety.

1–2 marks: Names a few features. Function generic or missing. Likely confuses stroma with stoma or thylakoids with grana.

Textbook checkpoint Q1 Critical thinking
Recall
(a) Chloroplasts are not present in all plant cells. Explain why not. [2]
Chloroplasts are only present in cells that receive light, typically those in the green parts of the plant such as leaves and young stems. Cells in roots, woody tissue, and any internal cell shielded from light have no chloroplasts because there is no light energy to capture. Producing chloroplasts where they cannot function would be a metabolic waste.
Apply
(b) Summarise the adaptations of chloroplasts for their role in photosynthesis, including the role of membranes. [5]
Key points (any 5):
· Double envelope isolates the internal environment and controls movement of substrates and products.
· Grana are stacks of thylakoids, providing a large surface area for chlorophyll and the photosystems on the thylakoid membranes.

· Lamellae connect grana and maintain working distance so each granum receives maximum light.
· Multiple pigments (chlorophyll a, b, carotenoids) capture a wider range of wavelengths than any single pigment.
· Stroma contains the enzymes for the light-independent reactions, sited next to the source of ATP and reduced NADP.
