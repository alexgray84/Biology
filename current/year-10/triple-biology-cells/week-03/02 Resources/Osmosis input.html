Why does water move?

Learning outcomes

  • Explain and predict net water movement through a partially permeable membrane. 4BI1: 2.15 — osmosis component.
  • Use the model to predict tissue-mass changes and prepare a fair comparison. 2.17 — practical preparation; links to 2.16.

Do now: predict

Imagine two similar pieces of living potato tissue. One goes into water; the other into a sucrose solution more concentrated than the fluid inside its cells. Predict a difference in their mass or firmness. Record a reason or an uncertainty that the membrane model will help you resolve.

Input: water and the membrane

A solute is a dissolved substance, such as sucrose. A more concentrated solution has more dissolved solute per unit volume than a more dilute solution.

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane. This is movement down a water-potential gradient.

In our model, the membrane allows water through but prevents the illustrated sucrose crossing. Water molecules cross in both directions. When more move one way than the other, there is net movement in that direction. With equal water potential, water still moves but there is no net movement. Osmosis does not require ATP from respiration.

Build and explain the model

Represent two compartments with a membrane between them. Label the concentration of sucrose on each side. Show water crossing in both directions, then identify the net direction.

Model example: the inside contains 10% sucrose; the outside contains 2% sucrose. The outside is more dilute, so net movement of water is into the model cell. Use the same concentration scale and assume only water can cross.

For a new case, first identify the more dilute side. Explain which substance crosses, which direction it moves overall, and which barrier it crosses. Then use the three cases on the osmosis practice sheet.

Input: a plant cell changes

When water enters a typical living plant cell, its vacuole expands. The cell wall resists expansion, helping the cell become turgid. When water leaves, the cell loses turgor. With enough water loss, the membrane can pull away from the wall; this is plasmolysis. The cell wall itself does not shrink inwards with the membrane.

In plant tissue, these changes can affect firmness and mass. The direction depends on the surrounding solution compared with the cell sap. The relevant partially permeable barrier is the cell membrane.

Explain how a potato cylinder could lose mass even while some water molecules continue to enter its cells.

Input: measure the change fairly

Illustrative measurements: a cylinder changes from 2.50 g to 2.80 g.

  1. Final mass minus initial mass gives +0.30 g.
  2. Divide by the initial mass: 0.30 ÷ 2.50.
  3. Multiply by 100: +12%.

The positive sign shows a gain. Use percentage change to compare changes relative to the starting masses. Keep cylinder dimensions consistent as well: differences in geometry can affect the rate of water exchange.

Change the sucrose concentration and measure percentage mass change after a fixed time. Keep the potato source, cylinder dimensions, immersion time, solution volume and temperature consistent. Blot surface liquid off consistently before weighing; otherwise, surface liquid can distort the measured tissue-mass change. Repeats help you judge how consistent the results are.

Think further

Two cylinders show the same percentage mass change, but one was left in solution twice as long. How strongly does that support a comparison of the solutions? Explain the limitation and suggest one improvement.
