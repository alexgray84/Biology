Do now

Answer briefly from memory in your book:

  2. Which structure regulates movement of substances into and out of a cell?
  3. What do ribosomes do?

Input: connect the structure to its function

Structure Function
Nucleus Contains most of a typical eukaryotic cell's genetic information.
Cell membrane Regulates movement of substances into and out of the cell.
Ribosomes Make proteins.
Mitochondria Sites of aerobic respiration.

The cell membrane and cell wall are different structures. A plant's cellulose wall supports the cell. Its cell membrane controls exchange. Chloroplasts absorb light for photosynthesis; they are found in photosynthetic plant cells, but not in every plant cell.

Use the structures to explain a function. For example, a cell producing and secreting proteins needs ribosomes for protein synthesis; aerobic respiration in its mitochondria helps provide energy for its activities.

Input: organise the evidence

Eukaryotic cells typically contain a membrane-bound nucleus. Plants, animals, fungi and protoctists are eukaryotic. Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria, have no membrane-bound nucleus; their DNA lies in the cytoplasm. Bacteria still have ribosomes and a cell membrane.

Compare one pair at a time:

Comparison Information that helps us distinguish them
Bacterium and yeast Both can be single-celled. A yeast cell has a nucleus and a chitin wall; a bacterium has no membrane-bound nucleus.
Plant and animal Both are multicellular eukaryotes. Plants have cellulose walls and photosynthetic cells contain chloroplasts. Animal cells have neither cell walls nor chloroplasts; animals obtain nutrients by feeding.
Plant and photosynthetic protoctist A plant is multicellular. Chlorella lives as one cell with a nucleus and chloroplasts. Chloroplasts alone do not distinguish these groups.

Many fungi grow as hyphae, thread-like structures that form a mycelium. They have chitin walls. In saprotrophic nutrition, enzymes are released onto dead material; digestion occurs outside the organism and soluble products are absorbed. Yeast is a single-celled fungus. Protoctists are varied: some, such as Chlorella, photosynthesise; others, such as Amoeba, do not.

A modelled classification

A specimen is one cell with a nucleus and a chitin wall. It lacks chloroplasts.

  • The nucleus supports a eukaryotic classification.
  • Being single-celled does not make it a bacterium.
  • Chitin is the decisive wall evidence here, supporting fungus.
  • Absence of chloroplasts alone would not be enough: animal cells and many protoctists also lack them.

Now use the classification cases. Give your decision, then identify the evidence that makes the decision reasonable. What extra information would you need if the description only said “has a nucleus and no chloroplasts”?

Input: differentiation and why it matters

Differentiation is the process in which an unspecialised cell develops specialised structures and functions. Different genes become active, leading to different proteins. Most nucleated body cells contain the same genes; they do not all use them in the same way.

Mitosis increases cell number. Differentiation enables cells to perform different jobs. These specialised cells work together in tissues and organs, allowing the organism to function. Producing more identical, unspecialised cells would not provide all these different functions.

  • A palisade cell has many chloroplasts. They absorb light for photosynthesis in a leaf.
  • A nerve cell has a long axon. It carries impulses over a distance.
  • A heart-muscle cell has specialised contractile proteins. It contracts, helping the heart pump blood.

Connect the feature to the specific function, then explain why that function matters to the tissue or organism. A definition alone does not fully explain the importance of differentiation.

Input: stem-cell abilities and sources

A stem cell is undifferentiated and has two key abilities:

  • Self-renewal: division by mitosis maintains a population of undifferentiated stem cells.
  • Differentiation: stem cells can produce specialised cells.
Source Range of cell types
Embryonic stem cells, from early embryos Almost all body-cell types
Adult tissue stem cells, in tissues such as bone marrow Usually a more restricted range

Blood-forming adult stem cells produce several blood-cell types. They are one adult example; not all adult stem cells are blood-forming. “Restricted” does not mean “only one type”.

Input: using stem cells in medicine

Blood-forming stem-cell transplantation is used to restore blood-cell production in treatment for some blood cancers. Other tissue-repair examples in this lesson are hypothetical proposals.

Embryonic cells have broad differentiation potential, so they could supply many types of replacement cell if development is controlled. The specific adult blood-forming cells considered here have a limited range, making them useful for blood-cell replacement but limiting their use for other proposed repairs.

Successful repair needs the correct cell type, survival and effective function in the tissue. For example, replacement heart-muscle cells would need to contract and contribute to pumping. Producing a cell type in a laboratory does not by itself demonstrate successful treatment in a patient.

Input: compare advantages and disadvantages

Issue What to explain
Suitable cell type The cells must be able to develop into the type needed by the damaged tissue. Compare the two sources in the specific question.
Donor mismatch The immune system may recognise donor cells as foreign and attack them. Matching and immune-suppressing medicines may be needed. Using the patient's own cells avoids donor mismatch but does not remove all treatment risks.
Growth control Uncontrolled division, including from undifferentiated cells left in a preparation, may produce a tumour.
Embryo use Obtaining embryonic cells can destroy an embryo. Some people object because they regard it as potential human life. Others weigh this against possible medical benefits. Adult-cell collection avoids this particular concern.
Evidence Cell production in a laboratory does not establish survival, function or safety after transplantation.

A biological risk concerns a harmful process in the body. An ethical concern asks what should be permitted. Both can matter in an evaluation; answer the exact question asked.

Prepare an exam-style explanation

Explain: give a point and the reason or mechanism linking it to the outcome.

Evaluate: weigh relevant strengths and weaknesses, apply them to the situation, and reach a judgement supported by evidence.

For a comparison, develop a linked sentence: “Source ___ could help because ___. Compared with ___, this matters because ___.” Then explain a limitation and use the evidence to reach a judgement. A fixed number of paragraphs or a memorised list does not guarantee marks.
