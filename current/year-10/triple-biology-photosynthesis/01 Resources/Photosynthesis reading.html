Big question: Where does a plant’s food, and its mass, come from?

Part A · Where did the mass come from?2.18 · screen 5

Jan Baptist van Helmont (published 1648) grew a willow in a pot of weighed soil for 5 years. He added only rainwater. The tree gained about 74 kg (fresh mass), but the dry soil lost only about 0.06 kg (historical data, rounded).

  • The soil cannot explain the tree’s mass: it lost almost nothing. The soil does supply mineral ions, but they are a tiny mass.
  • Van Helmont concluded that the mass came from water. He did not know that plants take in carbon dioxide from the air.
  • We now know that most of the dry mass comes from carbon dioxide: its carbon atoms are built into glucose, then into starch and cellulose. Water also contributes, and much of a living plant’s fresh mass is water.

Check your understanding

  1. Use van Helmont’s data to explain why the tree’s mass did not come from the soil. [2]

    The tree gained about 74 kg [1] but the dry soil lost only about 0.06 kg [1], so almost none of the mass came from the soil.

  2. Where does most of a plant’s dry mass come from? [1]

    Carbon dioxide from the air, whose carbon is built into glucose, starch and cellulose [1]. Do not accept “only from carbon dioxide”: water also contributes.

Part B · Photosynthesis is an energy transfer2.18 · screens 6, 8

Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts. Most chloroplasts are in the palisade mesophyll cells near the top of the leaf. Chlorophyll, the green pigment in chloroplasts, absorbs light energy, mostly red and blue light. Green light is mainly reflected, which is why leaves look green.

  • In: light energy; carbon dioxide (absorbed from the air by diffusion); water (from the roots).
  • Out: glucose (used or stored as starch); oxygen (released by diffusion).
  • Light energy is transferred to chemical energy stored in glucose. Glucose is made from carbon dioxide and water: light supplies energy, not atoms. Chlorophyll is not used up.

Why photosynthesis matters

  • It is the route by which energy enters food chains: plants are producers.
  • It makes the glucose that the plant uses in respiration and to build other molecules.
  • It releases oxygen and removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

Say that light energy is transferred. Never write that plants “make energy”, that glucose is “made of light”, or that plants “breathe in” carbon dioxide.

Check your understanding

  1. Describe the energy transfer in photosynthesis. [2]

    Light energy [1] is transferred to chemical energy stored in glucose [1].

  2. Explain why leaves look green. [2]

    Chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue light [1]. Green light is mainly reflected [1].

  3. Give two reasons why photosynthesis is important for animals. [2]

    Any two [1] each: energy enters food chains through plants (producers); it releases the oxygen animals need; it removes carbon dioxide from the air.

Part C · The equations2.19 · screens 6–7

The word equation, with light and chlorophyll written by the arrow as conditions:

To write the balanced symbol equation, write the formulae, count every kind of atom on each side, and add numbers in front until the counts match. Never change a formula.

The atom model shows the same thing: six carbon dioxide and six water molecules rearrange into one glucose and six oxygen molecules. No atom appears or disappears. Light adds no atoms.

Check your understanding

  1. Write the balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis. [2]

    6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2. Correct formulae [1]; balanced [1]. Accept “light” above and “chlorophyll” below the arrow.

  2. To make two glucose molecules, how many molecules of carbon dioxide and water are used, and how many oxygen molecules are released? [3]

    12 CO2 [1], 12 H2O [1], 12 O2 [1].

  3. A pupil balances the equation by writing H2O6. Explain why this is wrong. [1]

    Changing a formula changes the substance (H2O6 is not water); only the numbers in front may change [1].

Destarching and the starch test

  • A plant kept in the dark for about 48 hours uses up the starch in its leaves: starch is broken down to glucose, which is used in respiration or moved to other parts of the plant.
  • With no light there is no photosynthesis, so no new glucose and no new starch. Iodine stays orange-brown.
  • Starch test (full detail in Lesson 5): boil the leaf in water; warm it in ethanol in a hot water bath (no flames); rinse; add iodine. Blue-black means starch.

Check your understanding

  1. Explain why plants store glucose as starch. [2]

    Starch is insoluble [1], so it does not affect water movement by osmosis (and stays in the cells) [1].

  2. Name the extra substance a plant needs, as well as glucose, to make amino acids. [1]

    Nitrate ions (from the soil) [1].

  3. A leaf from a plant kept in the dark for 48 hours is tested with iodine. Predict the colour and explain your prediction. [3]

    Orange-brown: no starch is present [1]. With no light there is no photosynthesis, so no new glucose is made [1]. The stored starch was broken down to glucose (sugar) and used in respiration, or moved to other parts of the plant [1].
