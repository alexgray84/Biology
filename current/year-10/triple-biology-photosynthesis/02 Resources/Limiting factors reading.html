Limiting factors of photosynthesis

Year 10 Triple Biology · Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Biology (Modular) 4XBI1 · 2.20 · 2.23 (oxygen evolution)

The big question: what stops a plant photosynthesising faster? The graphs on this page use the lesson’s simulated data: numbers from a computer model of pondweed, not measurements from a real plant.

1 · What is a limiting factor? 2.20

Photosynthesis needs light energy, carbon dioxide and water, and its reactions are controlled by enzymes, so temperature matters too.

A limiting factor is the factor in shortest supply. It stops the rate of photosynthesis going any higher. Increasing a limiting factor increases the rate. Increasing a factor that is not limiting does not.

2 · Light intensity 2.20

  • Rising section (0–40 %): more light gives more energy, so the rate increases. Light is the limiting factor.
  • Plateau (60–100 %): more light makes little difference. Another factor, carbon dioxide concentration or temperature, is now limiting.
  • A plateau does not mean the plant is at its maximum rate. The next figure shows why.

3 · Carbon dioxide concentration 2.20

  • CO₂ is a raw material. More CO₂ raises the plateau: 4.0 cm³ at 0.1 %, 7.0 cm³ at 0.5 % and 9.2 cm³ at 1.0 % (at 100 % light).
  • So on the 0.1 % and 0.5 % plateaus, CO₂ concentration was limiting.
  • At 20 % light the 0.5 % and 1.0 % curves almost overlap (3.5 and 3.6 cm³): in dim light, light is limiting, so extra CO₂ hardly helps. The 0.1 % curve is a little lower (2.9 cm³) because, with so little CO₂, CO₂ also limits.

4 · Temperature 2.20 · links to enzymes

  • Up to the optimum: molecules have more kinetic energy, collide more often and form more enzyme–substrate complexes, so the rate rises.
  • Above the optimum: the enzymes denature. The active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits, and the rate falls.
  • Grade 8–9: at low light intensity, temperature has much less effect below the optimum, because light, not enzyme activity, is limiting.

5 · The pondweed investigation 2.23

  1. Put a piece of pondweed (Elodea or Cabomba), cut end up, in a boiling tube of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution, which supplies dissolved CO₂. The stem is cut under water.
  2. Stand the tube in a water bath with a thermometer to keep the temperature constant. An LED lamp gives off little heat.
  3. Set an LED lamp at a fixed distance and choose the brightness. Leave the pondweed to acclimatise for about 5 minutes.
  4. Collect the gas in a gas syringe (or an upturned measuring cylinder) for 5 minutes and read the volume.
  5. Repeat three times and calculate a mean, leaving out any anomaly. Then change the brightness.

Counting bubbles is quicker but less accurate, because bubble size varies. The gas is mostly, but not only, oxygen, and the plant uses some oxygen in its own respiration, so the volume underestimates the gross rate.

Safety

The lamp gets hot. Keep water away from mains electrics. Wash your hands after handling pondweed. Sodium hydrogencarbonate is low hazard.

Go further (not required): if you move the lamp instead of changing its brightness, light intensity ∝ 1 ÷ distance². Doubling the distance gives one quarter of the intensity.

6 · Describe, then explain a graph Exam skill

  • Describe = what the line does, with values and units.
  • Explain = why, using the limiting factor for each section and your evidence.

Key words

Limiting factor
The factor in shortest supply; it stops the rate going higher.
Rate of photosynthesis
How fast photosynthesis happens; here, the volume of gas in 5 minutes.
Sodium hydrogencarbonate
NaHCO₃; in solution it supplies carbon dioxide.
Acclimatise
Leave the pondweed about 5 minutes at a new condition before measuring.
Plateau
The flat part of a graph.
Denatured
The active site has changed shape, so the substrate no longer fits.
Simulated data
Numbers from a computer model, not a real experiment.

Never say

  • “The plant gets tired” or “has reached its maximum” (name the limiting factor).
  • “The enzymes are killed” or “die” (they denature).
  • “Doubling the light always doubles the rate.”
  • “Plants breathe in CO₂” (they take in or absorb CO₂ by diffusion).
