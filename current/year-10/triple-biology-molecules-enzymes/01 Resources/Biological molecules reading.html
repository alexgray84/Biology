Big question: What are foods made of — and how can we prove it?

Part A · Which elements?2.7 · screen 4

An element is one kind of atom. The large molecules in food are built from only a few elements:

  • Carbohydrates: carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O).
  • Proteins: C, H, O and nitrogen (N). Many proteins also contain sulfur (S).
  • Lipids (fats and oils): C, H and O, with a much smaller proportion of oxygen than carbohydrates.

Check your understanding

  1. Name the element found in proteins but not in carbohydrates or lipids. [1]

    Nitrogen (N) [1].

  2. Carbohydrates and lipids contain the same three elements. How are they different? [1]

    Lipids contain a much smaller proportion of oxygen than carbohydrates [1].

Part B · Big molecules from small units2.8 · screens 5–7

Carbohydrates, proteins and lipids are large molecules made from smaller basic units. Digestion breaks them back down into those units: you will meet this in the next lesson.

Carbohydrates

Glucose is a simple sugar. Starch and glycogen are large molecules made from many glucose units joined together.

  • Starch is the storage carbohydrate in plants, for example in potatoes and rice.
  • Glycogen is the storage carbohydrate in animals, in the liver and muscles. It is more highly branched than starch.

Cellulose, in plant cell walls, is also made from glucose, but its job is support, not storage.

Proteins

A protein is a chain of amino acids. There are about 20 different kinds, joined in a specific order. The chain folds into a specific 3D shape, and that shape decides the protein’s function. Enzymes, antibodies, haemoglobin, some hormones and muscle proteins are all proteins.

Order → shape → function: change the order of amino acids and the protein may fold into a different shape, so it may no longer do its job.

Lipids

A typical lipid is one glycerol joined to three fatty acids. Fats are solid at room temperature; oils are liquid. A lipid is not a long chain of one repeating unit, so it is not built like starch or a protein.

Check your understanding

  1. Name the smaller units that make up starch, glycogen, protein and lipid. [4]

    Starch: glucose [1]. Glycogen: glucose [1]. Protein: amino acids [1]. Lipid: glycerol and fatty acids [1].

  2. Give two differences between starch and glycogen. [2]

    Starch is the plant store; glycogen is the animal store, in the liver and muscles [1]. Glycogen is more highly branched [1].

  3. Explain why changing the order of amino acids can stop a protein working. [2]

    A different order folds into a different specific 3D shape [1]. The shape decides the function, so the protein may no longer do its job [1].

  4. A student writes: “A lipid is a long chain of glycerol units.” Correct the statement. [1]

    A lipid is one glycerol joined to three fatty acids: two kinds of unit, not a chain of one repeating unit [1].

  • Carbohydrate is the main source of energy. The energy is released in respiration.
  • Protein is needed for growth and repair, and to make enzymes and antibodies.
  • Lipid is an energy store. It releases about twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrate: about 37 kJ/g compared with about 17 kJ/g (approximate values). Lipids also insulate the body, protect organs and form part of cell membranes.

Say that respiration releases energy. Never write that food “makes energy”.

    Lipid releases about 37 kJ/g, carbohydrate about 17 kJ/g [1]. So the same mass of nuts releases about twice as much energy [1].

  2. Rewrite correctly: “Carbohydrates make energy.” [1]

    Carbohydrates release energy in respiration [1].

Part D · Testing foods2.9 · Core Practical 1 · screens 10–18

Prepare the sample

Grind solid food with a little distilled water using a mortar and pestle. Decant or filter off the liquid and test that. Iodine can also be dropped straight onto solid food.

One table, four tests

SubstanceReagentMethodPositive resultInterpretation
StarchIodine solutionA few drops; no heatingOrange-brown → blue-blackBlue-black = starch present
GlucoseBenedict’s solutionEqual volume; water bath at about 80 °C for about 5 minBlue → green → yellow → orange → brick-red precipitateFurther along the sequence = more reducing sugar, such as glucose
ProteinBiuret reagentAdd to a liquid sample and mix; no heatingBlue → purple (lilac)Purple = protein present
Fat (lipid)Ethanol, then waterShake the sample with ethanol; pour into an equal volume of waterCloudy white emulsionCloudy = lipid present

Negative results: iodine stays orange-brown; Benedict’s stays blue; Biuret stays blue; the ethanol–water mixture stays clear.

Biuret reagent can be replaced by sodium hydroxide solution followed by a few drops of dilute copper sulfate solution. In the emulsion test, lipid dissolves in ethanol but not in water, so in water it forms tiny droplets that scatter light: the mixture looks cloudy white.

Controls

  • A tube of distilled water tested the same way is a negative control: it shows the colour when no nutrient is present.
  • A known sample, such as glucose solution, is a positive control: it shows that the reagent and method work.
  • To compare samples, use the same volumes of sample and reagent. For Benedict’s, also use the same heating time and temperature.

Benedict’s shows how much

The further along the sequence blue → green → yellow → orange → brick-red, the more reducing sugar. This makes the test semi-quantitative: you can rank samples, but you get no actual concentration. To measure the amount, use a colorimeter, filter, dry and weigh the precipitate, or time the first colour change with the same volumes and temperature.

Benedict’s detects reducing sugars such as glucose and maltose. Sucrose and starch do not give a positive result, so “blue” means “no reducing sugar detected”, not “no sugar”.

Writing a method

In a 6-mark method, each line is one action with one detail: a volume, a temperature, a time or a result. Only the tests the question asks for earn marks.

Limitations

  • Coloured foods can mask a colour change: dilute or filter them, or use a colorimeter.
  • Judging colour by eye is subjective: compare against the control in the same light.
Safety. Eye protection throughout. Biuret reagent contains sodium hydroxide (irritant or corrosive) and copper sulfate (harmful). Benedict’s is an irritant. Iodine irritates and stains. Ethanol is highly flammable: no naked flames, and never heat it. Heat the water bath with a kettle or thermostatic bath, not a Bunsen. Use a test-tube holder and point tubes away from people.

Check your understanding

  1. Which test needs heating, and how is it heated? [2]

    The Benedict’s test [1]. In a water bath at about 80 °C for about 5 minutes [1].

  2. State the colour change for a positive Biuret test. [1]

    Blue → purple (lilac) [1].

  3. Explain why the Benedict’s test is described as semi-quantitative. [2]

    The colour shows the relative amount of reducing sugar, so samples can be ranked [1], but it gives no actual concentration [1].

  4. A sucrose solution stays blue when heated with Benedict’s. Explain why. [2]

    Benedict’s detects only reducing sugars [1]. Sucrose is not a reducing sugar, so no precipitate forms [1].
