Biological molecules and food tests: exam-style practice

Year 10 Triple Biology · Lesson 1 · Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Biology (Modular) 4XBI1 · 2.7, 2.8, 2.9, 2.25 (selected)

These are original practice questions, not questions from a Pearson paper. The marks are classroom allocations, not a Pearson mark scheme. Write your answers in your book, then check with Show / hide answers. Questions tagged Homework are extra original practice.

Part A · Recall and quick checkseveryone

  1. Lesson · screen 3Answer from memory.

    • (a) Ribosomes make ______. [1]
    • (b) Plants store carbohydrate as ______. Animals store it as ______. [2]
    • (c) Plant cell walls are made of ______. [1]
    • (d) Predict which releases most energy per gram: sugar, butter or egg white. Give a reason. [1]

    (a) Proteins [1]. (b) Starch [1]; glycogen [1]. (c) Cellulose [1].

    (d) Butter [1]. It is mostly lipid, which releases about twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrate or protein (about 37 kJ/g compared with about 17 kJ/g; approximate values).

    Do not accept “makes energy”: say releases.

  2. Lesson · screen 9Which statement is correct? [1]

    1. Glycogen is a chain of amino acids.
    2. Starch and glycogen are both made from glucose.
    3. A lipid is a long chain of glycerol units.
    4. Proteins contain only C, H and O.

    Then explain why one wrong option is wrong. [1]

    B [1]. Starch (plants) and glycogen (animals) are both made from many glucose units.

    Any one explanation [1]:

    • A: glycogen is made of glucose, not amino acids.
    • C: a lipid is one glycerol joined to three fatty acids, not a chain of glycerol.
    • D: proteins also contain nitrogen (and often sulfur).

  3. Lesson · screen 15Why is a tube of distilled water included in each food test? [1]

    1. To make the reagent react faster.
    2. To dilute the food so colours are easier to see.
    3. To show the colour with no nutrient present, for comparison.
    4. To prove the food contains water.

    What would testing a known glucose sample add? [1]

    C [1]. Distilled water has no nutrient, so it shows the negative colour to compare with.

    A known glucose sample is a positive control: it shows the reagent and method work [1].

  4. Lesson · screen 19Exit check.

    • (a) Name the building blocks of a lipid. [1]
    • (b) Which test needs heating? Why must there be no flames in the ethanol test? [2]
    • (c) Iodine turns blue-black; Benedict’s stays blue after heating. Interpret these results. [2]

    (a) Glycerol and fatty acids [1].

    (b) The Benedict’s test [1]. Ethanol is highly flammable [1].

    (c) Starch is present [1]. No reducing sugar, such as glucose, was detected [1].

  5. Homework · originalWhich substance gives a positive Benedict’s test when heated? [1]

    1. Starch
    2. Sucrose
    3. Maltose
    4. Amino acids

    Explain why sucrose gives a negative result even though it is a sugar. [1]

    C, maltose [1]: a reducing sugar made of two glucose units.

    Benedict’s detects only reducing sugars; sucrose is a non-reducing sugar, so it stays blue [1].

  6. Homework · originalA molecule from a food contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. Which group of biological molecules does it belong to? Explain your answer. [2]

    Protein [1]. Of the three groups, only proteins contain nitrogen: every amino acid contains N [1].

  2. UAE context · hypothetical classroom scenarioA local chickpea snack says “source of protein, no starch”. Iodine turned blue-black; Biuret turned purple. Is each claim supported? [4]

    Protein: only partly. Biuret turned purple, so protein is present [1]; the amount was not tested, so the claim is only partly supported [1]. The test shows presence, not amount.

    No starch: contradicted. Iodine turned blue-black, so starch is present [1]; “no starch” is not supported [1].

  3. Homework · originalTwo more samples were tested.

    SampleIodineBenedict’s (heated)BiuretEthanol, then water
    JOrange-brownGreenPurpleCloudy
    KOrange-brownBlueBlueClear
    • (a) Deduce the nutrients in J. Give the evidence for each. [3]
    • (b) A pupil says K “contains no food molecules at all”. Is this justified? Explain. [2]

    (a) J contains a small amount of reducing sugar: Benedict’s green [1]; protein: Biuret purple [1]; lipid: cloudy emulsion [1]. No starch: iodine orange-brown.

    (b) Not justified. No starch, reducing sugar, protein or lipid was detected [1]. K could still contain a non-reducing sugar such as sucrose, which Benedict’s does not detect [1].

Part C · Six-mark methodsmodel, then independent

  1. Lesson · screen 16Describe how a student could test a sample of cake for glucose and starch. [6]

    This was modelled in the lesson. Cover the lesson and write it from memory, one mark-earning step per line.

    Modelled answer. Grind the cake with distilled water in a mortar and pestle. Decant (or filter) the liquid. Put equal volumes in two test tubes. Add an equal volume of Benedict’s and heat in a water bath at about 80 °C for 5 minutes: a brick-red precipitate shows glucose. Add a few drops of iodine: blue-black shows starch. Test distilled water the same way as a control.

    Indicative content, 1 mark each, maximum 6:

    • Grind with water [1]; decant or filter [1]
    • Benedict’s, equal volume [1]
    • Water bath 70–100 °C [1]
    • Brick-red precipitate / the colour sequence [1]
    • Iodine [1]; blue-black [1]

    Do not accept: “turns red” alone; Biuret or ethanol tests (not asked for).

  2. Lesson · screen 17A sports drink label claims it contains glucose and protein but no fat. Describe how to test each part of this claim, including results that would support it. [6]

    Work alone, in silence. One mark-earning step per line.

    Glucose: add an equal volume of Benedict’s [1]. Heat in a water bath at about 80 °C for 5 minutes [1]. A brick-red precipitate supports glucose [1].

    Protein: add Biuret reagent to a fresh sample and mix; no heating [1]. Purple supports protein [1].

    No fat: shake a sample with ethanol, then pour into water [1]. Staying clear supports “no fat” [1].

    Also credit: comparing every test with a water control [1]. Maximum 6. Best: “brick-red”, not “red”.

  3. Homework · originalDescribe how a student could show that a sample of bread contains starch and protein. Include a control. [6]

    Indicative content, 1 mark each, maximum 6:

    • Grind the bread with a little distilled water in a mortar and pestle [1]
    • Decant or filter off the liquid [1]
    • Add a few drops of iodine solution to the liquid, or straight onto the bread [1]
    • Blue-black shows starch [1]
    • Add Biuret reagent to the liquid and mix, with no heating (or sodium hydroxide solution, then a few drops of dilute copper sulfate solution) [1]
    • Purple (lilac) shows protein [1]
    • Test distilled water the same way: it stays orange-brown with iodine and blue with Biuret [1]

    Do not accept: heating either test; Benedict’s or ethanol tests (not asked for).

Part D · Semi-quantitative resultsstretch · grade 8–9

  1. Lesson · screen 18Four samples were heated with Benedict’s: A brick-red, B green, C blue, D orange.

    • (a) Rank A–D, most glucose first. [1]
    • (b) Explain why the test is semi-quantitative. [2]
    • (c) Suggest a way to measure the glucose. [1]

    (a) A, D, B, C [1].

    (b) The colour shows the relative amount of reducing sugar, so samples can be ranked [1], but it gives no actual concentration [1].

    (c) Use a colorimeter; or filter, dry and weigh the precipitate; or time the first colour change [1].

  2. Homework · originalA student heated four fruit drinks with Benedict’s in a water bath at 80 °C and timed how long each took to first turn green.

    DrinkTime to first turn green / s
    F45
    G120
    HNo change after 300
    I70
    • (a) Rank the drinks from most to least reducing sugar. [1]
    • (b) Explain why a shorter time shows more reducing sugar. [2]
    • (c) Give two variables the student must keep the same. [2]
    • (d) The label on drink H says it contains sugar. Suggest why H did not change colour. [1]

    (a) F, I, G, H [1].

    (b) More reducing sugar forms precipitate faster [1], so the first colour change is reached sooner [1].

    (c) Any two, 1 mark each: volume of drink; volume (and concentration) of Benedict’s; water-bath temperature; the same end-point colour (first green).

    (d) No reducing sugar was detected in H, but it could contain a non-reducing sugar such as sucrose, which Benedict’s does not detect [1].

Review your responsebefore you check

  • Does every method line start with an action and include a detail: a volume, a temperature or a time?
  • Did you give the colour before and after for each result?
  • Did you write “brick-red precipitate”, not just “red”?
  • Did you compare each result with a control?
  • When a test was negative, did you write “not detected” rather than “none present”?
