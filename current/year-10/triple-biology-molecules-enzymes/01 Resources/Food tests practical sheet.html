  2. Which test is the only one that needs heating? State how it is heated. [2]

    The Benedict’s test for glucose (reducing sugar) [1]. Heat in a water bath at about 80 °C for about 5 minutes [1]. Accept 70–100 °C. Do not accept a Bunsen flame.

  1. Why is the ethanol test a teacher demonstration today, and why must there be no flames? [2]

    Ethanol is highly flammable [1]. Its vapour could catch fire from a flame, so there are no naked flames, the water bath is not heated with a Bunsen, and ethanol is never heated [1].

Part D · Analyse the evidenceafter the virtual practical · stretch in Q3 to Q6

  1. Why was the distilled-water tube tested with every reagent? [2]

    The water tube contains no nutrient, so it shows the negative result: the reagent’s own colour [1]. A sample that looks different from the water tube has changed, so the change is caused by a nutrient in the sample [1].

  2. The known glucose solution turned brick-red. Why does this help you trust sample W’s blue Benedict’s result? [2]

    It is a positive control: it shows the Benedict’s reagent and the heating worked [1]. So W staying blue is a true negative (no reducing sugar in W), not a failed test [1].

  3. A pupil concludes that sample W “contains no nutrients”. Your teacher then tells you that W is a sugar solution.

    • (a) Explain why W stayed blue with Benedict’s. [2]
    • (b) Rewrite the pupil’s conclusion so that it is valid. [2]

    (a) Benedict’s detects only reducing sugars, such as glucose and maltose [1]. W contains a non-reducing sugar, such as sucrose, so no precipitate forms [1].

    (b) “No starch, reducing sugar or protein was detected in W” [1]; it could still contain a non-reducing sugar, and lipid was not tested [1].

    Do not accept: “W has no sugar”.

  4. In a real lab, two pairs tested sample X with Benedict’s. One recorded orange; the other recorded green. Suggest two reasons for the difference. [2]

    Any two, 1 mark each: different volumes of sample or Benedict’s; a different heating time; a different water-bath temperature; colour judged by eye is subjective (lighting, viewing against the control); sample not mixed before it was taken.

  5. Describe one way to make the Benedict’s test quantitative. [2]

    Any one method, fully described:

    • Use a colorimeter to measure light absorbed or transmitted [1]; compare with a calibration curve made from known glucose concentrations [1].
    • Filter, dry and weigh the precipitate [1]: a greater mass means more reducing sugar [1].
    • Time the first colour change [1], keeping volumes and temperature the same [1].

  6. Explain why the ethanol goes cloudy when it is poured into water, if the sample contains lipid. [3]

    Lipid dissolves in ethanol [1]. Lipid is insoluble in water, so in water it comes out of solution as tiny droplets: an emulsion [1]. The droplets scatter light, so the mixture looks cloudy white [1].
