Enzymes, temperature and pH

Year 10 Triple Biology · Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Biology (Modular) 4XBI1 · 2.10–2.14B, with 2.29 introduced

Big question: how does a protein’s shape control the speed of a reaction? Read one part at a time. Answer the Part D questions in your book, then check them with Show / hide answers. All diagrams are original and schematic; all data are illustrative.

Part A · How enzymes work 2.10 · 2.29 introduced

Enzymes are biological catalysts

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up or permanently changed. Enzymes are biological catalysts: proteins made by cells. They speed up metabolic reactions, the chemical reactions in an organism. Some reactions break large molecules down, such as starch → maltose in digestion. Others build large molecules up, such as glucose → glycogen in the liver and muscles. Enzymes make these reactions fast enough at body temperature, about 37 °C.

The active site: lock and key

Each enzyme has an active site: a region with a specific shape that is complementary to its substrate. Complementary means the shapes fit together, like a key and its lock. It does not mean “the same shape”.

  1. The substrate collides with the active site.
  2. It binds, forming an enzyme–substrate complex.
  3. The reaction happens and the products are released.
  4. The enzyme is unchanged, so it binds another substrate. One enzyme molecule is reused many times.

Each enzyme is specific

An enzyme is specific: only a substrate with a complementary shape fits its active site. A molecule with a different shape cannot bind, so no complex forms and it is not broken down. That is why a cell makes thousands of different enzymes. The shape of the active site comes from the order of amino acids, which decides how the protein chain folds.

Part B · Temperature and pH 2.11 · 2.13

Temperature

  • Rising part: as the temperature rises, molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster. Enzyme and substrate collide more often, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form each second. The rate rises.
  • Peak: the optimum temperature gives the highest rate. It is about 40 °C for the amylase data here and about 37 °C for many human enzymes. Enzymes from other organisms have different optima.
  • Falling part: above the optimum, the enzyme vibrates more and the bonds holding its shape break. The active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits. Fewer complexes form and the rate falls. The enzyme is denatured. This is permanent.

Cold is not the same as hot

At low temperature an enzyme is slow because collisions are less frequent. It is not denatured: warm it up and its activity returns. Above the optimum, denaturation is permanent: cooling does not restore the active site.

Grade 8–9: a solution holds millions of enzyme molecules. Above the optimum an increasing proportion of them is denatured, so the rate falls progressively, not all at once.

pH

Each enzyme has an optimum pH, where it works fastest. Away from the optimum, the bonds holding the enzyme’s shape are altered, so the active site changes shape. The substrate fits less well, fewer complexes form and the rate falls. Extreme pH denatures the enzyme.

  • Pepsin, a protease in the stomach: about pH 2.
  • Salivary amylase: about pH 7.
  • Trypsin, a protease in the small intestine: about pH 8.

In a pH investigation, buffer solutions keep the pH constant.

Words examiners do not credit

  • “The enzyme is killed” or “dies”: enzymes are proteins, not living things.
  • “Denatured by cold”: cold only slows enzymes.
  • “The active site is destroyed”: say it changes shape.
  • “The substrate is denatured”: it is the enzyme that is denatured.
  • “The same shape”: say complementary.

Part C · Core Practical 2 2.12 · 2.14B

The method

  1. Wear eye protection. Put one drop of iodine solution in each well of a spotting tile.
  2. Warm 2 cm³ starch solution and 2 cm³ amylase solution in separate tubes in a water bath for 5 minutes, so both reach the chosen temperature before mixing.
  3. Mix them and start the stopwatch at once.
  4. Every 30 s, put one drop of the mixture into a fresh iodine well.
  5. End-point: the first well where the iodine stays orange-brown. All the starch has been broken down.
  6. Repeat at 10–60 °C, three times at each temperature. Calculate a mean time and rate = 1 ÷ mean time.

Control: boiled (denatured) amylase or water instead of amylase. The iodine stays blue-black, which shows that active amylase causes the breakdown. pH version (2.14B): add 1 cm³ of buffer solution to the starch, keep one temperature, and change the pH.

Safety: wear eye protection; iodine is an irritant; avoid skin contact with amylase, which can cause an allergic reaction; take care with hot water.

Rate and precision

Rate = 1 ÷ time, unit s⁻¹. For example, 1 ÷ 90 s = 0.011 s⁻¹. A shorter time means a faster rate.

  • Precision: sampling every 30 s means the starch may have run out up to 30 s before the recorded end-point. Known only to 30 s: a third of 90 s. Sample more often or use a colorimeter.
  • Repeats show whether results are repeatable and help you spot anomalies. They do not remove the 30 s gap, so they do not make a single result “accurate”.
  • Optimum: with 10 °C steps, the true optimum could lie anywhere between 30 °C and 50 °C. Test smaller steps near the peak.

  2. Describe what happens at the active site during an enzyme-catalysed reaction. [3]

    • An enzyme–substrate complex forms [1]
    • Products are released and the enzyme is unchanged, so it can be reused [1]
  3. Starch is digested to glucose in two steps. Name the enzyme for each step and the molecule made in the middle. [2]
    • Amylase: starch → maltose [1]

  4. Compare what happens to amylase at 5 °C and at 70 °C. [4]
    • 5 °C: less kinetic energy, fewer collisions, so a slow rate [1]
    • 5 °C: not denatured; activity returns when warmed [1]
    • 70 °C: bonds break and the active site changes shape: denatured [1]

  5. Explain why a buffer solution is used in a pH investigation. [2]
    • It keeps the pH constant during the reaction [1]
    • So any change in rate is caused by the chosen pH, not by a drifting pH [1]

    1 ÷ 150 = 0.0067 s⁻¹ [1].

Key words Glossary

Catalyst
Speeds up a reaction without being used up or permanently changed.
Enzyme
A protein made by cells that acts as a biological catalyst.
Metabolic reaction
A chemical reaction in an organism: breakdown (digestion) or build-up (glucose → glycogen).
Substrate
The molecule an enzyme acts on, for example starch for amylase.
Active site
The region of an enzyme with a specific shape, complementary to its substrate.
Complementary
Shapes that fit together, like a key and its lock. Not the same shape.
Enzyme–substrate complex
Forms when the substrate binds in the active site.
Specific
Only a substrate with a complementary shape fits the active site.
Optimum
The temperature or pH at which an enzyme works fastest.
Kinetic energy
Energy of movement. Warmer molecules move faster and collide more often.
Denatured
Bonds holding the shape break; the active site changes shape. Permanent. Never “killed”.
Buffer solution
Keeps the pH constant during an investigation.
Amylase · maltase
Amylase: starch → maltose. Maltase: maltose → glucose.
Protease · lipase
Protease: protein → amino acids. Lipase: lipids → fatty acids and glycerol.
End-point
The first sample where iodine stays orange-brown: all the starch is gone.
Rate
How fast a reaction happens. Here rate = 1 ÷ time, unit s⁻¹.
Precision
How finely a value is measured. Sampling every 30 s gives times only to 30 s.
