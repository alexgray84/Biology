Your question

How does temperature affect the activity of amylase? Amylase breaks down starch into maltose. The iodine test shows when all the starch has gone.

C Temperature: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C, set with water baths and checked with a thermometer.

O Amylase from the same stock solution for every trial.

R Three trials at each temperature; calculate a mean time, leaving out anomalies.

M Time in seconds until the iodine stays orange-brown (the end-point). Rate = 1 ÷ time, in s⁻¹.

M Every 30 s, one drop of the mixture into a fresh well of iodine.

S pH: add the same buffer solution to every tube, because pH also changes the rate.

S 2 cm³ starch and 2 cm³ amylase, same concentrations every time, because more substrate or enzyme changes the rate.

  1. Name the independent variable and the dependent variable in this investigation. [2]
    • Independent: temperature [1]
    • Dependent: time until the iodine stays orange-brown / time for the starch to be broken down (accept rate) [1]
  2. Explain why the starch and the amylase are warmed in separate tubes for 5 minutes before they are mixed. [2]
    • So both solutions reach the chosen temperature [1]
    • before the reaction starts, so all of the reaction happens at that temperature / no starch is digested at the wrong temperature [1]
  3. Describe a control for this investigation and explain what it shows. [2]
    • Boiled (then cooled) amylase, or water, instead of amylase [1]
    • The iodine stays blue-black, so it is the active amylase that breaks down the starch, not time or the iodine [1]

pH version (2.14B)

Use the same method, but change the pH. Add 1 cm³ of a buffer solution (for example pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9) to the starch before warming. Keep the water bath at one temperature, for example 37 °C. The buffer keeps the pH constant during the reaction.

  1. State what each iodine colour shows about the mixture. [2]
    • Blue-black: starch is still present [1]
    • Stays orange-brown: no starch left; all of it has been broken down (the end-point) [1]
  2. Explain why each drop of mixture goes into a fresh well of iodine. [1]

    So each well shows whether starch is present at that time only; colour from an earlier drop cannot hide the result [1].

  3. At 60 °C the result is recorded as “>600 s”, not as a number. Suggest why. [1]

    No end-point was reached in the 600 s tested; starch was still present, so the true time is unknown (the amylase is denatured) [1].

Part D · Evaluate Grade 8–9 · 16 marks

  1. Explain why no end-point was reached at 60 °C. [3]
    • The amylase is denatured [1]
    • Bonds holding its shape break, so the active site changes shape and starch no longer fits / is not complementary [1]
    • No enzyme–substrate complexes form, so the starch is not broken down and the iodine stays blue-black [1]
    • Do not accept “the enzyme is killed” or “the starch is denatured”.
  2. Explain how sampling every 30 s limits the precision of the 40 °C end-point time. [2]
    • The starch could have run out at any time after the 60 s sample, up to 30 s before the 90 s sample [1]
    • So the time is known only to the nearest 30 s, a large interval compared with 90 s [1]
  3. A student says: “Repeating each temperature three times makes our 30 s end-point times accurate.” Evaluate this claim. [3]
    • Repeats let you check repeatability, spot anomalies and calculate a mean [1]
    • But every repeat has the same 30 s sampling gap, so the mean still cannot place the end-point more precisely than 30 s [1]
    • Better: sample every 10 s, or use a colorimeter; so the claim is wrong [1]
  4. The fastest rate was at 40 °C. Explain why you cannot say the optimum is exactly 40 °C, and describe how to find it more precisely. [3]
    • Only 10 °C intervals were tested [1]
    • The true optimum could lie anywhere between 30 °C and 50 °C [1]
    • Test smaller intervals around the peak, for example every 2 °C from 30 °C to 50 °C, with three repeats each [1]
  5. At 50 °C the molecules have more kinetic energy than at 40 °C, yet the rate is lower. Explain why. [3]
    • At 50 °C molecules move faster, so there would be more collisions [1]
    • But an increasing proportion of amylase molecules is denatured: their active sites change shape [1]
    • So fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form per second; this outweighs the extra collisions [1]
  6. Give one limitation of judging the end-point by eye, and one way to reduce it. [2]
    • The colour change is gradual, so judging “orange-brown” is subjective [1]
    • Compare each well with a reference well of iodine and water; or use a colorimeter [1]
