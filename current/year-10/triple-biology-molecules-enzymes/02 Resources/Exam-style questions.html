Part A · How enzymes work 2.10 · 2.29 · 14 marks

  1. Lesson Define the term active site. [1]

    The region of an enzyme with a specific shape, complementary to its substrate, where the substrate binds [1].

  2. Lesson Why can’t a protease digest starch?
    1. Starch is too large for any enzyme to digest.
    2. Its active site is not complementary to starch, so no complex forms.
    3. Starch denatures the protease.
    4. The protease is used up digesting protein.
    1. Choose the correct option. [1]
    2. Explain why option D is wrong. [1]
    • (a) B [1]
    • (b) The protease is a catalyst, so it is not used up; it is unchanged and reused [1]
    • Option A: amylase digests starch, so size is not the reason. Option C: heat or extreme pH denature enzymes, not starch.
  3. Lesson Complete the table. [4]
    EnzymeSubstrateProduct(s)
    Amylase
    Maltase
    Protease
    Lipase
    • Amylase: starch → maltose [1]
    • Maltase: maltose → glucose [1]
    • Protease: protein → amino acids [1]
    • Lipase: lipids (fats and oils) → fatty acids and glycerol; both products needed [1]
    • Do not accept glucose as the product of amylase.
  4. Extra Enzymes speed up metabolic reactions.
    1. Give one metabolic reaction that builds a large molecule and one that breaks a large molecule down. [2]
    2. Explain why a cell needs only a small amount of each enzyme. [2]
    • (a) Build-up: glucose → glycogen (or glucose → starch) [1]. Breakdown: starch → maltose (or protein → amino acids; lipids → fatty acids and glycerol) [1]
    • (b) An enzyme is a catalyst: it is not used up or permanently changed [1]. The products are released, so the active site binds another substrate; each molecule is reused many times [1]
  5. Extra Explain how the order of amino acids in an enzyme decides which substrate it can act on. [3]
    • The order of amino acids decides how the chain folds [1]
    • Folding gives the active site its specific shape [1]
    • Only a substrate with a complementary shape can bind and form an enzyme–substrate complex [1]

Part B · Temperature and pH 2.11 · 2.13 · 20 marks

  1. Lesson Amylase is warmed from 40 °C to 60 °C. The rate falls to almost zero. Which is the best explanation? [1]
    1. The amylase is killed by the heat.
    2. The molecules move too fast to collide.
    3. Bonds break, so the active site changes shape and starch no longer fits.
    4. The starch is denatured.
    • C [1]
    • A: enzymes are not alive, so they cannot be killed. B: faster molecules collide more often, not less. D: the enzyme is denatured, not the starch.
  2. Extra Use Figure 1.
    1. Describe how the rate changes between 10 °C and 65 °C. [2]
    2. Explain the change in rate between 10 °C and 40 °C. [2]
    3. Explain the change in rate above 40 °C. [3]
    • (a) The rate increases to a peak (the optimum) at about 40 °C [1], then decreases steeply to zero by about 60–65 °C [1]
    • (b) Molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster [1], so enzyme and substrate collide more often and more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second [1]
    • (c) Bonds holding the enzyme’s shape break [1]; the active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits [1]; the enzyme is denatured and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form [1]
    • Do not accept “killed”, “dies” or “the active site is destroyed”.
  3. Lesson Boiled amylase is cooled to 37 °C. Explain why it still does not digest starch. [2]
    • Boiling denatured the amylase: its active site changed shape [1]
    • The change is permanent, so cooling does not restore the shape [1]
  4. Lesson Explain why pepsin works in the stomach but not in the small intestine. [2]
    • Pepsin’s optimum is about pH 2, which matches the acidic stomach [1]
    • The small intestine is slightly alkaline (about pH 8), so pepsin’s active site changes shape and protein no longer fits [1]
  1. Extra Use Figure 2.
    1. Give the optimum pH of trypsin. [1]
    2. Explain why trypsin has almost no activity at pH 2. [2]
    3. A student says: “Every enzyme works best at pH 7.” Use Figure 2 to evaluate this claim. [2]
    • (a) About pH 8 [1]
    • (b) pH 2 is far from its optimum, so the bonds holding its shape are altered and the active site changes shape / it is denatured [1]; the substrate no longer fits, so few or no enzyme–substrate complexes form [1]
    • (c) The claim is wrong: pepsin’s optimum is about pH 2 [1] and trypsin’s about pH 8; each enzyme has its own optimum pH [1]
  2. Extra Hypothetical scenario. A shop in Dubai keeps fresh milk at 4 °C, not in a 35 °C summer storeroom. Explain why the milk stays fresh for longer, and why it must stay cold after it has been chilled. [3]
    • Spoilage is controlled by enzymes, in bacteria and in the milk [1]
    • At 4 °C molecules have less kinetic energy, so there are fewer collisions and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes: spoilage is slower [1]
    • Cold does not denature enzymes; if the milk warms up, their activity returns [1]

Part C · Calculations and data 2.12 · Core Practical 2 · 13 marks

  1. Lesson Amylase end-points: 90 s at 40 °C and 240 s at 20 °C. Samples were tested every 30 s.
    1. Calculate both rates (rate = 1 ÷ time), to 2 significant figures, with units. [2]
    2. Explain how 30 s sampling limits the precision of the 40 °C time. [2]
    • (a) 40 °C: 1 ÷ 90 = 0.011 s⁻¹ [1]. 20 °C: 1 ÷ 240 = 0.0042 s⁻¹ [1]. Accept 1.1 × 10⁻² and 4.2 × 10⁻³.
    • (b) The starch could have run out up to 30 s earlier, any time from 60 s to 90 s [1]; the time is known only to 30 s, which is large compared with 90 s [1]
    • Do not accept “repeats make it accurate”.

Part D · Planning an investigation 2.12 · 2.14B · 18 marks

  1. Lesson · worked in class Describe how you could investigate the effect of pH on the activity of amylase. [6]

    Write it from memory, then compare it with the worked example from the lesson.

    • Use buffer solutions to set a range of pH values, e.g. pH 4–9, 1 cm³ added to the starch [1]
    • Amylase from the same stock solution [1]
    • Every 30 s, test one drop of the mixture with iodine on a spotting tile [1]
    • Time until the iodine stays orange-brown; rate = 1 ÷ time [1]
    • Keep the temperature constant, e.g. a 37 °C water bath [1]
    • Repeat three times at each pH and calculate a mean [1]
    • Also credit: same volumes and concentrations of starch and amylase. Maximum 6 marks.
  2. Lesson · independent A cloudy suspension of casein (a milk protein) goes clear when a protease digests it. Describe how you could investigate the effect of temperature on this protease’s activity. [6]

    1 mark per point, any order, maximum 6:

    • At least five temperatures, set with water baths [1]
    • Warm the casein and the protease separately first [1]
    • Time until the mixture goes clear, e.g. a cross behind the tube becomes visible [1]
    • Same volume and concentration of casein [1]
    • Same volume of protease from the same source [1]
    • Same pH, using a buffer [1]
    • Three repeats at each temperature; calculate a mean [1]
    • Rate = 1 ÷ time [1]
    • Do not accept “the protease dies”.
  3. Extra A student investigated the effect of temperature on amylase. She:
    • mixed the starch and amylase at room temperature, then put the tube in the water bath
    • tested a drop of the mixture with iodine every 2 minutes
    • made a new amylase solution from powder for each temperature
    • did one trial at each temperature.

    Identify three weaknesses. For each, suggest an improvement and explain why it improves the investigation. [6]

    For each weakness: improvement [1] + reason [1]; maximum 6.

    • Warm the starch and amylase separately for 5 minutes before mixing [1], so the whole reaction happens at the chosen temperature [1]
    • Sample every 30 s or more often [1], so the end-point time is more precise [1]
    • Use one stock amylase solution [1], so the enzyme concentration is the same and only temperature changes [1]
    • Do three trials and calculate a mean [1], to check repeatability and spot anomalies [1]
    • Also credit: add a buffer so pH is controlled, because pH also changes the rate.
