The osmosis practical and CORMMSS planning

“Osmosis/diffusion practical and CORMMSS planning skills.”

Big ideaPotato cylinders gain mass in solutions more dilute than their cell sap and lose mass in more concentrated ones, because of the net movement of water by osmosis. Percentage change makes the cylinders comparable, and the concentration where the graph crosses 0 % matches the cells. CORMMSS plans the method.

AThe method and the calculation

% change in mass = (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100

Worked: 2.50 g → 2.80 g: (2.80 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = +12 % (gain). Keep the sign: + gained, − lost.

Knowledge organiser · Assessment point 3 · part 2 of 3

CReading the graph

Above 0 %: mass gained — net water into the cells (more dilute solution). Below 0 %: mass lost — net water out. At 0 %: no net movement.

DKey words and what not to confuse them with

Independent variableThe one you change: sucrose concentration.≠ the dependent variable
Dependent variableThe one you measure: % change in mass.≠ a control variable
Control variableKept the same so it cannot affect the result: time, volume, temperature.≠ the independent variable
Percentage change(final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. Negative = mass lost.≠ change in mass in grams
AnomalyA result that does not fit the pattern. Check it, then leave it out of the mean.≠ a result you don’t like
MeanAdd the repeats (without anomalies) and divide by how many there are.≠ the middle value

FMisconception busters

  • ✗ “% change = final − initial.”✓ Divide the change by the initial mass, × 100, and keep the sign.
  • ✗ “Repeats make the results accurate.”✓ Repeats show how repeatable results are and help you spot anomalies.
  • ✗ “0 % change means no water moved.”✓ Water still crosses both ways. There is no net movement.
  • ✗ “Weigh them straight out of the tube.”✓ Blot gently, the same way each time: surface liquid adds to the mass.

GIn the UAE

Maleh (salted fish): fish is packed in salt to preserve it. The salt is far more concentrated than the fluid in the fish’s cells, so there is a net movement of water out of them by osmosis — and microbes on the fish lose water too.

★ Going further: the 0 % concentration has the same water potential as the cell sap — an estimate, not the sap’s exact sucrose content.

★Worked example watch how the marks are earned

Question · 5 marks

A student repeats the 0.4 mol dm⁻³ test three times. Masses (initial → final): repeat 1 2.50 → 2.40 g; repeat 2 2.00 → 1.90 g; repeat 3 2.40 → 2.10 g. (i) Calculate the % change for repeat 3. (ii) Calculate the mean % change and explain how you dealt with repeat 3.

Before you look: Change first (keep the sign), then ÷ initial × 100. Compare the three percentages before you average.

Model answer, step by step

  1. Repeat 3: change = 2.10 − 2.40 = −0.30 g.Mark 1: final − initial, keeping the minus sign.
  2. % change = −0.30 ÷ 2.40 × 100 = −12.5 %.Mark 2: divide by the initial mass.
  3. Repeats 1 and 2 are −4.0 % and −5.0 %, so −12.5 % is an anomaly.Mark 3: compare all three; repeat 3 does not fit.
  4. Check it (was it blotted or timed differently?), then leave it out of the mean.Mark 4: keep, check, then exclude — with a reason.
  5. Mean = (−4.0 + −5.0) ÷ 2 = −4.5 %.Mark 5: average only the values that fit.

Why this gets full marks: The sign is kept at every step, the anomaly is spotted by comparing, and the mean uses only the repeats that agree.

6Exam technique for this point

  • Plan = CORMMSSWrite C O R M M S S down the margin before a “Describe how…” question. One sentence each = six marks.
  • What and how“Measure the mass” is half a line. Say how: blot, weigh to 0.01 g, before and after 30 minutes.
  • Keep the signA loss is negative: −10.0 %. A missing minus sign usually costs a mark.
  • Read graphs from the lineFind where the line (not a point) crosses 0 %, then read down. Give a unit.
  • Improve the right thingTo pin down the crossing, add concentrations near it. Repeats improve the mean, not the gaps.
