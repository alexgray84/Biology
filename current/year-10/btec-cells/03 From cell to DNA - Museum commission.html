The commission

Work in a team of two or three

How do scientists move from seeing cells to studying their DNA?

Make a small, layered exhibit a visitor can explore in under two minutes. Use a real cell micrograph, a model you build, and an audio guide or short video.

Your exhibit must correct one common mistake: a classroom light microscope cannot show an individual DNA double helix.

What your team will make

  • A physical fold-out or layered display that moves from cell → nucleus → chromosome → DNA → gene.
  • One visitor guide: choose audio or video below.
  • A final space left blank for next week’s microscopy and DNA-extraction observations.

What each person keeps: your own short scientific script or explanation, your source notes, and one change you made after visitor feedback. Write these in your book or a personal document.

Choose the visitor guide

One format per team

Short video

Film your model, not people. In 60–90 seconds, guide a visitor from a real cell image to an explained DNA model. Add a spoken explanation or readable captions.

Audio guide

Record up to two minutes of visitor instructions. Pair the audio with your layered physical display so listeners can point to each structure as you explain it.

Design is part of the science.

Use colour, movement, sound and layout to make the relationship easier to understand. Do not let an attractive model make a false claim. Label drawings and 3D shapes as models; label microscope photographs as observations.

Start with something real

Look before you design

Examine the evidence

In your book, list two structures you can see in these images. Then name one structure you know about but cannot identify in them. Do not label a model as if it were a photograph.

Keep the biology accurate

Use this to test your draft
  • Show a real plant or animal cell image and describe visible structures and their functions.
  • Make the pathway cell → nucleus → chromosome → DNA → gene clear.
  • Show DNA as a double helix with correct base pairing: A with T; C with G.
  • Explain that genes are sections of DNA carrying instructions for making proteins or functional RNA.
  • Say that the white material in a simple extraction contains many DNA molecules together—not one visible helix.
  • Say what is an observation, what is a model, and what cannot be concluded from the image or extraction alone.

Two lessons. One exhibit.

Keep your own work traceable

Lesson 1
Investigate

Today, agree the science and design the visitor’s journey. You do not need to record yet.

  1. 1
    Inspect the micrographs. Record two visible observations and one limit in your book.
    7 min
  2. 2
    Research the pathway. Use the source links below. Write a one-sentence meaning for nucleus, chromosome, DNA and gene in your own words.
    10 min
  3. 3
    Plan the visitor route. Sketch the layers of your exhibit. Decide where your real image, model and correction of the DNA misconception will appear.
    10 min
  4. 4
    Build the first version. Make the cell and inner layers. Each team member drafts the words for one part of the visitor guide.
    18 min
  5. 5
    Check the science. Point to one observation, one model and one claim you still need to verify.
    5 min

Lesson 2
Communicate

Today, make the explanation clear enough for a visitor who has never studied DNA.

  1. 1
    Recall without notes. Explain the pathway cell → DNA to a partner. Repair any missing link in your own script.
    5 min
  2. 2
    Finish the exhibit. Label the real image, the model and next week’s empty practical-evidence space.
    12 min
  3. 3
    Record the guide. Make your audio or video. Every team member must contribute an accurate scientific explanation.
    20 min
  4. 4
    Test it with visitors. Ask another team what they think a microscope can and cannot show. Note where your exhibit confused them.
    8 min
  5. 5
    Revise and record. Make one improvement. In your book, state what you changed and why.
    5 min

Next week, test your story.

Leave a panel titled “Predicted / Observed / Explained”. After the booked microscopy and DNA-extraction practicals, add what your group actually observed. If the result differs from the prediction, explain the difference. Do not invent results today.
