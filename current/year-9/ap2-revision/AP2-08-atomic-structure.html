Atomic structure: atomic and mass numbers

“Describe atomic structure; use atomic and mass numbers.”

Big ideaAn atom has a tiny nucleus of protons (+1, mass 1) and neutrons (0, mass 1), with electrons (−1, mass about 1/1840) outside it. Atomic number = protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.

AInside an atom

ParticleWhereRelative chargeRelative mass
Protonin the nucleus+11
Neutronin the nucleus01
Electronoutside the nucleus−1very small (about 1/1840)

Lithium-7: 3 protons, 4 neutrons, 3 electrons. Protons and neutrons each have a relative mass of 1; an electron’s is tiny, so almost all the mass is in the nucleus — that is why the mass number counts only protons + neutrons.

BAtomic number and mass number

  1. 1Check: protons + neutrons must add up to the mass number (11 + 12 = 23).
  2. 2Use the whole-number mass number you are given, not the decimal on a periodic table.

Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 8 · part 2 of 3

CIsotopes

Isotopes = same element (same protons), different numbers of neutrons. Test: compare protons first, then neutrons. Carbon-14 (6 p) and nitrogen-14 (7 p) share a mass number but are not isotopes. Isotope does not mean radioactive: carbon-12 and carbon-13 are both stable.

DKey words and what not to confuse them with

ProtonParticle in the nucleus: relative charge +1, relative mass 1. The number of protons decides the element.≠ neutron (no charge)
NeutronParticle in the nucleus: no charge (0), relative mass 1.≠ electron — neutrons do not set the electron number
ElectronParticle outside the nucleus: relative charge −1, relative mass about 1/1840 (very small).≠ proton (+1, mass 1)
Atomic number (Z)The number of protons in an atom. Elements are ordered by it.≠ mass number
Mass number (A)The number of protons + neutrons in one atom. Neutrons = A − Z.≠ the decimal relative atomic mass on a periodic table
IsotopesAtoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.≠ atoms that just have the same mass number

FMisconception busters

  • ✗ “The number of electrons equals the number of neutrons.”✓ Electrons equal protons in a neutral atom. Neutrons have no charge, so they do not need cancelling.
  • ✗ “Mass number = number of protons” or “it counts every particle”.✓ Mass number counts protons + neutrons — the particles with relative mass 1. Electrons are so light (about 1/1840) that they are not counted.
  • ✗ “Neutral means the atom has no charged particles.”✓ A neutral atom is full of charged particles: equal + and − charges cancel to zero overall.
  • ✗ “Same mass number means they are isotopes.”✓ Isotopes need the same protons. Argon-40 (18 p) and calcium-40 (20 p) are different elements.

GIn the UAE

Barakah, Al Dhafra: the UAE’s nuclear power plant uses uranium fuel. Uranium-235 and uranium-238 are isotopes: both have 92 protons (atomic number 92), but 235 − 92 = 143 and 238 − 92 = 146 neutrons.

★ Going further (GCSE): the decimal on a periodic table is the relative atomic mass, a mean of the isotopes. Chlorine is 75 % chlorine-35 and 25 % chlorine-37: (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = 35.5.

Mark scheme · 4 marks

  1. 1
    Both have atomic number 1, so each has 1 protonThe atomic number gives the proton number.
  2. 1
    Same number of protons means the same element: hydrogenProton number decides the element.
  3. 1
    Hydrogen-1 has 1 − 1 = 0 neutrons; hydrogen-2 has 2 − 1 = 1 neutronNeutrons = mass number − atomic number, with working.
  4. 1
    Same protons but different numbers of neutrons, so they are isotopesUses both conditions of the definition.

Accept: “Same atomic number” for mark 1. Hydrogen-2 may be called deuterium. Mention that both have 1 electron when neutral is correct but not needed.

Do not accept: “They have different masses, so they are isotopes” on its own. “Different numbers of electrons.” “Different protons.”

★ Gold extension answer: Carbon-14 (6 p, 8 n) and nitrogen-14 (7 p, 7 n) both have mass number 14, but different proton numbers, so they are different elements — not isotopes.

Examiner’s eye — three answers to part (c)
1 / 4
They are isotopes because hydrogen-2 is heavier than hydrogen-1.

Only the idea of different mass. No proton numbers, no neutron working, and “heavier” alone is not the definition. Mark 4 is not given.

2 / 4
They both have 1 proton so they are both hydrogen. Hydrogen-2 has more neutrons.

Marks 1 and 2 ✓. The neutron numbers are not worked out, and the word isotope is not linked to the definition.

4 / 4
Both atoms have atomic number 1, so each has 1 proton. The same number of protons means they are the same element, hydrogen. Hydrogen-1 has 1 − 1 = 0 neutrons and hydrogen-2 has 2 − 1 = 1 neutron. They have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so they are isotopes.

6Exam technique for this point

  • Write the three linesFor any atom, write: p = Z · n = A − Z · e = p (neutral). Three lines, three marks — and it stops you mixing them up.
  • Big number on topIn a symbol box the bigger number is the mass number. It can never be smaller than the atomic number.
  • Protons firstFor “isotopes or not?”, compare the protons first. Different protons = different elements — stop there.
  • Labels earn the marksIn an atom diagram, art doesn’t score. Particles in the right place and a key with charges (+1, 0, −1) and masses (1, 1, very small) do.
  • Explain = numbers + becauseUse the actual numbers as evidence: “8 protons (+8) are cancelled by 8 electrons (−8), so…”.
