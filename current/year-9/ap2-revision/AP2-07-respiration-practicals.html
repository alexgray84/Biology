Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 7 · Biology

Respiration in practicals: results and conclusions

“Apply respiration ideas to practical results and conclusions.”

Big ideaIn a practical you can’t see respiration, so you measure what it makes or uses: the carbon dioxide from yeast (a balloon or gas syringe), or the breathing and heart rate that supply oxygen to muscles. A faster rate of carbon dioxide made means a faster rate of respiration. Every conclusion quotes data, then explains it with respiration and enzymes.

AThe yeast balloon investigation

BFrom readings to rate to explanation lab model data

Rate = increase ÷ time. At 25 °C: (12.5 − 8.0) ÷ 10 = 0.45 cm/min. At 40 °C: (17.0 − 8.0) ÷ 10 = 0.90 cm/min — the optimum.

Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 7 · part 2 of 3

CExercise investigations example data

During exercise: muscle cells respire faster → heart and breathing rate rise to deliver more oxygen and glucose and remove carbon dioxide (limewater turns cloudy faster). After: rates stay high to repay the oxygen debt (lactic acid). Fair test: same person, same exercise for the same time; take a mean of repeats.

DKey words and what not to confuse them with

RateHow fast something changes: increase in reading ÷ time, e.g. cm/min.≠ the total reading
EnzymeA protein that speeds up the reactions of respiration in cells.≠ a living thing
OptimumThe temperature at which the rate is fastest (about 40 °C in the lab model).≠ the highest temperature tested
DenatureThe active site changes shape so glucose no longer fits. It cannot be undone.≠ “the enzyme dies”
FermentationAnaerobic respiration in yeast: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide.≠ aerobic respiration
Recovery timeTime for breathing or heart rate to return to the resting value.≠ the time of the exercise

FMisconception busters

  • ✗ “The balloon fills with oxygen.”✓ Yeast releases carbon dioxide when it respires. That is what inflates the balloon.
  • ✗ “The hottest temperature tested is the optimum.”✓ Only if the rate has already peaked. The optimum could lie between two tested temperatures.
  • ✗ “At high temperature the yeast enzymes die.”✓ Enzymes are not alive. They denature: the active site changes shape.
  • ✗ “Breathing rate rises because you need more energy from the air.”✓ Muscles respire faster: more oxygen must be delivered and more carbon dioxide removed.

GIn the UAE

A UAE bakery proves dough at about 35–40 °C: fast carbon dioxide production, but safely below the temperature where the yeast enzymes start to denature and the dough stops rising.

★ Going further (GCSE): plot gas volume against time. The rate at any moment is the gradient of a tangent to the curve — it falls as the glucose is used up.

6Exam technique for this point

  • Rate = change ÷ timeSubtract the start reading first, then divide by the time. The unit has “per”: cm/min, cm³/min.
  • Describe before explainQuote the pattern with two numbers and units, then give the reason.
  • Rise and fallA temperature curve needs two explanations: collisions for the rise, denaturing for the fall.
  • Enzymes don’t dieWrite “denature: the active site changes shape”. “Killed” or “died” scores nothing.
  • Stay inside the dataOnly claim what you tested. A peak between two temperatures can’t be pinned down.
