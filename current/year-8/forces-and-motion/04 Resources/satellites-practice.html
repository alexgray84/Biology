Gravity and satellites — practice

Recall, explain and apply

Supplied classroom values · g (N/kg): Earth 10 · Moon 1.6 · Mars 3.7 · Jupiter 25 · Venus 8.9 · Mercury 3.7

How to check your work: attempt every question first. Then open Answers at the bottom. Self-assess in green pen, tick each mark, then swap books and peer-assess against the mark scheme. Finish by correcting one mistake on a fresh line.

Answer in full sentences. For explain questions, give a reason for each mark.

Part A — Recall and describe

1. Name the force that keeps a satellite in orbit around Earth.

2. State the direction of this force.

3. Give two uses of artificial satellites.

4. True or false: there is no gravity in space. If false, correct it.

Part B — Explain and apply

5. Explain why a satellite stays in orbit instead of flying off in a straight line. [3]

6. Explain why a satellite in a steady orbit does not need engines to keep moving. [2]

7. Gravity pulls a satellite towards Earth, yet it does not fall to the ground. Explain how this is possible. [3]

8. Describe one way a weather or communications satellite is useful. [2]

Part C — Apply independently

9. On a diagram of a satellite orbiting Earth, describe the two arrows you would draw and where they point. [2]

10. A student says astronauts on the Space Station float because there is no gravity. Explain why this is wrong. [3]

Answers — reveal to self-assess, then peer-assess
Marking (explain): 1 mark per correct linked point, up to the marks shown.
  1. 1) Gravity (Earth’s gravitational attraction).
  2. 2) Towards the centre of the Earth.
  3. 3) Any two: communications/TV, weather monitoring, GPS/navigation, Earth observation, scientific research.
  4. 4) False. Gravity acts everywhere in space; it is what holds satellites and moons in orbit.
  5. 5) The satellite has forward motion (1); Earth’s gravity pulls it towards the centre (1); this force constantly changes its direction into a curve, so it orbits rather than going straight (1).
  6. 6) There is no air resistance/friction in space to slow it (1), and gravity only changes its direction, not its speed, so no driving force is needed (1).
  7. 7) It is moving forwards fast (1); as gravity pulls it towards Earth it ‘falls’, but its forward motion carries it past the horizon (1); so it keeps falling around Earth without getting closer (1).
  8. 8) Any: a weather satellite images cloud/storms to forecast weather (1) which helps warn people / plan (1). (Accept comms/GPS equivalents.)
  9. 9) One arrow for the satellite’s motion (along the orbit, a tangent) (1); one arrow for gravity pointing towards Earth’s centre (1).
  10. 10) Gravity does act on the Station (only a little weaker than on the ground) (1); the astronauts and Station are both falling around Earth together / in free fall (1); so they feel weightless but are not beyond gravity (1).
