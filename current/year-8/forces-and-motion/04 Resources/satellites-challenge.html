Satellites — challenge

Stretch and exam practice

Supplied classroom values · g (N/kg): Earth 10 · Moon 1.6 · Mars 3.7 · Jupiter 25 · Venus 8.9 · Mercury 3.7

How to check your work: attempt every question first. Then open Answers at the bottom. Self-assess in green pen, tick each mark, then swap books and peer-assess against the mark scheme. Finish by correcting one mistake on a fresh line.

Explain fully — use because / which means / so.

1. Explain why a satellite in a higher orbit takes longer to go around the Earth than one in a low orbit. [3]

2. A geostationary satellite stays above the same point on Earth. Explain what this means and give one use. [2]

3. Predict what would happen to a satellite if it slowed down significantly. Explain. [2]

4. Exam practice — unpack it. Circle the command word and check the marks, then answer: “Explain how gravity keeps the Moon in orbit around the Earth.” [3]

Answers — reveal to self-assess, then peer-assess
Marking (explain): 1 mark per correct linked point, up to the marks shown.
  1. 1) A higher orbit is a longer path around Earth (1) and gravity is weaker further out, so the satellite moves more slowly (1); both mean it takes longer to complete one orbit (1).
  2. 2) It orbits at the same rate as the Earth spins, so it stays above the same point (1); use: TV/communications or continuous weather monitoring of one region (1).
  3. 3) With less forward motion, gravity would pull it into a lower orbit and it could spiral inwards / fall back towards Earth (1), because motion no longer balances the inward pull (1).
  4. 4) Explain [3]: Earth’s gravity acts on the Moon, pulling it towards Earth (1); the Moon also moves forwards/sideways (1); gravity changes the Moon’s direction so it follows a curved orbit rather than a straight line (1).
