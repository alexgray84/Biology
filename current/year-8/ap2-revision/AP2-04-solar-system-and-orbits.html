The Solar System and orbits

“Describe the Solar System and explain how gravity causes planets and satellites to stay in orbit.”

Big ideaThe Sun is a star at the centre of the Solar System; eight planets orbit it. Gravity acts everywhere in space. It pulls an orbiting object towards the centre of the larger body, which keeps changing its direction — so it moves in a curved orbit instead of a straight line.

AThe Solar System in order from the Sun

BHow gravity keeps an orbit motion along the path · gravity to the centre

Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 4 · part 2 of 3

CWhat orbits what

  1. 1Planets orbit the Sun — held by the Sun’s gravity.
  2. 2Moons orbit planets — our Moon is held by Earth’s gravity.
  3. 3Artificial satellites (weather, GPS, communications) orbit Earth.
  4. 4Bung on a string: string = gravity. Let go → straight line.
  5. 5The bigger body’s gravity always points to its centre.

DKey words and what not to confuse them with

OrbitThe curved path of one object around another, kept by gravity.≠ a straight path
GravityA non-contact force that pulls objects with mass towards each other. Acts everywhere in space.≠ “there is no gravity in space”
PlanetA large body that orbits a star (our Sun).≠ a moon (orbits a planet)
MoonA natural satellite that orbits a planet.≠ a planet
SatelliteAny object that orbits a larger body: the Moon (natural) or a weather satellite (artificial).≠ something floating with no force on it
StarA huge ball of gas that gives out its own light, e.g. the Sun.≠ a planet (reflects light)

FMisconception busters

  • ✗ “There is no gravity in space.”✓ Gravity acts everywhere in space — it is what keeps satellites and moons in orbit.
  • ✗ “Satellites float because they are weightless.”✓ A satellite is falling around Earth: gravity is pulling it the whole time.
  • ✗ “An engine pushes the satellite forward to keep it up.”✓ No forward force is needed. The only force is gravity, towards the centre.
  • ✗ “If gravity stopped, it would fall down.”✓ With no gravity, nothing changes its direction: it would go off in a straight line.

GIn the UAE

The Hope probe: the UAE’s Emirates Mars Mission has orbited Mars since 2021, studying its weather. Mars’s gravity pulls the probe towards the planet’s centre, changing its direction all the time — so it keeps circling Mars without any engine pushing it forward.

★ Going further: a satellite in a higher orbit travels more slowly and takes longer to go round, because gravity is weaker there and the path is longer.

Mark scheme · 4 marks

  1. 1
    There is gravity in space: Mars’s gravity acts on the probeDirectly corrects the claim.
  2. 1
    The probe is not floating freely — it is pulled towards Mars all the time
  3. 1
    Gravity (towards Mars’s centre) changes the probe’s directionDirection, not necessarily speed.
  4. 1

Accept: “Falling around Mars” for mark 4. “Pulled towards the centre of Mars” for mark 2.

Do not accept: “Gravity is weaker in space so it floats.” “The engine keeps it up.” “It is weightless so there is no force.”

★ Gold extension answer: Like a bung whirled on a string: the string’s inward pull keeps changing its direction, and if you let go it flies off in a straight line. Mars’s gravity plays the part of the string.

6Exam technique for this point

  • Planet orderWrite the eight planets in order the moment the paper starts: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.
  • Arrows that scoreStart every arrow on the object. Gravity points to the centre of the big body. Motion points along the path. Label both.
  • Say “direction”The key orbit idea is that gravity changes the direction of motion. Use the word.
  • Correct, then replaceWhen a question quotes a wrong idea, first say why it is wrong, then give the right science. Plan one point per mark.
  • No gravity → straight lineWithout gravity an orbiting object goes off in a straight line. It doesn’t stop, float or fall.
