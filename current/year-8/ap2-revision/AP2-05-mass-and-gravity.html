Mass, gravity and other planets

“Explain how mass affects gravitational force and compare gravity on different planets.”

Big ideaGravity is a pull between masses. The bigger the mass, the bigger the pull; the further away, the weaker it is. Each planet’s g (N/kg) is its pull on every kilogram, so the same object has a different weight on each planet — but the same mass.

AWhat sets the size of the pull?

Red arrows start on the 1 kg block and point to the planet’s centre. Arrow length = size of the pull (same scale in A, B and C).

Bg on four worlds

weight (N) = mass (kg) × g (N/kg)  ·  W = m × g

Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 5 · part 2 of 3

CMass and radius together

Surface gravity depends on the planet’s mass and its radius. Saturn has about 95 × Earth’s mass, but it is so wide that its surface g is only about 10 N/kg — close to Earth’s.

DKey words and what not to confuse them with

GravityA non-contact force that pulls masses towards each other.≠ magnetism (only some metals)
Gravitational forceThe size of the pull. Bigger with more mass; smaller further away.≠ mass (the amount of matter)
Gravitational field strength (g)The pull on each kilogram, in N/kg. Earth 10, Moon 1.6, Mars 3.7, Jupiter 25.≠ weight (the pull on the whole object)
MassThe amount of matter, in kg. The same on every planet.≠ weight
WeightThe gravitational force on an object, in N. W = m × g.≠ mass
RadiusDistance from a planet’s centre to its surface. Bigger radius → surface further from the centre.≠ mass

ECommand words

CompareSay how both are similar or different — mention both.Jupiter’s g is 25 N/kg, whereas Earth’s is 10 N/kg.
CalculateFormula, substitution, answer with unit.W = 15 × 3.7 = 55.5 N
ExplainSay what happens and why. Link with because / so.Mars has less mass, so it pulls less on each kg.
PredictSay what will happen, using the pattern.Twice the mass, same radius → about twice the g.
JudgeDecide, then back it up with evidence.Not always — Saturn is bigger but its g is close to Earth’s.

FMisconception busters

  • ✗ “There’s no gravity on the Moon — there’s no air.”✓ Gravity doesn’t need air. The Moon has mass, so it pulls: g = 1.6 N/kg, weaker but not zero.
  • ✗ “You have less mass on Mars.”✓ Same matter, same mass. Only the weight is smaller, because Mars’s g is smaller.
  • ✗ “Bigger planet always means stronger gravity.”✓ Surface pull depends on mass and radius. A huge planet can have a surface g close to Earth’s.
  • ✗ “Gravity pulls things down the page.”✓ Gravity pulls towards the centre of the planet, wherever you are on it.

GIn the UAE

Emirates Mars Mission: the UAE’s Hope probe has orbited Mars since February 2021, studying its atmosphere and weather. Mars has much less mass than Earth, so its g is only 3.7 N/kg: a 1 kg mass would weigh about 3.7 N there instead of 10 N.

★ Going further: Mars has about half Earth’s radius. Half the radius alone would make g about 4× bigger, but Mars has only about a tenth of Earth’s mass, which makes it about 10× smaller — overall g is about 0.4 of Earth’s (3.7 N/kg).

6Exam technique for this point

  • Mass of the PLANET, mass of the OBJECTTwo masses are in play. The planet’s mass sets g; the object’s mass stays the same. Say which one you mean.
  • Formula · substitution · unitEvery calculation: W = m × g, then the numbers, then the answer in N. Each step can earn a mark.
  • Compare = both numbers“Jupiter’s g is 25 N/kg, whereas Earth’s is 10 N/kg.” Quote both values, not just one.
  • Never “no gravity”The Moon and Mars have weaker gravity, not zero. Write “weaker pull” or quote g.
  • “Always” needs one counterexampleTo judge an “always” claim, find one case that breaks it (Saturn) and give the reason.
