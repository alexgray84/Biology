Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 2 · Light and space

Refraction

“Explain refraction and describe how light changes direction when it passes between different materials.”

Big ideaRefraction is light changing direction at the boundary between two transparent materials. Going from air into glass, a ray bends towards the normal; coming out into air it bends away. A ray travelling along the normal does not change direction. It happens because light changes speed: it is slower in glass and water than in air.

AA ray through a glass block towards in, away out

BThe glass block practical ray box + rectangular block

  1. 1Draw round the block. Draw a normal at the entry point. Replace the block.
  2. 2Aim a narrow ray at the entry point. Mark two crosses on the incident ray and two on the emergent ray.
  3. 3Switch off. Remove the block. Join the crosses with a ruler; join the path through the block.
  4. 4Draw the exit normal. Measure angles from the normal with a protractor. Repeat for 0°, 20°, 40°, 60°.
  5. 5Safety: beam below eye level, never look into it; switch off between readings; hold the cool base.

Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 2 · part 2 of 3

CThree boundary rules — and why

Smaller angle to the normal = towards. Bigger angle = away. 0° = straight on.

Why? Light is slower in glass and water than in air. Slowing down → turns towards the normal; speeding up → turns away. Along the normal the whole beam slows at once, so it does not turn.

DKey words and what not to confuse them with

RefractionA change in direction of light as it crosses a boundary between transparent materials.≠ reflection (bouncing off)
BoundaryThe surface where two materials meet, e.g. air and glass.≠ the edge of the paper
NormalA dashed line at 90° to the boundary where the ray crosses it.≠ the boundary itself
Towards / away from the normalThe angle to the normal gets smaller (towards, 40° → 25°) or bigger (away, 25° → 40°).≠ “bends down / up”
Speed of lightAbout 300 000 km/s in air, 225 000 km/s in water, 200 000 km/s in glass. A change of speed causes refraction.≠ “light always travels at the same speed”
Emergent rayThe ray that comes out of the block back into the air.≠ the refracted ray inside the block

FMisconception busters

  • ✗ “Light always bends at a boundary.”✓ A ray along the normal (0°) goes straight through without changing direction.
  • ✗ “It bends the same way going in and coming out.”✓ Into glass: towards the normal. Out into air: away from it.
  • ✗ “Towards the normal means bending down the page.”✓ It means the angle to the normal gets smaller — whichever way the page is turned.
  • ✗ “The glass pulls the light towards the normal.”✓ Nothing pulls it. Light slows down in glass; the side of the beam that enters first slows first, so the beam turns.

GIn the UAE

Hotel pool, Dubai Marina: light from the pool floor bends away from the normal as it leaves the water, so the pool looks shallower than it really is. Lifeguards warn swimmers for exactly this reason — the same refraction that makes a pencil look broken in a glass of water.

★ Going further: the refractive index n = speed in air ÷ speed in the material — 1.5 for glass, 1.33 for water. Snell’s law, sin i = n × sin r, predicts every angle in this unit: sin 40° = 1.5 × sin 25.4° = 0.64.

6Exam technique for this point

  • A normal at every boundaryDraw a dashed normal at 90° where the ray goes in and another where it comes out. Each one earns marks.
  • Towards or away? Check the angleCompare angles to the normal: smaller = towards, bigger = away. Never say “down” or “up”.
  • Explain = what + whySay what happens to the angle AND why: “light slows down in glass, so it changes direction”. Then quote a data pair as evidence.
  • Don’t forget 0°If the table has 0°, say what happens: no change of direction. It is often a mark on its own.
  • Method = accuracyPractical questions reward why: narrow ray, crosses far apart, ruler, protractor on the normal.
