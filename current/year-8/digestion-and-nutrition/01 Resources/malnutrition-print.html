Balanced diet & malnutrition

Enough energy does not always mean enough nutrients.

MALNUTRITION is the wider category

Too little, too much or an imbalance of energy and/or nutrients. Examples include:

StarvationProlonged shortage of energy.
Nutrient deficiencyToo little of a needed nutrient, even when energy needs are met.
Energy excessPersistently taking in more energy than needed.

1Seven components of a balanced diet

Balance means suitable amounts for needs over time, not equal amounts. Foods can supply several components.

ComponentUseful body functionUseful sources
CarbohydrateEnergy for respirationRice, bread, potatoes
ProteinMaterials for growth and repairBeans, lentils, eggs, fish
Fat / lipidEnergy storage, insulation and absorption of fat-soluble vitaminsOil, nuts, seeds
VitaminsSupport specific body processes in small amountsVitamin C: peppers; vitamin A precursor: carrots
MineralsSupport specific processes, e.g. iron for oxygen transportIron: lentils; calcium: dairy or fortified alternatives
FibreHelps food move through the gutWholegrains, fruit, vegetables
WaterTransport, cooling and a medium for many cell reactionsDrinking water; water in foods

2When a nutrient is too low

Iron

Haemoglobin carries oxygen in the blood.

Deficiency can cause iron-deficiency anaemia.

Sources: Beans, lentils, meat.

Vitamin A

Supports normal vision.

Low intake can impair night vision. Severe, prolonged deficiency can cause blindness.

Sources: Eggs; carrots provide beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A.

Vitamin C

Supports healthy tissues and wound healing.

Severe deficiency can cause scurvy.

Sources: Peppers, citrus fruit.

Vitamin D

Helps regulate calcium and phosphate for healthy bones.

Deficiency can cause rickets in children.

Sources: Oily fish, egg yolk, appropriately fortified foods.

These are possible effects, not diagnoses. Anaemia has other causes too; rickets can also involve calcium deficiency.
