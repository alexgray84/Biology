Test the law of reflection

Use this sheet to set up the practical and organise your evidence. Record every prediction, result, diagram and conclusion in your exercise book.

Measure both angles from the dashed normal. The normal is drawn at 90° to the mirror.

Method

  1. Place the mirror on a ruled line. Mark its position.
  2. Draw a normal at 90° to the mirror line.
  3. Aim one narrow ray at the point where the normal meets the mirror.
  4. Mark two points on the incident ray and two on the reflected ray.
  5. Switch off the ray box. Remove the equipment. Join each pair of points with a ruler.
  6. Measure angle i and angle r from the normal.
  7. Repeat for incident angles of 20°, 30°, 40° and 50°.

Safe working: Keep the beam below eye level. Never look into the ray box. The lamp housing can become hot, so switch it off between readings and hold only the cool base.

Your practical record

For one reading, draw the mirror, normal, incident ray and reflected ray. Add arrowheads. Label i and r with the measured values.

Quality check before you move on

  • The ray box has one narrow, visible ray.
  • Every ray reaches the same point on the mirror.
  • The normal is exactly 90° to the mirror.
  • Angles are measured between each ray and the normal.
  • Ray lines use a ruler and arrowheads show direction.

Write your conclusion in your book

  1. State the relationship between angle i and angle r.
  2. Support the relationship with two pairs of your measured values.
  3. State the law of reflection in one precise sentence.
  4. Explain why one pair of measured angles might differ by 1–2° even if the law is correct.

Go deeper

Imagine rotating the mirror clockwise by 10° while the incident ray stays fixed. Predict how the reflected ray changes. Explain your prediction using the normal.

A secure conclusion

  • Names the normal, not the mirror surface, as the reference line.
  • Uses angle of incidence and angle of reflection accurately.
  • Uses measured evidence, not “the results were good”.
  • Separates the scientific law from small measurement uncertainty.
