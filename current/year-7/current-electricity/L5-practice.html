Go further. Sentence 5 says adding a lamp in series makes the current smaller. Adding a lamp in parallel does the opposite to the supply current. Explain why, using the word “paths”.
Answer. A new parallel branch adds another complete path for charge, so the total resistance of the circuit falls and the supply current rises. Each existing branch still has the full supply voltage, so it keeps roughly the same current — the extra current is the new branch's.

Go further. Two of these quantities are “per something” quantities: current is charge per second and voltage is energy per coulomb. Explain why that means a big voltage does not automatically mean a big current.
Answer. They measure different things. Voltage says how much energy each coulomb carries; current says how many coulombs pass each second. A high voltage across a high resistance still gives a small current, because resistance limits how fast the charge can flow.

Part D · Always, sometimes, never everyone · talk first, then write

For each statement write always, sometimes or never, then give your reason. If you write “sometimes”, you must say when.

  1. The current is the same at every point in a series circuit.
    Always. One path only, so the same charge flows past every point.
  2. The current splits equally at a junction.
    Sometimes — only when the branches have equal resistance. Otherwise the branch with less resistance takes the larger share. The branch currents still add to the supply current.
  3. Each branch of a parallel circuit has the full supply voltage.
    Always (in the ideal circuits we use). Each branch is joined across the same two supply points. In a real circuit it is very slightly less, because of small losses in the source, wires and switch.
  4. Adding another lamp in series makes all the lamps dimmer.
    Always, on the same supply. More total resistance means a smaller current, and the supply voltage is now shared between more lamps.
  5. If one lamp in a circuit breaks, the others go out.
    Sometimes — yes in series (the only path is broken); no in parallel (each branch is its own complete path), unless the break is in the shared supply wire.
  6. The supply voltage is shared equally between components in series.
    Sometimes — only when the components have equal resistance. The drops always add up to the supply voltage, but a larger resistance takes a larger share.
  7. Current is used up as it travels round a circuit.
    Never. Charge is not destroyed. Energy is transferred from the charge to the components, but the current returning to the battery is the same as the current leaving it.
  8. A voltmeter is connected across a component.
    Always. It compares two points, so it needs its own branch across the component, leaving the original path intact.
Build understanding. Use this stem: “This is sometimes true. It is true when ______, but not when ______.” If you cannot finish the second gap, the answer is probably always or never.

Part E · Read the circuit everyone

Both supplies are 6 V and every lamp is identical. Treat the circuits as ideal.

Circuit 1 — series.
Circuit 2 — parallel.
  1. Circuit 1. What would an ammeter placed at X read? Explain how you know.
    Answer. 0.30 A — the same. There is one path, so the current is the same at every point in the loop.
  2. Circuit 1. The lamps are identical. State the voltage across each one, and the rule you used.
    Answer. 3 V each. The voltages across series components add up to the supply voltage, and identical lamps have equal resistance, so 3 + 3 = 6 V.
  3. Circuit 2. Branch L1 carries 0.40 A and the supply current is 0.90 A. Calculate the current in branch L2.
    Answer. 0.90 − 0.40 = 0.50 A. The branch currents add to the supply current.
  4. Circuit 2. State the voltage across L1 and across L2. Explain why it is not 3 V each.
    Answer. 6 V across each. Each branch is joined across the same two supply points, so each gets the whole supply voltage. Voltage is not “split at the junction” — it is the current that splits there.
  5. In Circuit 2, are the branch currents equal? The lamps are identical — what does that tell you about the reading of 0.40 A?
    Answer. Identical lamps across the same voltage should carry equal currents, so you would expect about 0.45 A each. A 0.40 A / 0.50 A split means the lamps are not really identical, or a reading or connection is faulty. Good scientists check the apparatus when the numbers disagree with the rule.
Build understanding. Before you answer, trace each circuit with your finger and count the complete paths back to the battery: one path means series, two or more means parallel. Then pick the matching rule: series — current the same, voltages add up; parallel — currents add up, voltage the same.
Go further. One lamp in Circuit 2 is unscrewed. Describe what happens to the supply ammeter reading and to the remaining lamp, and explain both.
Answer. The remaining lamp stays lit at about the same brightness, because its own path is still complete and it still has the full supply voltage. The supply ammeter reading falls to just that branch's current, because one of the two paths has been removed.

Part F · Check your understanding on your own — no help, no talking

Choose one letter for each. Be ready to defend it: your teacher will ask why, not just what.

  1. An ideal 6 V battery is connected to two lamps in series. A voltmeter across the first lamp reads 4 V. The voltage across the second lamp is:
    • A6 V
    • B2 V
    • C3 V
    • D10 V
    B — 2 V. The series drops add to the supply voltage: 6 − 4 = 2 V.
    A treats it as parallel (each component across the full supply). C assumes equal sharing — but the 4 V reading already proves the lamps are not equal. D adds instead of subtracting.
  2. Three identical lamps are in parallel across a 6 V supply. One lamp is unscrewed. The other two:
    • Ago out
    • Bget dimmer
    • Cstay at about the same brightness
    • Dget much brighter
    C — stay at about the same brightness. Each branch is a complete path across the same two supply points, so each keeps the full supply voltage and its own current.
    A is the series answer. B assumes the voltage is shared between branches. D assumes the “spare” current is pushed into the others.
  3. A lamp is added in series to an existing series circuit, with the same supply. The ammeter reading:
    • Aincreases, because there is more to light up
    • Bdecreases, because the total resistance is greater
    • Cstays the same, because the battery is unchanged
    • Ddecreases, because current is used up by the extra lamp
    B. More components in series means greater total resistance, so a smaller current on the same supply.
    D gives the right answer for the wrong reason — current is never used up. C forgets that the circuit, not only the battery, sets the current.

Part G · Spot the mistake everyone

Each pupil has made one clear error. Write what is wrong and write a corrected version.

  1. “The battery gives out current. Each lamp uses some of it up, so the lamp furthest from the battery is the dimmest.”
    Error. Current is not used up, and position in the circuit does not affect brightness.
    Correction. The current is the same all the way round a series circuit. Identical lamps in series are equally bright, wherever they are. It is energy that is transferred to each lamp, not current.
  2. “In a parallel circuit the voltage splits at the junction, so each lamp gets 3 V from a 6 V battery.”
    Error. It is the current that splits at a junction, not the voltage.
    Correction. Each branch is connected across the same two supply points, so each lamp has the full 6 V. The branch currents are what add up to the supply current.
  3. “To measure the voltage across a lamp, I broke the circuit and put the voltmeter in the gap.”
    Error. That is how you connect an ammeter. A voltmeter in the path stops the current, so the lamp goes out and the reading is meaningless.
    Correction. Leave the lamp's own connections in place and add the voltmeter on a separate branch, one lead on each terminal of the lamp.
  4. “A 6 V supply has a 10 Ω lamp and a 20 Ω lamp in series, so each lamp has 3 V across it.”
    Error. Equal sharing only applies to equal resistances.
    Correction. The drops still add to 6 V, but the bigger resistance takes the bigger share: 2 V across the 10 Ω lamp and 4 V across the 20 Ω lamp.
Build understanding. Use two steps every time: (1) underline the word that is wrong, (2) write “It should say ______ because ______.” Question 1 is done as a model — check your wording against the reveal before you do the rest.

Part H · Go further when Part F is checked

resistance = voltage ÷ current
  1. A lamp has 4.0 V across it and carries a current of 0.50 A. Calculate its resistance. Give the unit.
    Answer. R = V ÷ I = 4.0 ÷ 0.50 = 8.0 Ω.
  2. A 10 Ω and a 20 Ω resistor are in series on a 6 V ideal supply. The current is 0.20 A. Calculate the voltage across each, and show that your two answers are consistent with the series rule.
    Answer. V = I × R, so 0.20 × 10 = 2.0 V and 0.20 × 20 = 4.0 V. Check: 2.0 + 4.0 = 6.0 V, which equals the supply voltage. The same current passes through both, so the larger resistance has the larger voltage.
  3. Explain why house lights are wired in parallel and not in series. Give two reasons.
    Answer. Any two of: each light gets the full mains voltage, so it is at full brightness; each can be switched independently; if one bulb fails the others stay on, because each has its own complete path.
  4. In the real lesson, two series lamps measured 2.98 V and 2.98 V while the supply measured 5.97 V, not 6.00 V. Does that disprove the series voltage rule? Explain.
    Answer. No. 2.98 + 2.98 = 5.96 V, which matches the measured supply voltage of 5.97 V to within rounding. The 6.00 V is the setting on the source; once current flows there are small voltage losses inside the source, in the leads and in the switch, so the terminal voltage is slightly lower. The rule holds for the whole loop.

  1. State the rule for current in a series circuit and the rule for current in a parallel circuit. (2 marks)
    Answer. Series: the current is the same at every point (1). Parallel: the branch currents add up to the supply current (1).
  2. Two identical lamps are in parallel across a 9 V supply. State the voltage across each lamp and explain your answer. (2 marks)
    Answer. 9 V across each (1), because each branch is connected across the same two supply points, so each has the full supply voltage (1).
  3. Explain, using resistance, why three lamps in series are dimmer than one lamp on the same supply. (2 marks)
    Answer. Three lamps give a greater total resistance, so the current is smaller (1). The supply voltage is also shared between three lamps, so each transfers less energy per second and is dimmer (1).
