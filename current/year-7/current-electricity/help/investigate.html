L2 · Current

1 · Does current get used up?

Words beside you: current = charge each second · reading = measured value

Use the one-lamp circuit. Keep the lamp and battery unchanged.

Predict: I think ___ will happen because ___.

  1. Choose the One lamp example. Close the switch.
  2. Insert an ammeter before the lamp. Remove the original wire and reconnect through A. Leave no bypass. Use the meter guide if needed.
  3. Read the current. Write it in the first row with unit A.
  4. Move the ammeter after the lamp. Restore the first connection. Read and record again.
  5. Open the switch. Record the current. Compare your results with your prediction.

Draw: Draw the one-lamp circuit and one valid ammeter position. Label both measured positions on your drawing.

Draw on paper, use symbols, or direct a scribe. Label your circuit and readings.

Explain: Before the lamp the current was ___ A. After it, ___. This supports ___ because ___.

Model answer & success criteria

Closed-loop readings should be approximately equal (about 0.59 A with the standard setup); opening the loop gives 0 A. Charge is not used up in the lamp, although energy is transferred. Accept your own sensible readings and account for setup differences.

Success: Three labelled readings with units; explain equal series current and why the gap stops current. Predictions can be revised.

L3 · Series and parallel

2 · What changes with two lamps?

Words beside you: series = one path · parallel = separate branches · compare = same or different

You will compare one lamp, two series lamps and two parallel lamps.

Predict: I think ___ will happen because ___.

  1. Choose One lamp. Record the battery current and lamp current from the component readings.
  2. Choose Series circuit. Keep both lamps at 10 Ω and the battery at 6 V. Record battery and both lamp currents.
  3. Choose Parallel circuit. Keep the same settings. Record the same three currents.
  4. Compare each row: are the currents equal, or do the branch currents add to the battery current?

Draw: Draw the two-lamp series and parallel circuits. Mark one path in series and both branches in parallel.

Draw on paper, use symbols, or direct a scribe. Label your circuit and readings.

Explain: In series, ___. In parallel, ___ A + ___ A is about ___ A. This tells me ___.

Model answer & success criteria

Standard model values are approximately: one lamp 0.594 A; two series lamps 0.298 A throughout; two parallel branches about 0.588 A each, adding to about 1.175 A at the source. Rounding can change the last digit. Adding series resistance reduces current. Adding a parallel branch increases source current.

Success: Three labelled comparisons; use your readings to distinguish equal series current from the sum of parallel branch currents.

L3 · Faults

3 · Which break stops which lamp?

Words beside you: branch = one path between junctions · common = shared by both branches

Use two identical lamps. Predict separately for each break.

Predict: I think ___ will happen because ___.

  1. Load Series circuit. Remove one wire in the loop. Record both lamps. Undo the removal.
  2. Load Parallel circuit. Remove a wire in only Lamp 2’s branch. Leave Lamp 1 connected to the supply. Record both lamps.
  3. Undo that removal. Remove a wire in the shared supply path instead. Record both lamps.
  4. Trace the surviving paths. Use them to explain all three observations.
BreakLamp 1: on/offLamp 2: on/off
Series loop
Only parallel branch 2
Common supply path

Draw: Draw the parallel circuit with the break in branch 2. Mark the surviving complete route.

Draw on paper, use symbols, or direct a scribe. Label your circuit and readings.

Explain: When I broke ___, lamp ___ stayed on / went off because its complete path ___.

Model answer & success criteria

A series-loop break turns both lamps off. Opening only parallel branch 2 leaves lamp 1 on and lamp 2 off. A break in the common supply path turns both off. Use the existence of a complete source-to-lamp-and-back path to explain each outcome.

Success: Correct predictions or corrections; all three observations; explain a branch fault and a common-path fault.

L4 · Voltage

4 · How is voltage shared?

Words beside you: voltage = energy per charge · across = one lead on each side

Measure across the source terminals as well as across each lamp. The source setting is not the measured terminal voltage.

Predict: I think ___ will happen because ___.

  1. Load Series circuit with two 10 Ω lamps and a 6 V battery.
  2. Add a voltmeter across the battery terminals. Leave the lamp path intact. Record its reading.
  3. Place the voltmeter across Lamp 1, then Lamp 2. Record both readings. Add the two lamp readings.
  4. Load Parallel circuit with the same settings. Repeat all three measurements.
  5. Compare each parallel lamp reading with the source-terminal reading. Do not add parallel branch voltages along a single path.

Draw: Draw one circuit with a voltmeter across Lamp 1. Show that the lamp’s own current path stays complete.

Draw on paper, use symbols, or direct a scribe. Label your circuit and readings.

Explain: In series the lamp readings add to about ___ V. In parallel each lamp has about ___ V. Therefore ___.

Model answer & success criteria

Typical model values: series source terminals about 5.97 V, each lamp about 2.98 V; parallel source terminals about 5.88 V, each lamp about 5.88 V. Lead losses and rounding explain small differences. Series component drops add approximately to the terminal voltage; branches between the same two points share the same voltage. Across a lamp, 3 V means 3 J transferred per C of charge.

Success: Record six readings with V; state different series and parallel rules; connect a voltmeter across two points.
