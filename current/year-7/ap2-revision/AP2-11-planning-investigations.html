Planning a fair investigation

“Apply practical skills: equipment and safety, Bunsen burner use/write-up, planning investigations, recording results in tables and drawing/analysing graphs.”

Big ideaChange one thing (the independent variable). Measure one thing (the dependent variable). Control what matters — keep the same anything that could affect the result. Write a method so clear that another scientist could repeat it without asking you a question.

AChange one · measure one · control what matters

The control test: if it changed between tests, could it change the fall time? Yes → control it. No → leave it out.

BThe planning chain

Testable question: How does the number of paper clips affect the time a paper helicopter takes to fall 2 metres?
Prediction: I predict more clips will give a shorter fall time, because the helicopter is heavier.

Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 11 · part 2 of 3

CA method another scientist can follow

✗ Vague✓ Precise
Drop it and time it.Release the helicopter from 2.0 m and start the timer at the moment it is released.
Stop when it lands.Stop the timer when it first touches the floor. Record the fall time / s.
Do it again.Repeat three times for each number of paper clips and calculate a mean.

Each step: equipment → quantity → action → endpoint. Follow test: equipment named · quantities and units · controls · clear endpoint · repeats · safety control.

DKey words and what not to confuse them with

Independent variableWhat you deliberately change.≠ dependent variable
Dependent variableWhat you measure as the outcome, with a unit.≠ independent variable
Control variableA condition kept the same because it could affect the result.≠ everything in the room
Fair testOnly the independent variable changes, so it must be causing any change in the result.≠ a test that is just careful
PredictionWhat you expect to happen and why, using science.≠ a guess with no reason
RepeatsDoing each test (usually 3 times) so you can see if readings agree, then take a mean.≠ doing the whole experiment once more

FMisconception busters

  • ✗ “Control everything in the room.”✓ Only control things that could affect the result. Ask: could it change the outcome?
  • ✗ “Change two things to save time.”✓ Then you can’t tell which one caused the change. Change one.
  • ✗ “One careful reading is enough.”✓ Repeats show whether readings agree, so a result that does not fit stands out.
  • ✗ “A prediction is just a guess.”✓ A prediction says what will happen and why, using science.

GIn the UAE

Summer in Abu Dhabi: which water bottle keeps water coolest in a parked car? Change: bottle material. Measure: water temperature / °C after 30 minutes. Control: starting temperature, volume of water, same place in the car, same time of day.

★ Going further: repeatable = you get similar results when you repeat it; reproducible = another scientist gets similar results.

Mark scheme · 4 marks

  1. 1
    Equipment: identical boxes, a thermometer in the same position in eachAnother group knows what to set up.
  2. 1
    Action and endpoint: leave all three in the same sunny place, read each thermometer after a stated time (e.g. 30 min)“Leave them for a while” is not repeatable.

Accept: Any sensible stated time. Steps as a numbered list or clear sentences.

Do not accept: “Do it again to make it fair.” (Repeats don’t make a test fair — controls do.) “Check the temperature.” with no time.

6Exam technique for this point

  • Name the variable fullyWrite “the temperature inside the box / °C”, not just “temperature” or “the box”.
  • Control what mattersFor each control ask: if this changed, could it change the result? If not, leave it out.
  • Number your methodOne action per step: equipment → quantity → action → endpoint. Use real numbers and units.
  • Repeats need a reason“Repeat three times and take a mean, because…” — the reason earns the mark, not the word repeat.
  • Judge = verdict + reasonStart with yes or no, then link your reason to the result: “…so you can’t tell which caused it.”
