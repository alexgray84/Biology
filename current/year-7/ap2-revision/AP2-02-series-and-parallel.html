Series and parallel circuits

“Compare series and parallel circuits, including how current and voltage behave in each.”

Big ideaCount the paths. Series = one path: the current is the same everywhere and the voltage is shared. Parallel = two or more paths: the current splits at a junction and adds back up, and every branch gets the full supply voltage.

ASeries — one path

  • Paths: one complete path
  • Current: the same at every point
  • Voltage: shared — the lamp voltages add up to the supply (equal only if the lamps are equal)
  • One lamp breaks: all go out — the only path is broken

BParallel — two or more paths

  • Paths: two or more complete paths
  • Current: splits at a junction; branch currents add up to the supply current
  • Voltage: each branch has the full supply voltage — it spans the same two supply points
  • One lamp breaks: the others stay on — their paths are still complete

The mirror: what adds up in series (voltage) stays the same in parallel — and what stays the same in series (current) adds up in parallel.

Real readings: two series lamps read 2.98 V + 2.98 V = 5.96 V while the supply measured 5.97 V (set to 6.00 V). The small gap is losses in the supply and leads — the rule still holds.

DKey words and what not to confuse them with

Series circuitOnly one complete path for the charge.≠ “lamps in a row” — check the paths, not the look
Parallel circuitTwo or more complete paths (branches) for the charge.≠ a diagram that just looks spread out
JunctionA point where the circuit splits into branches or where they rejoin. Drawn as a dot.≠ a corner
Potential difference (voltage)The energy transferred for each coulomb of charge between two points. Unit: volt (V).≠ current (A)
VoltmeterMeasures voltage. Connected across a component, on its own branch.≠ ammeter (in the path)
ResistanceThe opposition to the flow of charge. More resistance → less current.≠ voltage

FMisconception busters

  • ✗ “The voltage splits at the junction.”✓ It is the current that splits. Each parallel branch spans the same two supply points, so it has the full voltage.
  • ✗ “Series always shares the voltage equally.”✓ Only for equal components. A bigger resistance takes a bigger share, but the shares always add up to the supply.
  • ✗ “The lamp furthest from the battery is dimmest.”✓ Identical lamps in series are equally bright: the current is the same everywhere.
  • ✗ “The current always splits equally.”✓ Only for identical branches. The branch with less resistance takes the bigger share.

GIn the UAE

UAE National Day lights: old light strings were wired in series — one failed bulb and the whole string went dark. Your home is wired in parallel: switching off the majlis lights doesn’t switch off the AC, and each appliance gets the full mains voltage.

★ Going further: a 10 Ω and a 20 Ω lamp in series on 6 V share it 2 V and 4 V — the bigger resistance takes the bigger share.

6Exam technique for this point

  • Count paths firstBefore any rule, trace with your finger: one path back to the supply = series; two or more = parallel.
  • Rule, numbers, unitWrite the rule in words, then the numbers, then the answer with its unit: “branches add up: 0.50 + 0.40 = 0.90 A”.
  • Compare = both, with whereasName both circuits in one sentence: “In series…, whereas in parallel…”. One side alone scores nothing.
  • Check it adds backSeries voltages must add to the supply; parallel branch currents must add to the supply current. Check before moving on.
  • Current or voltage?Underline A or V in the question. Current splits at junctions; voltage is shared along a series path.
