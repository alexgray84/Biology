Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 8 · Physics · Forces

Friction, air and water resistance

“Describe friction and other resistive forces and apply this understanding to practical situations.”

Big ideaResistive forces act against motion. Friction acts where two surfaces slide (or try to slide) over each other. Air resistance and water resistance (drag) act on things moving through air or water. They can be useful or a problem — and we can increase or reduce them.

AFriction: what it is and why

BAir and water resistance (drag)

The faster something moves, the bigger its air or water resistance. So a cyclist reaches a top speed when the drag has grown to equal the forward force.

Knowledge organiser · AP2 point 8 · part 2 of 3

CUseful or a problem? Increase or reduce?

More friction / dragLess friction / drag
Howrougher surfaces, tread, rubber, bigger load, larger area facing the flowlubricant (oil, grease, wax), smoother surfaces, wheels or bearings, streamlining
Useful forgrip (shoes, tyres), brakes, parachutes, walkingspeed, less wear and heating in engines, saving energy

Measuring friction: pull a block at a steady speed with a force meter — the reading equals the friction. Change only the surface; keep the load, block and force meter the same; repeat and average.

DKey words and what not to confuse them with

FrictionA contact force between surfaces that slide, or try to slide, over each other. It opposes the sliding.≠ air resistance
Air resistanceDrag from the air on a moving object. Bigger at higher speed.≠ gravity
Water resistanceDrag from water on a moving object. Bigger than air resistance at the same speed.≠ upthrust
DragThe name for air resistance and water resistance together.≠ a pull forward
LubricantA substance (oil, grease, wax) that reduces friction between surfaces.≠ glue
StreamlinedShaped so air or water flows smoothly around it, which reduces drag.≠ lighter

ECommand words

DescribeSay what happens — no reason needed.Friction acts to the left, against the sliding.
ExplainSay what happens and why.Wax smooths the bumps, so less friction.
SuggestApply your science to a new situation.Use tyres with deep tread to grip the sand.
CompareSay how both differ — mention both.Rubber gives more friction than wax.
EvaluateGive for and against, then a judgement.Low friction is faster, but you need some to stop.

FMisconception busters

  • ✗ “Friction is always bad.”✓ Without friction you couldn’t walk, grip, brake or steer. It is useful as well as a problem.
  • ✗ “Friction only acts when things are moving.”✓ If you push a heavy box and it doesn’t move, friction is pushing back just as hard.
  • ✗ “Moving things stop because their force runs out.”✓ They slow because friction and drag give a resultant force against the motion.
  • ✗ “Streamlining gets rid of air resistance.”✓ It reduces drag. Anything moving through air or water still has some.

GIn the UAE

Sandboarding in the desert: riders wax the base of their boards so it slides with less friction on the sand. To slow down, they lean back and dig the tail in — more friction. Dune drivers let some air out of their tyres so more tyre touches the sand, which helps grip.

★ Going further: a falling skydiver speeds up until air resistance grows to equal their weight — then the forces are balanced and they fall at a steady top speed.

Examiner’s eye — three answers to part (c)
0 / 4
Wax makes the board faster because it is slippy, and the rider stops because the force runs out.

“Slippy” is not science, and “the force runs out” is a misconception. No cause of friction.

2 / 4
Wax is a lubricant so there is less friction and the board goes faster. At the bottom friction slows it down.

Mark 2 ✓ (less friction) and mark 3 ✓ (faster). No cause of friction; mark 4 needs “against the motion” or an unbalanced force.

4 / 4
Friction happens because tiny bumps on the board and the sand catch on each other. Wax smooths these bumps, so there is less friction. That means less force acts against the motion, so the rider speeds up more on the slope. On the flat sand, friction and air resistance still act against the motion, so the forces are unbalanced backwards and the rider slows down until they stop.

Cause → change → effect on speed → why they stop, each linked with “so”.

6Exam technique for this point

  • Direction every timeResistive forces act against the motion. Draw the arrow backwards and say “opposite to the motion”.
  • Cause → effectFor friction, start from the cause (bumps catching) before saying what a change does.
  • Never “runs out”Things slow because of an unbalanced force against the motion — not because their force runs out.
  • Steady = balancedIn practicals, a steady-speed pull equals the friction. Say “balanced forces” to earn the mark.
  • Judge both sidesIn “is friction good?” questions, give one benefit and one problem, then decide.
