Recap & connect — what do you already know?

3 min

Quick brain-dump. Without looking back at L1–L4, answer in your book:

  1. What's the word equation for photosynthesis?
  2. What's the IV, DV and one CV for today's practical?
  3. What gas are the bubbles made of, and where do they come from?
📘 Why we recap (Rosenshine 01)

Pulling info OUT of memory makes it stronger than re-reading. If you can't quite remember, leave a gap and come back — that's the moment your brain learns most.

Hint
  • The reactants are CO₂ and H₂O. The products are…?
  • The IV is what you change. Today, you change the distance of what?
  • Photosynthesis produces a gas. Which one?
Sentence stems
  • "Photosynthesis: ____ + ____ → ____ + ____ "
  • "My IV is ____, measured in ____."
  • "My DV is ____, measured in ____."
  • "The bubbles are ____ gas, produced by ____."
🔒 Riddle to unlock the answers

"I'm the variable you change on purpose. The 'I' in IV/DV/CV. What am I called? (1 word)"

✓ Answers
  1. Carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (sunlight + chlorophyll over the arrow).
  2. IV = distance of lamp from beaker (cm). DV = bubbles per minute (or cm³ O₂/min). CV = e.g. same pondweed, same temperature, same NaHCO₃.
  3. The bubbles are oxygen. Photosynthesis produces O₂ as a by-product, which leaves the pondweed and floats to the top.
2 You do

Predict — sketch your graph BEFORE you start

4 min
📘 Why predict first?

You'll remember and notice far more if you commit to a prediction first. Being a bit wrong is way more memorable than just being told.

In your book:

  1. Sketch a pair of axes: distance from lamp on x-axis, bubbles per minute on y-axis.
  2. Draw the line you think you'll get.
  3. Write a one-sentence prediction underneath.
Hint

When the lamp is closer, the pondweed gets more light. More light → more photosynthesis → more bubbles. So as distance increases, what happens to bubbles per minute?

Sentence stems
  • "As the distance from the lamp increases, the number of bubbles per minute will ____."
  • "This is because ____."
🎯 What good predictions look like

A weak prediction: "The bubbles will go down."

A strong prediction: "As the lamp moves further away (10 cm → 50 cm), the bubbles per minute will decrease, because lower light intensity means less energy for photosynthesis. The drop will be steepest at first and then level off when light is no longer the limiting factor."

Notice it: gives direction, includes numbers, gives a reason, predicts the shape.

🔒 Riddle to unlock the model prediction

"What you make BEFORE the experiment. A scientific guess about what will happen — but not quite a hypothesis. Starts with P, 10 letters."

✓ Model prediction

"As distance increases from 10 cm to 50 cm, the number of bubbles per minute will decrease because the light intensity reaching the pondweed is lower. There will be a sharp drop at first, then the line will start to level off (because light won't be the only limiting factor any more)."

Sketch: a curve that starts high on the left, drops steeply, then flattens off on the right.

3

Apparatus check & safety

3 min

At your bench, find each item. Tap to tick it off as you find it.

  • ✓Pondweed (Cabomba or Elodea) — 1 sprig, ~10 cm
  • ✓Beaker (250 cm³) filled with water
  • ✓Sodium hydrogen carbonate (NaHCO₃) — 1 g
  • ✓Inverted funnel + inverted test tube
  • ✓Lamp (with bulb) + ruler (30+ cm)
  • ✓Thermometer (to check the water stays at the same temperature)
  • ✓Stopwatch / timer
  • ✓Safety goggles · paper towel
⚠️ Safety reminders
  • Lamp gets hot — hold by the base, never the bulb.
  • Water + electricity = danger. Keep the lamp lead well away from the beaker.
  • Glass beaker / test tube — handle with two hands. Tell your teacher if anything breaks.
  • Wear goggles when handling NaHCO₃ powder.
🔒 Riddle to unlock the troubleshooting tips

"I'm a thin glass tool with a red or silver line. I tell you the temperature of the water. (One word, starts with T.)"

✓ What could go wrong (and how to fix it)
  • No bubbles at all? → Pondweed might be old, lamp might be too far, water might be too cold. Try crushing the cut end of the pondweed gently and put the lamp at 10 cm to start.
  • Bubbles are tiny / inconsistent? → Add a fresh pinch of NaHCO₃. Make sure the funnel is over the pondweed, not next to it.
  • Water heating up? → Use a beaker of cold water as a heat shield between lamp and beaker. Heat is a CV — keep it constant!
4 I do

Watch the demo — what to look for

8 min

Your teacher (or video) will run through the practical once. Don't try to do it yet. Just watch. Note 3 things you notice in your book.

📘 What experts pay attention to (live modelling)

Your teacher will be talking aloud while doing it. They'll point out five things that beginners miss:

  1. Where exactly the ruler is measured from (lamp or bulb? front of beaker or middle?)
  2. Why we wait 1 minute before counting (the rate needs to stabilise).
  3. How to count fast bubbles (count for less time × scale up).
  4. What to do if bubbles come too fast to count.
  5. How they record the number STRAIGHT into the table (not "I'll remember").
Sentence stems for what you noticed
  • "I noticed that ____."
  • "The teacher waited ____ before ____."
  • "One thing I would do differently is ____."
🔒 Riddle to unlock the answers

"When a teacher demonstrates a task and talks through it out loud, this is called live ___. (One word, starts with M, 9 letters.)"

✓ 3 things you should have noticed
  1. The lamp gives off heat — that's why the lamp is positioned away from the beaker (and a heat-shield beaker of cold water can be used).
  2. Bubbles aren't perfectly steady — there's a "warm-up" period after the lamp moves, which is why we wait 1 minute.
  3. Numbers are written down immediately, in pencil, in the right column. Not "I'll write it after."
5 We do

Run 1 together — calibrate at 30 cm

10 min

As a class, set up at 30 cm. Everyone counts together. Compare numbers — should be similar across the room (same conditions!).

📘 The 6-step run (do this every time)
  1. Move the lamp to the chosen distance from the beaker.
  2. Wait 1 minute — let the rate stabilise.
  3. Start your stopwatch and count bubbles for 1 minute.
  4. Write the number straight into the results table (in pencil).
  5. Glance at the thermometer — has the water heated up? (Aim to keep it constant.)
  6. Move to the next distance and repeat.
After your "warm up + count" at 30 cm, write down: my count = ____ bubbles/min. Then compare with two other people on your bench.
Troubleshooting
  • Cut a fresh end on the pondweed stem — bubbles often start straight after a fresh cut.
  • Move the lamp closer (15 cm) just for the calibration. Then move it back to 30 cm.
  • Add a small pinch more NaHCO₃.
Sentence stems
  • "At 30 cm, I counted ____ bubbles per minute."
  • "My partner counted ____. The difference is ____."
  • "This could be because ____."
🔒 Riddle to unlock the calibration check

"How many minutes do you wait before starting to count bubbles, after moving the lamp? (Number word — spell it out, e.g. 'two'.)"

✓ Calibration check — what counts as "good data"?
  • If three people on your bench got within ±5 bubbles of each other: excellent — go for it.
  • If results vary wildly: check that the funnel is centred over the pondweed, the distance is measured the same way (lamp bulb to centre of beaker), and the lamp wattage is the same.
  • If you got 0: see the troubleshooting hint and rerun. Don't panic — record what you did, that's still data.
  • Always note the temperature of the water (CV check).

7

Plot & analyse — what does your data say?

6 min
📘 Rules for a good graph
  • IV on the x-axis (horizontal). DV on the y-axis (vertical).
  • Both axes labelled with units in brackets.
  • Use a sensible scale (use 80%+ of the page).
  • Points marked with a small × or +. Draw a smooth line / curve of best fit.
  • Title: "Graph showing how distance from lamp affects rate of photosynthesis in pondweed."

Plot your results. Then answer in your book:

  1. What's the shape of your line?
  2. At what distance does the rate start to level off?
  3. Compare your graph to your prediction. Were you right?
Hint

If your graph is going DOWN (left to right), distance is on x and bubbles on y, so your line shows: more distance → fewer bubbles. That makes sense — further from the light = less light = slower photosynthesis. Look for the plateau: the part where the line stops dropping much.

Sentence stems
  • "My graph shows that as distance increases, the rate ____."
  • "The rate started to level off at about ____ cm."
  • "My prediction was ____ . My results ____ this."
🔒 Riddle to unlock the model analysis

"The flat part of a graph after the steep change. The rate has stopped speeding up because another factor is now limiting. (One word, 7 letters, starts with P.)"

✓ Model analysis
  1. Shape: the line is highest at 10 cm and decreases as distance increases. The drop is steepest between 10 cm and 30 cm.
  2. Plateau: after about 30–40 cm, the line starts to level off. This means light is no longer the only limiting factor — something else (CO₂, temperature, or chlorophyll) is now controlling the rate.
  3. Compared to prediction: if you predicted a curve that dropped steeply then levelled, your prediction matched. If you predicted a straight line, your real data is more nuanced — well done for noticing.
8 You do

Reflect & evaluate

4 min

Three questions in your book:

  1. What was the most surprising thing you observed?
  2. If you had an anomaly, what do you think caused it?
  3. If you ran this again tomorrow, name one improvement you'd make.
📘 Plan-monitor-evaluate cycle

You planned in L4. You monitored as you went today. Now you evaluate. Real scientists do all three — and the evaluation is usually where the next investigation comes from.

Reflection stems
  • "The most surprising thing was ____, because ____."
  • "An anomaly happened at ____ cm — I think this was caused by ____."
  • "To improve the experiment, I would ____, because this would ____."
  • "My results would be more reliable if ____."
  • "My results would be more accurate if ____."
🎯 Model evaluation paragraph

"The most surprising thing I observed was that the rate didn't keep dropping forever — it levelled off at around 40 cm. This shows that light isn't the only limiting factor. I had one anomaly at 30 cm where I counted 25 bubbles compared to 38 in my other trials. I think this was because the water had warmed slightly from the lamp, which changed the temperature CV. To improve, I would put a beaker of cold water between the lamp and the experiment as a heat shield, so temperature stays constant. I'd also use a measuring cylinder to collect O₂ over water — that would give a more accurate measure of volume than counting bubbles, which vary in size."

🔒 Final riddle — last letter of OBSERVED

"A result that doesn't fit the pattern. You usually leave it out when calculating the mean. (One word, 7 letters, starts with A.)"

Key Words

Practical vocab + planning vocab.

🧪
Pondweed
Cabomba or Elodea — a water plant that produces visible O₂ bubbles.
💧
NaHCO₃
Sodium hydrogen carbonate — provides extra CO₂ in the water.
📐
Distance (IV)
Distance from lamp to beaker, in cm. The variable you change.
🫧
Bubbles/min (DV)
What you count — your dependent variable.
⚖️
Control variable
Kept the same to make it a fair test.
🔁
Repeat
Do each measurement at least 3 times. Improves reliability.
µ
Mean
Average of your repeats. Total ÷ number of values.
⚡
Anomaly
A reading that doesn't fit the pattern. Usually excluded from the mean.
📊
Plateau
Flat part of a graph — rate has stopped changing because another factor is limiting.
📈
Limiting factor
The thing currently slowing the rate. Increase it and rate goes up.
🎯
Accuracy
How close to the true value.
📏
Precision
How close repeats are to each other.
✓
Reliability
Same results if repeated. Improved by repeats.
🌡️
Calibration
Setting up your measurement so different people get similar readings.
