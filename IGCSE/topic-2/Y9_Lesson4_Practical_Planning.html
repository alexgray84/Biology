Do Now — What affects the rate of photosynthesis?

3 min

Write the balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis. Then list 4 things that might change how fast it goes.

📘 Quick content recap

Photosynthesis happens faster when conditions are better. The four main factors that affect the rate are:

  • Light intensity — more light → more energy for the reaction.
  • Carbon dioxide concentration — CO₂ is a reactant.
  • Temperature — affects the speed of enzymes that catalyse the reaction.
  • Chlorophyll (amount/colour of leaf) — needed to absorb light.
✍️ Write in book🧠 No peeking
Hint

Think back to L2 — 6 in front of CO₂, H₂O and O₂. Sunlight + chlorophyll go above the arrow. Then for "what affects rate", think: what would the plant need MORE of to grow faster?

Sentence stems
  • "6 ____ + 6 ____ → ____ + 6 ____"
  • "The rate of photosynthesis depends on ____, ____, ____ and ____."
🎯 Worked example — how to "think rate"

For any reaction, ask: "What does the reaction need?" Then ask "More of any of those things would speed it up."

For burning a candle: needs oxygen, fuel, heat. So removing oxygen, fuel or heat slows it down.

Apply the same logic to photosynthesis. What does the reaction need?

🔒 Riddle to unlock the answers

"I'm a colourless gas, formula CO₂. Plants take me in through their stomata as a reactant for photosynthesis. What's my full name? (2 words)"

✓ Answers

Equation: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ (sunlight + chlorophyll over the arrow).

4 factors that affect rate: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, temperature, chlorophyll amount.

The brief — Pondweed & light

3 min

Today's investigation: "How does light intensity affect the rate of photosynthesis in pondweed?"
Look at the diagram. What do you think the bubbles are? Why do they appear faster when the lamp is closer?

Hint

Look at the photosynthesis equation. What gas is a product? When the reaction happens, that gas escapes from the plant as bubbles. The faster the reaction, the more bubbles per minute.

🎯 Worked example — what counts as "rate"?

"Rate" = how much happens per unit time. Examples:

  • Rate of running = metres per second
  • Rate of reaction = grams of product per second
  • Rate of photosynthesis = bubbles per minute (or cm³ of O₂ per minute)

Always: amount ÷ time.

🔒 Riddle to unlock the answers

"I'm a product of photosynthesis. I'm a gas. My formula is O₂. The bubbles in the pondweed practical are made of me. What am I?"

✓ Answers

The bubbles are oxygen gas. Photosynthesis produces oxygen as a by-product, and the bubbles escape from the leaf and rise up through the water.

When the lamp is closer, the pondweed gets more light energy, so photosynthesis goes faster and more bubbles are produced per minute.

Why NaHCO₃? Sodium hydrogen carbonate provides extra CO₂ in the water — so CO₂ doesn't run out and slow the reaction.

Variables — IV, DV, CVs

10 min
📘 Content knowledge — the 3 types of variable
  • Independent Variable (IV) — the one thing you change on purpose.
  • Dependent Variable (DV) — what you measure in response.
  • Control Variables (CVs) — everything you keep the same so the test is fair.

⚠️ "Fair test" = only the IV is changed. If you change two things, you can't tell which one caused the effect.

Write down for the pondweed practical:

  1. The IV (and its units / how you'll change it)
  2. The DV (and its units / how you'll measure it)
  3. Three CVs (with how you'll keep them the same)
Hint — good vs bad answers
✗ Weak IV "Light"
✓ Strong IV "Distance from the lamp to the beaker, measured in cm using a ruler"
✗ Weak DV "How fast it goes"
✓ Strong DV "Number of bubbles released per minute, counted by eye"
Sentence stems
  • "My IV is ____, measured in ____. I'll change it by ____."
  • "My DV is ____, measured in ____. I'll record it by ____."
  • "I'll keep ____ the same by ____."
🎯 Worked example — DIFFERENT practical

"Investigate how temperature affects how fast amylase breaks down starch."

  • IV: Temperature, in °C, set using a water bath (10, 20, 30, 40, 50 °C).
  • DV: Time taken (in seconds) for iodine to stop turning blue-black, measured with a stopwatch.
  • CVs: volume of amylase (5 cm³), volume of starch (5 cm³), pH of solution (kept at 7), time the reaction is allowed to run before testing.

Notice each variable has a name + a unit + how it's measured/controlled. Copy that pattern for the pondweed practical.

🔒 Riddle to unlock the answers

"In an experiment, anything that can change is called a ____. There are 3 types: independent, dependent and control. What's the missing word?"

✓ Answers — for the pondweed practical

IV: Distance from lamp to beaker (cm), measured with a ruler. Change it: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 cm.

DV: Number of bubbles per minute (counted), or volume of O₂ collected (cm³ per minute).

CVs (any 3):

  • Same piece of pondweed (same species, same length/mass).
  • Same temperature of water (use thermometer).
  • Same volume of water + concentration of NaHCO₃.
  • Same time interval (e.g. count for 1 minute each).
  • Same lamp power (wattage).
4

Write a hypothesis

5 min
📘 What makes a good hypothesis?

A hypothesis is a testable prediction. It must:

  • Mention the IV and the DV.
  • Say what will happen to the DV when you change the IV.
  • Include a scientific reason ("because…").

Write a hypothesis for the pondweed investigation. Use the sentence frame to scaffold it.

Hint

If the lamp is closer, light intensity is higher. Higher light → more energy for photosynthesis → reaction speeds up → more bubbles per minute. So which way does the DV go when the IV (distance) goes down?

Sentence frame

"As the [IV] increases / decreases, the [DV] will increase / decrease, because [scientific reason linked to photosynthesis]."

🎯 Worked example — hypothesis for amylase practical

"As the temperature of the water bath increases from 10 °C to 40 °C, the time taken for iodine to stop turning blue-black will decrease, because higher temperatures give amylase enzymes more kinetic energy, so they collide with starch more often and break it down faster. Above 40 °C the enzyme will start to denature."

Notice it: names IV and DV with units, says direction of change, gives a scientific because, and even predicts the limit.

🔒 Riddle to unlock the answers

"I'm a testable scientific prediction. I always include 'as ___ changes, ___ will change because…'. I start with H. What am I?"

✓ Model hypothesis

"As the distance between the lamp and the pondweed decreases (from 50 cm to 10 cm), the number of bubbles produced per minute will increase, because moving the lamp closer increases the light intensity reaching the pondweed. More light energy means chlorophyll can absorb more, so the rate of photosynthesis goes up and more oxygen is released."

Stretch: "However, at very high light intensities the rate will plateau because another factor (like CO₂ concentration) becomes limiting."

Method — put the steps in order

8 min
📘 What makes a good method?
  • Numbered steps in the right order.
  • Specific quantities (volumes in cm³, distances in cm, times in minutes).
  • Someone else could repeat it exactly — no vague words like "a bit".
  • States the repeats (do at least 3 times).

The 7 steps below are scrambled. Re-write them in the correct order in your book.

  1. Repeat for distances of 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm.
  2. Place a beaker of water containing pondweed and 1 g of NaHCO₃ on the bench.
  3. Repeat each distance 3 times and calculate a mean.
  4. Move the lamp to 10 cm from the beaker.
  5. Cover an inverted funnel over the pondweed and place an inverted test tube on top.
  6. Wait 1 minute for the rate to stabilise, then count bubbles for 1 minute.
  7. Plot a graph of mean bubbles per minute against distance.
Hint — the structure of a method
  1. Set up the apparatus.
  2. Position / set the IV at its first value.
  3. Wait, then measure the DV.
  4. Change the IV, repeat.
  5. Repeat for reliability, calculate mean.
  6. Plot a graph.
🎯 Worked example — method writing tips

Bad: "Set up the lamp and time it."

Good: "Place the lamp 10 cm from the beaker. Wait 1 minute for the rate to stabilise. Count the bubbles produced for exactly 1 minute using a stopwatch. Record the count in your results table."

Add quantities and times — anyone can repeat it.

🔒 Riddle to unlock the answers

"I'm the step-by-step procedure for an experiment. Someone else should be able to follow me exactly to get the same results. What am I called?"

✓ Correct order
  1. Place a beaker of water containing pondweed and 1 g of NaHCO₃ on the bench.
  2. Cover an inverted funnel over the pondweed and place an inverted test tube on top.
  3. Move the lamp to 10 cm from the beaker.
  4. Wait 1 minute for the rate to stabilise, then count bubbles for 1 minute.
  5. Repeat for distances of 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm.
  6. Repeat each distance 3 times and calculate a mean.
  7. Plot a graph of mean bubbles per minute against distance.

Tip: set up → IV → wait → DV → change IV → repeat → graph.

Risk assessment — Hazards & control measures

5 min
📘 Hazard vs Risk vs Control
  • Hazard — something that could cause harm (e.g. a hot lamp).
  • Risk — what could actually happen (e.g. burning your hand).
  • Control measure — what you do to reduce the risk (e.g. don't touch the lamp; turn it off when not in use).

📘 Rules for a good results table
  • IV in the left column, DV in the right columns.

Evaluate & apply

8 min
📘 Evaluating an experiment — 4 key words
  • Accuracy — how close to the true value.
  • Precision — how close repeats are to each other.
  • Reliability — would you get the same results if you did it again? (Improved by repeats.)
  • Validity — were you measuring the right thing? (Improved by controlling CVs.)

(a) Suggest two limitations of counting bubbles by eye.
(b) Suggest two improvements to make the experiment more reliable or accurate.

Apply — your turn:
Plan an investigation into "How does CO₂ concentration affect the rate of photosynthesis in pondweed?"
In your book, write: IV (with units) · DV (with units) · 2 CVs · a hypothesis.

Hint
  • What goes wrong if you count bubbles too fast? Or if some are bigger than others?
  • Could a machine count better than a human?
  • For the apply task: what could you change to give the pondweed different amounts of CO₂?
Sentence stems
  • "One limitation is ____, because ____."
  • "This could be improved by ____, which would ____."
  • "For the new investigation, my IV would be ____."
🎯 Worked example — limitations for amylase practical

Limitation: "It's hard to spot the exact moment iodine stops turning blue-black, because the colour change is gradual. Improvement: use a colorimeter to measure the colour change objectively, giving more accurate timing."

🔒 Final riddle — last letter of the master code

"How close my results are to the true value. If my readings are far from the truth, my results have low ____. (Starts with A — 8 letters.)"

✓ Answers

(a) Two limitations of counting bubbles:

  • Bubbles can come too quickly to count by eye → human error.
  • Bubbles vary in size → counting them isn't a true measure of volume of O₂ produced.
  • It's hard to start/stop the count exactly on time.

(b) Improvements:

  • Collect the gas in an inverted measuring cylinder over water and measure volume of O₂ in cm³ per minute (more accurate).
  • Use a data logger with an oxygen sensor.
  • Do more repeats (5 instead of 3) and calculate a more reliable mean.
  • Use a light meter to measure light intensity directly (in lux), instead of distance.

Apply — CO₂ investigation:

  • IV: Mass of NaHCO₃ added to the water (g), e.g. 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5 g.
  • DV: Number of bubbles per minute (or volume of O₂ in cm³ per min).
  • CVs: Same pondweed, same light intensity (distance), same temperature, same volume of water, same time interval.
  • Hypothesis: "As the mass of NaHCO₃ added increases, the rate of photosynthesis will increase, because more CO₂ is dissolved in the water — CO₂ is a reactant for photosynthesis. The rate will eventually plateau when another factor becomes limiting."
