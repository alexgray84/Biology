01Mon

Gas Exchange — Thorax, Ventilation, Alveoli & Smoking

Dual Coding
What you need to know

The thorax contains the lungs, protected by the ribs and bounded by the diaphragm. Air flows: trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. Ventilation (breathing) is driven by the intercostal muscles (move the ribs) and the diaphragm. Alveoli are adapted for efficient gas exchange by diffusion. You must also know the biological consequences of smoking on both the lungs (tar → cancer, mucus build-up → chronic bronchitis, destruction of alveoli → emphysema) and the circulatory system (nicotine → raised blood pressure; carbon monoxide → reduced oxygen-carrying capacity of blood → coronary heart disease).

Tasks
  1. Thorax diagram (15 min): Draw and label the thorax from memory. Include: ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli, pleural membranes. Then annotate the pathway of air during inhalation and exhalation:
    • Inhalation: External intercostal muscles contract → ribs move up and out. Diaphragm contracts → flattens and moves down. Volume of thorax increases → pressure decreases → air rushes in.
    • Exhalation: External intercostal muscles relax → ribs move down and in. Diaphragm relaxes → domes upward. Volume decreases → pressure increases → air is pushed out.
  2. Alveolus diagram (10 min): Draw a single alveolus surrounded by a capillary network. Annotate the four key adaptations:
    • Large surface area (millions of alveoli)
    • Thin walls (one cell thick) = short diffusion distance
    • Rich blood supply = maintains steep concentration gradient
    • Moist lining = gases dissolve for diffusion
    Add arrows showing O2 diffusing into blood and CO2 diffusing into alveolus.
  3. Smoking effects summary (10 min): Create a two-column table: "Effect on lungs" and "Effect on circulatory system". Include:
    • Tar: Carcinogen → lung cancer. Damages cilia → mucus builds up → chronic bronchitis
    • Smoke particles: Destroy alveolar walls → emphysema → reduced surface area for gas exchange
    • Nicotine: Addictive, raises heart rate and blood pressure → increased risk of coronary heart disease
    • Carbon monoxide: Binds to haemoglobin (irreversibly) → reduces oxygen-carrying capacity → heart works harder → CHD
  4. Spaced retrieval from Weeks 1–2 (5 min): Write the balanced symbol equation for aerobic respiration. Name the enzyme that converts starch to maltose. Define osmosis.
Common mistake: Students confuse the mechanism of breathing with gas exchange. Breathing (ventilation) is the physical movement of air in and out of the lungs. Gas exchange is the diffusion of O2 and CO2 across the alveolar walls. They are different processes — the exam tests both separately.

02Tue

Transport in Plants — Xylem, Phloem & Why Organisms Need Transport

Comparison / Contrast
What you need to know

Simple unicellular organisms can rely on diffusion alone because they have a large surface area to volume ratio and a short diffusion distance. Multicellular organisms need a transport system because diffusion alone would be too slow over larger distances. In plants, xylem transports water and mineral ions from roots to leaves (one direction, upward). Phloem transports sucrose and amino acids from the leaves to other parts of the plant (translocation, bidirectional). You must also understand that CO2 and O2 are waste products of metabolism that leave through stomata.

Tasks
  1. SA:V ratio explanation (5 min): Explain in your own words why a single-celled organism like Amoeba does not need a transport system, but a human does. Use the terms: surface area to volume ratio, diffusion distance, metabolic demand.
  2. Xylem vs phloem comparison table (15 min): Draw a table from memory with columns: Feature | Xylem | Phloem. Fill in the following rows:
    • Substance transported (water & mineral ions vs sucrose & amino acids)
    • Direction of transport (roots → leaves vs leaves → all parts)
    • Type of cells (dead, hollow, thick walls, no end walls vs living cells with sieve plates)
    • Process name (transpiration stream vs translocation)
  3. Stem cross-section sketch (10 min): Draw a simplified cross-section of a stem showing the arrangement of xylem (inner) and phloem (outer) within vascular bundles. Label both tissues. This visual is often shown in exam diagrams.
  4. Excretion in plants (5 min): Write from memory: What are the excretory products of plants? (CO2 from respiration, O2 from photosynthesis.) How are they removed? (Diffusion through stomata in leaves.)
  5. Past paper questions (10 min): Complete 2–3 questions on plant transport from the resources below.
  6. Spaced retrieval (5 min): From Week 2: name the two roles of bile. Write the word equation for anaerobic respiration in yeast.
Spec precision: The Double Award spec says phloem transports "sucrose and amino acids." Do not write "glucose" — plants convert glucose to sucrose for transport. This distinction is tested.

03Wed

Blood, Immune Response & the Heart

Retrieval Practice
What you need to know

Blood is composed of red blood cells (transport O2), white blood cells (fight infection), platelets (clotting), and plasma (liquid that carries dissolved substances). Red blood cells are adapted: biconcave disc shape (large SA), no nucleus (more room for haemoglobin), contain haemoglobin (binds O2). The immune response involves phagocytes (engulf/ingest pathogens) and lymphocytes (produce antibodies specific to the pathogen). You must also know the structure and function of the heart as a double pump, and how heart rate changes with exercise and adrenaline.

Tasks
  1. Blood composition summary (10 min): Create four flashcards — one for each blood component: red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, plasma. On the back of each, write: structure, function, and one key fact. For plasma, list the substances it transports: CO2, digested food (glucose, amino acids), urea, hormones, and heat energy.
  2. RBC adaptations (5 min): List three adaptations of red blood cells from memory and explain how each helps with oxygen transport. Check against the spec.
  3. Immune response diagram (10 min): Draw two simple annotated diagrams:
    • Phagocytosis: Phagocyte detects pathogen → engulfs it → digests it inside the cell
    • Lymphocyte response: Lymphocyte recognises specific antigen on pathogen → produces antibodies that are specific/complementary to that antigen → antibodies bind to pathogen and destroy/neutralise it
  4. Heart diagram (15 min): Draw and label the heart from memory. Include: left atrium, right atrium, left ventricle, right ventricle, aorta, vena cava, pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein, valves, septum. Add arrows showing the direction of blood flow. Then answer:
    • Why is the left ventricle wall thicker than the right? (Pumps blood all around the body at higher pressure vs only to the lungs)
    • What is the role of valves? (Prevent backflow of blood)
    • Why is it called a double circulatory system? (Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit — once to the lungs, once to the body)
  5. Heart rate (5 min): Explain how exercise and adrenaline increase heart rate, and why this is necessary (more O2 and glucose delivered to muscles for increased respiration).
  6. Spaced retrieval (5 min): List four adaptations of alveoli. Name the substance transported by phloem.
Exam tip: Heart diagrams are drawn as if you are looking at the person, so the left side appears on the right of the diagram. Always label from the diagram's perspective. The exam frequently gives an unlabelled heart and asks you to identify chambers and vessels — practise drawing this until it's automatic.

04Thu

Blood Vessels, Circulation, CHD Risk Factors & Excretion

Self-Explanation
What you need to know

You need to relate the structure of arteries (thick muscular walls, small lumen, carry blood away from heart at high pressure), veins (thinner walls, larger lumen, valves to prevent backflow, carry blood toward heart at low pressure), and capillaries (one cell thick, tiny, allow exchange of substances) to their function. You need the general structure of the circulatory system including vessels to and from the heart, lungs, liver and kidneys. Coronary heart disease risk factors include diet (high fat), smoking, lack of exercise, and genetic factors. For excretion, know the excretory products of lungs (CO2), kidneys (urea, excess water/salts), and skin (water, salts in sweat).

Tasks
  1. Blood vessel comparison table (15 min): Draw a table from memory: Feature | Artery | Vein | Capillary. Rows: wall thickness, lumen size, valves, blood pressure, direction of flow relative to heart, key function. Alongside, draw a cross-section of each vessel type showing relative wall thickness and lumen size.
  2. Circulation diagram (10 min): Draw a simplified diagram of the double circulatory system showing: heart (with left and right sides), lungs, body organs. Draw arrows for blood flow and label:
    • Pulmonary artery (heart → lungs, deoxygenated)
    • Pulmonary vein (lungs → heart, oxygenated)
    • Aorta (heart → body, oxygenated)
    • Vena cava (body → heart, deoxygenated)
    • Include the liver (hepatic artery, hepatic vein, hepatic portal vein) and kidneys (renal artery, renal vein)
  3. CHD risk factors (5 min): List the specific factors that may increase the risk of coronary heart disease. For each, explain in one sentence how it increases risk. Self-explain aloud if possible.
  4. Excretion definitions and products (10 min): Define excretion (the removal of waste products of metabolism from the body). Then create a table: Organ of excretion | Excretory product(s). Include: lungs (CO2), kidneys (urea in urine, excess water and salts), skin (water and salts in sweat). Also note: in plants, CO2 and O2 are lost through stomata.
  5. Spaced retrieval (5 min): Draw a quick labelled sketch of an alveolus. Name the four components of blood.
Common mistake: Excretion is NOT the same as egestion. Excretion is the removal of metabolic waste (substances produced by chemical reactions in cells). Egestion is the removal of undigested food (faeces) from the body — faeces were never part of the body's metabolism. The exam specifically tests whether you know this distinction.

05Fri

Coordination — Nervous System, Reflex Arc, Eye & Hormones

Dual Coding + Retrieval
What you need to know

Organisms respond to changes in their environment. Coordinated responses require a stimulus, a receptor, and an effector. The nervous system provides fast, short-lived, precise responses using electrical impulses along nerves. The hormonal (endocrine) system provides slower, longer-lasting, widespread responses using chemical messengers in the blood. The CNS (brain + spinal cord) coordinates responses. You need the structure of a reflex arc (receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → effector), the role of neurotransmitters at synapses, the structure and function of the eye (focusing, light adaptation), and the five named hormones: adrenaline, insulin, testosterone, progesterone, oestrogen.

Tasks
  1. Nervous vs hormonal table (5 min): Compare the two communication systems from memory: speed of response, duration of response, transmission method (electrical impulses along nerves vs chemicals in blood), precision of response (specific target vs widespread).
  2. Reflex arc diagram (10 min): Draw and label a complete reflex arc for the example of withdrawing a finger from a hot object. Include: receptor (in skin) → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector (muscle). Add a synapse between each neurone and annotate: "Neurotransmitter is released across the synapse to stimulate the next neurone."
  3. Eye diagram (15 min): Draw and label the structure of the eye from memory. Include: cornea, iris, pupil, lens, ciliary muscles, suspensory ligaments, retina, optic nerve. Then annotate:
    • Focusing on near objects: Ciliary muscles contract → suspensory ligaments slacken → lens becomes fat/more curved → more refraction
    • Focusing on distant objects: Ciliary muscles relax → suspensory ligaments pull tight → lens is pulled thin/less curved → less refraction
    • Bright light: Circular muscles in iris contract → pupil constricts → less light enters
    • Dim light: Radial muscles in iris contract → pupil dilates → more light enters
  4. Hormone summary table (10 min): Create a table: Hormone | Source | Role/Effect. Fill in from memory:
    • Adrenaline: Adrenal glands → prepares body for "fight or flight" (increases heart rate, blood glucose)
    • Insulin: Pancreas → lowers blood glucose level (converts glucose to glycogen for storage)
    • Testosterone: Testes → controls male secondary sexual characteristics
    • Progesterone: Ovaries → maintains uterus lining during menstrual cycle/pregnancy
    • Oestrogen: Ovaries → controls female secondary sexual characteristics; involved in menstrual cycle
  5. Spaced retrieval (5 min): Draw a quick cross-section of an artery and a vein, labelling wall thickness and lumen size. Define excretion.
Spec precision: When describing the reflex arc, always state that the response is rapid and automatic — it does not involve conscious thought. This is because the impulse travels to the spinal cord, not the brain. The exam often asks why reflex actions are important (they protect the body from harm before the brain has time to process the stimulus).
Memory trick for the eye: For near objects, think "contract, slack, fat" (ciliary muscles Contract, ligaments Slack, lens Fat). For distant objects, think "relax, tight, thin" (muscles Relax, ligaments Tight, lens Thin). Students frequently get the ciliary muscle action backwards — practise this until it's automatic.

Homeostasis, Plant Responses & Weekly Consolidation

Practice Testing
What you need to know

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment. Body water content and body temperature are both examples. Temperature regulation involves the skin: sweating (evaporation cools the body), vasodilation (blood vessels near skin surface widen → more heat lost by radiation), and vasoconstriction (blood vessels narrow → less heat lost). In plants, responses to stimuli include phototropism (growth towards light) and geotropism (growth in response to gravity). Auxin is a plant hormone that causes phototropic responses in stems by moving to the shaded side, causing cells to elongate more on that side, bending the stem towards light.

Tasks
  1. Thermoregulation annotated diagram (15 min): Draw a simplified skin cross-section and annotate what happens when the body is:
    • Too hot: Sweat glands produce more sweat (evaporation removes heat energy); blood vessels near surface vasodilate (widen) so more blood flows near the skin surface, increasing heat loss by radiation; hairs lie flat
    • Too cold: Sweat production decreases; blood vessels vasoconstrict (narrow) so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss; shivering occurs (muscle contraction generates heat); hairs stand up (trapping insulating layer of air)
  2. Plant responses (10 min): Define phototropism and geotropism. Draw a diagram showing a plant shoot growing towards a light source from one side. Annotate: auxin moves to the shaded side → cells on shaded side elongate more → shoot bends towards the light. Describe root responses too: roots show positive geotropism (grow downward with gravity) and negative phototropism.
  3. Traffic-light spec checklist (5 min): Go through every spec point from 2.46–2.94. Mark green/amber/red. This week covers a huge amount of content — be honest about gaps.
  4. Re-revise red/amber areas (5 min): Focus specifically on your weakest areas. Re-draw diagrams, re-test definitions.
  5. Timed mini-test (20 min): Under exam conditions, answer:
    1. Describe the role of the intercostal muscles and diaphragm during inhalation. (4 marks)
    2. Explain two adaptations of alveoli for efficient gas exchange. (4 marks)
    3. Draw a table comparing xylem and phloem. Include: substance transported, direction, and one structural feature. (3 marks)
    4. Describe how phagocytes respond to a pathogen entering the body. (2 marks)
    5. Explain why the left ventricle wall is thicker than the right ventricle wall. (2 marks)
    6. Describe and explain how the body responds when core body temperature rises above normal. (4 marks)
    7. Explain how auxin causes a plant shoot to grow towards a light source. (3 marks)
  6. Self-mark (5 min): Use the specification content as your mark scheme. Be strict — vague statements do not earn marks.
