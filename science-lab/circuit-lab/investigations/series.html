A · One, two, then three lamps

Adding series lamps increases total resistance, so current falls. Expected battery currents are about 0.594, 0.298 and 0.199 A; Lamp 1 power is about 3.53, 0.891 and 0.397 W. Small wire/meter losses affect the last digits. Before and after Lamp 1, steady current magnitudes match: charge is conserved and current is not used up. The lamp transfers energy. A belongs in the path; V belongs across the lamp.

B · Add a resistor to the same loop

All three components share the same series current. Increasing resistance decreases that current and reduces the power of both unchanged lamps. Expected current is about 0.199, 0.150 and 0.100 A for total load resistances 30, 40 and 60 Ω. Each drop is I × its resistance; the larger resistor takes a larger share of the voltage. The component drops plus small wire/switch/meter drops add to measured source-terminal voltage. Use terminal voltage, which is slightly below the source setting. The graph decreases but is not a straight-line proportional relationship: current depends on the reciprocal of total loop resistance, including both lamps.

C · Find the fault and infer a rule

Opening the common switch breaks the only conducting path. Both lamp currents and powers become zero. Nearly the full 6 V is across the open switch: a potential difference can exist without a current. Voltage is energy per charge, whereas current is charge flowing per second. A voltmeter connected across the gap reads the voltage; it does not restore a conducting path in this ideal-meter model.

Challenge answer

The claim is supported for identical lamps added in series with an unchanged source. It is not a universal rule: three lamps could be in parallel, the source voltage could change, or lamp resistances could differ. Name the connection and controlled variables before predicting brightness; compare measured lamp power rather than appearance alone.
