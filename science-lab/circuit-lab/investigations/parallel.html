A · One, two, then three branches

Battery current equals the sum of the branch currents, within rounding. Adding branches reduces equivalent resistance and increases battery current: about 0.594 A for one branch, 1.175 A for two and roughly 1.74 A for three, depending on shared leads. Equal branches have approximately equal currents. Each lamp spans nearly the same voltage as the source terminals, so its power is nearly unchanged. More source current causes slightly greater internal voltage loss; the model therefore shows a small reduction in lamp voltage/power.

B · Do unequal branches share equally?

Current only divides equally for equal branch resistances at the same voltage. With a 10 Ω lamp and 20 Ω resistor, the lamp current is approximately twice the resistor current; at 40 Ω it is approximately four times. Both branches connect across the same two junctions, so their voltages are nearly equal. I = V/R explains the different currents. Their currents still sum to battery current. Small lead/internal losses mean the lamp current can change slightly when the other branch changes. The resistor-branch graph decreases approximately as 1/R; the three points do not imply a straight-line proportional relationship.

C · Disconnect a branch, then the supply

Disconnecting the resistor branch stops its current but leaves the lamp’s complete supply path intact. Battery current decreases; lamp power can rise slightly as source losses fall. Opening the common switch interrupts the supply path shared by both branches, stopping both currents. For independent control, each lamp needs its own switch in its own parallel branch. Tests with one on and the other off demonstrate independence; a common switch cannot do this.

Challenge answer

A separate series switch in each parallel lamp branch gives on/on, on/off, off/on and off/off. The mixed states—on/off and off/on—are the essential evidence that each light can be controlled independently. Both-on and both-off alone could be produced by a common switch.
