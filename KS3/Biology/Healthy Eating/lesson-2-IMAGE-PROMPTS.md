## Shared style (paste at the start of every prompt for a consistent set)

> Clean, modern flat-vector educational illustration for a school science lesson, audience age 12–14. Minimal and uncluttered, bold simple shapes, consistent thin line weight, friendly. White background. Clear, legible labels in a simple sans-serif. Colour palette: deep navy #0b1b3a, pitch green #10b981, magenta #ec1c6e, electric teal #06b6c4, sunny amber #f7a823, violet #7c3aed. Subtle football/World Cup motif allowed but never the focus. No photorealism, no clutter, no watermark.

**Fixed colour code — keep identical across all images** (this is the dual-coding payoff): starch/carbohydrate = **amber**, protein = **magenta**, amylase = **teal**, protease = **violet**, simple sugars = **light yellow**, amino acids = **pink beads**.

## 1. `digestion-hook.png` — Tab 1 (Do Now hook)
> [shared style] A cartoon footballer in kit eating a bowl of pasta, with a bold amber arrow leading from the bowl into a simple human body silhouette. Inside the body, the stomach/gut area is marked with a large question mark. Caption space at the bottom. Conveys "where does the food go?"

## 2. `digestive-system.png` — Tab 3 (canonical journey diagram, reused all lesson)
> [shared style] A clear labelled diagram of the human digestive system as a simple "journey": mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine. Each part clearly labelled with a label line touching the part itself (not in a separate key). Numbered 1–5 to show the order food travels. Clean and simple enough for a Year 8 student to copy. Landscape.

## 3. `surface-area.png` — Tab 4 (mechanical digestion)
> [shared style] Two side-by-side panels showing the idea of increasing surface area. Left: one large whole piece of food (amber) labelled "before chewing — small surface area". Right: the same food broken into many small pieces (amber) labelled "after chewing — large surface area", with small arrows pointing at the newly exposed surfaces. A simple tooth icon between them.

## 4. `enzymes-scissors.png` — Tab 5 (chemical digestion, named enzymes)
> [shared style] Two stacked rows showing enzymes as "molecular scissors". Top row: a pair of TEAL scissors labelled "amylase" cutting a long AMBER chain labelled "starch" into small LIGHT-YELLOW circles labelled "sugars". Bottom row: a pair of VIOLET scissors labelled "protease" cutting a MAGENTA chain labelled "protein" into small PINK beads labelled "amino acids". Clean, diagrammatic, clearly labelled.

## 5. `wholegrain-vs-refined.png` — Tab 6 (why whole foods are healthier)
> [shared style] Two side-by-side cross-sections of a grain. Left, labelled "wholegrain": shows the outer bran/fibre layer and germ intact, labelled "fibre kept — digested slowly". Right, labelled "refined / white": the bran and germ stripped away leaving only the pale starchy centre, labelled "fibre removed — digested fast". Simple and clear.

## 6. `energy-release-graph.png` — Tab 6 (slow-release vs spike)
> [shared style] A clean line graph. X-axis "time (minutes)", Y-axis "blood sugar". One green line labelled "wholegrain — slow, steady release" stays fairly flat and lasts across the graph. One red line labelled "refined/sugary — quick spike then crash" rises sharply then drops below the green line. A subtle vertical marker at "90 mins (full time)". Educational, easy to read.

## 7. `history-beaumont.png` — Tab 7 (history of digestion)
> [shared style but in a muted sepia/vintage tone to signal "history"] A respectful 1820s scene: an army surgeon in period dress observing and taking notes beside a seated patient, with a small dotted-circle inset over the patient's stomach area indicating a healed opening used for observation. Educational and dignified, NOT gory or graphic — no blood, no wounds shown. Caption space for "William Beaumont & Alexis St. Martin, 1822".

## 8. `world-staple-carbs-map.png` — Tab 7 (geography of food)
> [shared style] A simple flat world map (no fine detail). Small clear icons placed on regions for staple carbohydrate crops: rice in Asia, wheat in Europe, maize/corn in the Americas, potato in South America, cassava in Africa — each with a tiny label. The three 2026 World Cup host nations (USA, Canada, Mexico) outlined or flagged with a small football icon. Landscape.
