## Objective and success criteria

**Objective:** Describe mechanical and chemical digestion.

Tiered success criteria (your T1/T2/T3 model):

- **T1 (secure):** State that mechanical digestion physically breaks food up and chemical digestion uses enzymes to break it down. Name one example of each.
- **T2 (core):** Describe how mechanical and chemical digestion work together, explaining how chewing/churning increases surface area so enzymes work faster.
- **T3 (stretch):** Explain the role of named enzymes (amylase → starch into sugars; protease → protein into amino acids) and use this to explain why whole foods give a footballer steadier energy than refined foods.

## Lesson sequence (9 tabs, mirrors Lesson 1)

**1. Kick off (Do Now)** — retrieval, ~5 min
Interleaved recall from Lesson 1 (which food group is the body's main energy source? what does protein do?) plus a hook question: "A player eats pasta two hours before kick-off. Trace where it goes." Image: the hook diagram (food → body silhouette with a question mark gut).

**2. Key words** — ~4 min
digestion, mechanical digestion, chemical digestion, enzyme, amylase, protease, surface area, soluble, absorption. Colour-coded to match the diagrams. Quick "match the word" retrieval.

**3. The journey (main input)** — dual-coded, ~10 min
The digestive system as a "stadium tour" the food takes: mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine. Labelled canonical diagram, built up stage by stage (dual coding move 6). Big idea framed up front: digestion = breaking big, insoluble food into small, soluble molecules the blood can absorb to fuel the player.

**4. Mechanical digestion** — input + short *doing* activity, ~8 min
Teeth chew, stomach churns: physically breaks food into smaller pieces, increasing **surface area**. Quick hands-on surface-area model (e.g. one whole sugar cube vs a crushed one dissolving in water, or cut vs whole — teacher demo or pair task). Image: surface area (whole lump vs many small pieces with more exposed surface).

**5. Chemical digestion + enzymes (the practical)** — research/doing, ~12 min
The **cracker test**: chew a plain cream cracker and hold it; salivary **amylase** breaks starch into sugar, so it tastes sweet. Students record what they notice, then explain it. Enzyme idea introduced as "molecular scissors": amylase cuts starch (amber) into sugars; protease cuts protein (magenta) into amino acids. Images: enzyme-as-scissors for amylase and for protease, consistent colours. Tiered task: F describe what happened; C explain why using "enzyme/amylase/starch"; S predict the result if the cracker were wholemeal vs white and justify.

**6. Whole foods vs refined (the extension link)** — critical thinking, ~8 min
Why whole foods are healthier: the fibre and intact structure of wholegrains are broken down **slowly**, giving a steady "slow-release" energy supply across 90 minutes; refined/white foods are digested fast, spiking then crashing blood sugar. Image: wholegrain (bran/fibre intact) vs refined (stripped) cross-section + a blood-sugar-over-time line graph (slow-release vs spike). Critical-thinking task: read the graph and decide which bread a player should eat before extra time, and justify from the graph.

**7. Time detectives & the world plate (interdisciplinary)** — research + critical thinking + creativity, ~10 min
- **History (web-verified — see facts box below):** "How do we even know what happens inside a stomach?" A timeline of changing evidence: **Galen** (c. 2nd century) thought the stomach was like a cauldron that "cooked" (concocted) food using body heat → **Spallanzani** (1780s) swallowed perforated tubes and sponges and showed digestion is a *chemical* process, not just crushing → **Beaumont & St. Martin** (1822–33) observed gastric juice working directly through a healed opening in a patient's stomach. Critical-thinking prompt: how did the *evidence* get stronger each time, and was Beaumont's work ethical (he housed and arguably exploited his patient over years)?
- **Geography:** "Where does the world's energy come from?" Map of staple **carbohydrate crops** — rice (Asia), wheat (Europe), **maize (Mexico — a 2026 host)**, potato, cassava. Link to host nations and to whole vs refined (whole grains vs polished/refined versions). Image: world map of staple carbs with the three host nations flagged.

**8. Training ground (activities)** — creativity + critical thinking, ~ flexible
A choice board of "doing" tasks:
- *Create:* design a labelled comic strip or infographic "The Journey of the Pre-Match Pasta," showing mechanical then chemical digestion with the named enzymes (creativity + dual coding by the student).
- *Critical thinking (AI lab):* "Mark the AI / Spot the hallucination" on a digestion paragraph (reuse the format from Lesson 1's Training Ground; planted enzyme errors to catch).
- *Apply:* design a slow-release pre-match meal and explain the digestion science behind the choice.

**9. Full time (plenary / exit)** — ~3 min
Draw-and-label retrieval of the journey from memory; 2 MCQs (mechanical vs chemical; what amylase does); 3-2-1 exit ticket.

## Verified history facts box (safe to teach)

- **Galen (c. 129–216 AD):** taught that the stomach worked like a cauldron, "concocting" (cooking) food with body heat into a juice (chyle) that passed to the liver to become blood. Wrong, but the dominant view for ~1,500 years.
- **Lazzaro Spallanzani (1780s):** swallowed small perforated wooden tubes and sponges holding food; showed food was *dissolved* by gastric juice (a chemical process), not merely crushed mechanically. Published 1780.
- **William Beaumont & Alexis St. Martin (1822 onward):** St. Martin, a Canadian fur trader, survived an accidental musket wound that healed leaving a permanent fistula (opening) into his stomach. US Army surgeon Beaumont ran ~238 experiments (lowering food on string into the stomach, sampling gastric juice) between 1825 and 1833, publishing *Experiments and Observations on the Gastric Juice and the Physiology of Digestion* (1833). Widely flagged today as ethically troubling — St. Martin was a poor, dependent patient who could not easily refuse.
- **Teaching angle:** the through-line is *how the evidence improved* — from reasoning (Galen) to controlled experiment (Spallanzani) to direct observation (Beaumont). That is the critical-thinking spine of the section.

## Design spec — fixed "slide" mode (build requirement)

This lesson is **not** a scrolling page. Each section behaves like a presentation slide:

- Each section fills the screen (target one viewport, `100dvh`); the page itself does **not** scroll up and down.
- A fixed container holds: the lesson title/nav at top, the active slide in the middle, and the Prev/Next + tab controls. Only the active slide's content shows.
- **All interaction happens inside the slide.** Buttons within a slide swap or reveal content in place (e.g. "Mechanical → Chemical" toggle, reveal-answer, tier tabs, next-stage of the diagram). Content must never push the slide off-screen.
- Author each slide to *fit* one screen: concise text, responsive type (`clamp`), and where a topic is content-heavy (e.g. enzymes), break it into in-slide steps revealed by buttons rather than stacking it all vertically.
- Sizing must hold on a classroom projector (16:9) and a student laptop. If a slide ever exceeds the height, only an inner content area may scroll — never the whole page. Aim to design so even that is not needed.
- Horizontal nav (the tab bar) still jumps between slides; arrow keys / Prev-Next advance them.
