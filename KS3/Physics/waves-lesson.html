What Is a Wave?

In everyday English, "wave" can mean lots of things — waving hello, a Mexican wave at a football match. But in physics, it has a very precise scientific meaning.

Key Definition

In physics, a wave is an oscillation that transfers energy from one place to another.

An oscillation is a repeated back-and-forth movement. Waves transfer energy without transferring matter — the particles vibrate around a fixed position but don't travel with the wave.

💡 Think About It

A Mexican wave goes around a football stadium — but do the people actually move around the stadium? No! They stand up and sit down — they oscillate in place. The wave carries energy around the stadium, but the people stay where they are.

This is exactly how a physics wave works. Energy travels. Matter doesn't.

Transverse Waves

There are two main types of wave. Let's start with transverse waves. Pay close attention to the direction the particles move compared to the direction the wave travels.

Key Definition

In a transverse wave, the oscillation is perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of energy transfer.

The particles vibrate up and down ↕ while the wave travels horizontally →.

Examples: water waves, light, microwaves, X-rays, gamma rays, infrared — all electromagnetic waves are transverse.

Properties of a Wave

Scientists use four key words to describe the features of a transverse wave. Learn these definitions carefully — you'll need them for the rest of the lesson.

Peak / CrestThe highest point on a transverse wave above the rest position.
TroughThe lowest point on a transverse wave below the rest position.
Wavelength (λ)The distance between one point on a wave and the same point on the next wave (e.g. peak to peak).
AmplitudeThe maximum displacement from the rest position to a peak or trough. Greater amplitude = more energy.

Frequency

Frequency tells us how quickly waves are being produced. Imagine clicking your fingers — if you click 3 times in one second, that's a frequency of 3 Hz.

Key Definition

Frequency = the number of waves passing a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz).

frequency = number of waves ÷ time (seconds)

Example: 12 waves pass in 4 seconds → frequency = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 Hz

Longitudinal Waves

The second type of wave works differently. In a longitudinal wave, the particles vibrate in the same direction as the wave travels — not at right angles.

Key Definition

In a longitudinal wave, the oscillation is parallel to the direction of energy transfer.

This creates areas of compression (particles pushed together) and rarefaction (particles spread apart).

Examples: Sound waves, P-waves (earthquake primary waves), waves in a slinky pushed along its length.

Speed of Sound

Sound travels at about 340 m/s in air — roughly 1 km every 3 seconds. That's why you see lightning before you hear thunder!

Key Equation
speed = distance ÷ time

Speed in m/s, distance in m, time in s.

⚠️ Echo problems: Sound travels to the wall and back. Total distance = 2 × distance to wall.

Worked Example — Echo

A person stands 170 m from a cliff. They clap and hear the echo after 1 second.

Total distance = 170 × 2 = 340 m

Speed = 340 ÷ 1 = 340 m/s

🟢 Foundation
