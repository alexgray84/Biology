Understanding Waves

Exploring how energy transfers through oscillations — from ripples in water to the sound of your voice. By the end of this lesson, you will understand the key properties of waves and how to describe them scientifically.

1 Describe waves as transfers of energy through oscillations and identify examples of waves in everyday life.
2 Identify and describe the properties of transverse and longitudinal waves, including amplitude, wavelength and frequency.
3 Measure and calculate wave properties including frequency and wave speed from given data.
4 Compare transverse and longitudinal waves, relating particle motion to the direction of energy transfer.

What Is a Wave?

🎓 Teacher Guidance — Explicit Instruction

"I want everyone looking at me and listening carefully. I'm going to teach you the scientific definition of a wave. This is important because in everyday English, 'wave' means lots of things — waving hello, a Mexican wave at a football match. But in physics, it has a very precise meaning."
  • GTT Strategy: Explicit instruction with modelling — define, then exemplify, then check understanding
  • Use the "I do — We do — You do" model
  • Demonstrate with a slinky spring if available
  • Emphasise that waves transfer energy, not matter

Key Definition

In physics, a wave is an oscillation that transfers energy from one place to another.

An oscillation is a repeated back-and-forth movement. Waves transfer energy without transferring matter — the particles vibrate around a fixed position but don't travel with the wave.

💡 Checking Understanding — Hinge Question

"Before we move on, I need to check everyone understands the key idea. A Mexican wave goes around a stadium — but do the people actually move around the stadium? No! They stand up and sit down — they oscillate. The wave carries energy around the stadium, but the people stay put. This is exactly how a physics wave works."

✓ Correct! Waves transfer energy, not matter. The particles oscillate but stay in roughly the same position.
✗ Not quite. Remember — waves transfer energy. The particles vibrate but don't actually travel with the wave.

Transverse Waves

🎓 Teacher Guidance — Dual Coding

"Now we know what a wave is, we need to learn there are two main types. We'll start with transverse waves. Watch me carefully with the slinky spring — I'm going to move one end up and down while keeping the other end still. Notice the direction my hand moves versus the direction the wave travels."
  • GTT Strategy: Dual coding — combine verbal explanation with visual demonstration
  • Demo: Use a slinky, rope, or the interactive animation below
  • Emphasise the perpendicular relationship between vibration and wave travel

Key Definition

In a transverse wave, the oscillation is perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of energy transfer.

The particles vibrate up and down while the wave travels horizontally. Think of a Mexican wave — people stand up and sit down, but the wave moves sideways.

Examples: Water waves, light waves, microwaves, X-rays, gamma rays, infrared radiation — all electromagnetic waves are transverse.

  • Use the interactive diagram below to show each property
Peak / Crest The highest point on a transverse wave above the rest position.
Trough The lowest point on a transverse wave below the rest position.
Wavelength (λ) The distance between one point on a wave and the same point on the next wave (e.g. peak to peak).
Amplitude The maximum displacement from the rest position to a peak or trough. Greater amplitude = greater energy.

Frequency

🎓 Teacher Guidance

"Frequency tells us how many waves are produced every second. The unit is hertz — named after Heinrich Hertz. 1 Hz means 1 wave per second. If I click my fingers 3 times in one second, that's a frequency of 3 Hz."
  • GTT Strategy: Concrete-Representational-Abstract (CRA) approach
  • Demonstrate physically (clapping), then show on diagram, then introduce the formula

Key Definition

Frequency is the number of waves passing a point each second. It is measured in hertz (Hz).

frequency = number of waves ÷ time (seconds)

If 4 waves pass a point in 1 second → frequency = 4 Hz
If 12 waves pass a point in 4 seconds → frequency = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 Hz

Longitudinal Waves

🎓 Teacher Guidance — Contrasting Examples

"We've looked at transverse waves where the vibration is perpendicular. Now we're going to learn about the second type. In a longitudinal wave, the vibration is parallel — in the same direction as the wave. Sound is a longitudinal wave. When I speak, the air particles are pushed together and pulled apart in the same direction that the sound travels."
  • GTT Strategy: Contrasting examples — show non-example alongside example to sharpen understanding
  • Use a slinky: push and pull along its length (vs up/down for transverse)
  • Introduce compressions and rarefactions visually

Key Definition

In a longitudinal wave, the oscillation is parallel (in the same direction) to the direction of energy transfer.

The particles vibrate back and forth in the same direction the wave travels. This creates areas of compression (particles pushed together) and rarefaction (particles spread apart).

Examples: Sound waves, P-waves (earthquake primary waves), waves in a spring pushed along its length.

How Fast Does Sound Travel?

🎓 Teacher Guidance

"Sound travels at about 340 m/s in air — that's roughly 1 kilometre every 3 seconds. This is why you see lightning before you hear thunder. We can calculate the speed of sound using a simple equation."
  • GTT Strategy: Connecting to real-world context, then explicit equation teaching
  • Use the thunder/lightning example to make it concrete
  • Model the echo calculation step-by-step

Key Equation

speed = distance ÷ time

Speed is measured in metres per second (m/s). Distance in metres (m). Time in seconds (s).

⚠️ Echo warning: When measuring speed using an echo, the sound travels to the wall and back. So the total distance = 2 × distance to the wall.

Worked Example — Echo Calculation:

A person stands 170 m from a cliff. They clap and hear the echo after 1 second.

Total distance = 170 m × 2 = 340 m (to the cliff and back)

Speed = 340 m ÷ 1 s = 340 m/s
