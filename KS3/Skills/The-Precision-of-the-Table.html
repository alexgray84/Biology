Lesson 1: The Precision of the Table

Phase 1 — Activate & Connect (Retrieval)

Rapid retrieval of variable identification. You cannot build a table if you don't know what you are measuring.

Teacher Note — Setup
Cold-call students for these answers. Use the "I do, We do, You do" method if they struggle. Reinforce the definitions: Independent = "I change", Dependent = "Data I collect", Control = "Keep constant".
Retrieval Practice — Spot the Variable

Task: For the three scientific scenarios below, identify the Independent Variable (IV), the Dependent Variable (DV), and at least one Control Variable (CV).

Scenario 1: The Bouncing Ball

A student drops a tennis ball from 1 metre, 2 metres, and 3 metres to see how high it bounces back up.

Teacher Note — Answers
IV: Drop height. DV: Bounce height. CV: Type of ball, surface dropped on, person dropping it.

Scenario 2: The Thirsty Plant

A scientist gives three identical tomato plants different amounts of water each day (10ml, 50ml, 100ml) and measures their height after two weeks.

Teacher Note — Answers
IV: Amount of water. DV: Height of plant. CV: Type of plant, amount of light, soil type, temperature.

Phase 2 — The Golden Rules of Tables

Data is useless if it cannot be read clearly. Scientists follow strict rules so that anyone in the world can understand their results.

Key Knowledge — The 3 Golden Rules

Rule 1: Placement. The Independent Variable (IV) ALWAYS goes in the very first column on the left. The Dependent Variable (DV) goes in the columns to the right.

Rule 2: Units. Units (like cm, seconds, °C) only ever go in the column headers. NEVER write units inside the data boxes. If you write "5 cm" in a box, a computer cannot calculate an average because it sees letters, not numbers.

Rule 3: Consistency. All data in a single column must be recorded to the same number of decimal places, matching the precision of your measuring equipment.

Apply It: The "Messy" Table

Q1 3 marks
A Year 7 student has recorded the time it takes for sugar to dissolve in water at different temperatures. Look at their table below.
Time to dissolve Temperature of water
45 s 20
30.5 s 40 °C
12 seconds 60
Identify three errors the student has made in the design of their table, based on the Golden Rules.
Mark Scheme
  • The Independent Variable (Temperature) should be in the first column, not the second. (1)
  • Units (s and °C) have been written inside the data boxes instead of just in the headers. (1)
  • Inconsistent decimal places in the time column (45 vs 30.5). (1)
Examiner note: Emphasize that writing units in the body of a table is the most common reason students lose marks in practical exams. Numbers only in the boxes!

Phase 3 — The Reflex Challenge

You will now collect your own "Human Data" and record it with scientific precision.

Teacher Note — Differentiation & Scaffolding
Support: Draw the table frame on the board for students to copy: IV (Hand) on the left, Trial 1-5 in the middle, Mean on the right.
Stretch: Have students convert their distance (cm) into reaction time (s) using the formula t = √(2d/g) or provide a conversion chart.
Working Scientifically

The Ruler Drop Test

The Scientific Link: This tests your nervous system. Your eye sees the ruler drop, sends a signal to your brain, which sends a motor signal to your hand to close. We are testing if this pathway is faster in your dominant hand.

  1. Work in pairs. Person A holds a 30cm ruler vertically, with the 0cm mark exactly between Person B's open thumb and index finger.
  2. Without warning, Person A drops the ruler.
  3. Person B catches it as quickly as possible.
  4. Record the distance (in cm) where Person B's fingers caught the ruler.
  5. Repeat for a total of 5 trials on the Left Hand, and 5 trials on the Right Hand.
Task: Table Design & Data Collection — 20 Minutes

Before you begin dropping the ruler, you must design a perfect table using a pencil and ruler. Ensure it has space for your IV, your 5 repeats, and a final column to calculate your Mean. Once your table is drawn, collect your data.

Q2 4 marks
Look closely at your own data for Trial 1 compared to Trial 5. In many scientific experiments involving human performance, the first trial is often discarded as an "anomaly" or source of error.
(a) Calculate the mean distance for your dominant hand. (1)

(b) Explain why Trial 1 in this specific experiment might be unreliable and naturally slower than Trial 5. (2)

Mark Scheme
  • (a) Correct addition of 5 trials divided by 5. (1)
  • (b) "The Practice Effect" / The participant was not used to the motion yet. (1)
  • (b) By Trial 5, the nervous pathway has been primed / they know what to expect. (1)
  • (c) Allow the participant to have 2 "practice drops" before officially recording the data. (1)
Examiner note: This introduces the concept of methodology evaluation. Human data is notoriously messy, and anticipating human error is a key Working Scientifically skill.
