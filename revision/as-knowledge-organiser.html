Proteins · Reference

Proteins

Spec refs: 2.6(i–iv) · 2.7(i–iii)

Key Terms

TermDefinition
Amino acidMonomer of proteins. Central carbon bonded to an amine group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom and a variable R group. There are 20 different amino acids — the R group determines their properties.
Condensation reactionA reaction joining two monomers with the release of a water molecule. Used to form peptide bonds between amino acids.
Peptide bondA covalent bond formed between the –COOH of one amino acid and the –NH₂ of the next, releasing water. Written –CO–NH–.
Dipeptide / PolypeptideDipeptide = 2 amino acids joined. Polypeptide = many amino acids joined in a chain by peptide bonds.
Primary structureThe specific sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain. Determined by the gene. Dictates all higher levels of structure.
Secondary structureRegular, repeated folding of the polypeptide chain held by hydrogen bonds between –C=O and –N–H groups. Forms α-helices or β-pleated sheets.
Tertiary structureThe overall 3D shape of a single polypeptide, formed by further folding. Held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges and hydrophobic interactions.
Quaternary structureTwo or more polypeptide chains associated together. E.g. haemoglobin has 4 subunits.
Globular proteinSpherical, water-soluble, metabolically active protein. E.g. haemoglobin (O₂ transport), enzymes (catalysis).
Fibrous proteinLong, insoluble, structural protein with a regular repeating structure. E.g. collagen (in tendons, bone, skin).
Active siteA specific region on an enzyme with a shape complementary to its substrate. Where the enzyme–substrate complex forms.
EnzymeA biological catalyst made of protein. Reduces activation energy and is highly specific due to its active site shape.
Induced fitModel of enzyme action: the active site changes shape slightly when the substrate binds, improving the fit and speeding up the reaction.

Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts — they speed up reactions by lowering activation energy. They are not used up in reactions.

Specificity: Each enzyme has an active site with a shape complementary to one specific substrate. This is due to the tertiary structure of the protein.

Induced fit model: The active site is not rigid — it flexes slightly to fit the substrate, forming a more stable enzyme–substrate complex.

Intracellular vs extracellular: Intracellular enzymes work inside cells (e.g. enzymes of respiration). Extracellular enzymes are secreted and work outside cells (e.g. digestive enzymes like amylase, lipase).

Reproduction in
Flowering Plants

Spec ref: 3.13 · Fertilisation in flowering plants

Key Terms

TermDefinition
SepalLeaf-like structure at the base of a flower, usually green. Protects the developing flower bud. Collectively called the calyx.
PetalModified leaf, often brightly coloured and/or scented to attract insect pollinators. Collectively the corolla.
StamenMale reproductive organ. Consists of an anther (produces pollen) and a filament (stalk).
AntherThe structure at the top of the stamen that produces and releases pollen grains containing the male gametes.
FilamentThe stalk of the stamen that supports the anther and positions it for pollen dispersal.
Carpel (Pistil)Female reproductive organ. Consists of the stigma, style and ovary.
StigmaSticky surface at the top of the carpel that receives pollen grains during pollination.
StyleThe stalk connecting the stigma to the ovary. The pollen tube grows down the style.
OvaryEncloses the ovules. After fertilisation it develops into the fruit.
OvuleContains the female gamete (egg cell). After fertilisation it develops into the seed.
PollinationTransfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma. Self-pollination = same plant; cross-pollination = different plant.
Pollen tubeGrows from the pollen grain down through the style to the ovule. Carries the male nuclei to the egg cell.
Double fertilisationUnique to angiosperms. One sperm + egg cell → zygote (diploid). Second sperm + 2 polar nuclei → endosperm (triploid, food store).

Fertilisation — Step by Step

Spec 3.13: "Know the process of fertilisation in flowering plants, starting with the growth of a pollen tube and ending with the fusion of nuclei."

1
Pollination — pollen grain lands on the stigma of a compatible flower and germinates.
2
Pollen tube growth — a pollen tube grows down the style, digesting its way through the style tissue. Controlled by the pollen tube nucleus.
3
Generative nucleus divides — the generative nucleus in the pollen grain divides by mitosis to produce two sperm nuclei.
4
Pollen tube reaches ovule — the tube enters the ovule through the micropyle.
5
Double fertilisation — Sperm 1 + egg cell → zygote (diploid; develops into embryo). Sperm 2 + two polar nuclei → endosperm nucleus (triploid; develops into food store for seed).
6
Seed and fruit formation — the ovule develops into the seed; the ovary wall develops into the fruit.

Key Terms

TermDefinition
Gymnosperm"Naked seed" — seeds not enclosed in a fruit; produced in cones. E.g. conifers.
Angiosperm"Enclosed seed" — seeds enclosed within a fruit (developed from the ovary). E.g. all flowering plants.
Vascular tissueInternal transport tissue (xylem and phloem). Allows plants to grow taller and survive in drier conditions.
RhizoidHair-like structure anchoring bryophytes to the substrate; not a true root — no vascular tissue.
GametophyteThe haploid generation of a plant that produces gametes by mitosis.
SporophyteThe diploid generation of a plant that produces spores by meiosis.
BiodiversityThe variety of life in an area — includes species richness, genetic diversity, and habitat diversity.
EndemismA species found only in a specific geographic area and nowhere else on Earth.

Classification (Spec 4.14)

What is classification?

Classification organises the variety of life based on similarities and differences in phenotypes and genotypes. It is built around the species concept.

Three-domain system

Based on molecular evidence (rRNA sequences, DNA base sequences), life is divided into three domains: Archaea, Bacteria, and Eukarya. Plants belong to Eukarya.

Why does classification change?

New molecular evidence — e.g. DNA and protein sequences — reveals evolutionary relationships that cannot be seen from anatomy alone. New data is critically evaluated by the scientific community before changing taxonomic groupings.

Why does biodiversity matter?

Species-rich habitats are more stable, more productive, and provide more ecosystem services. Human activity (habitat destruction, pollution, over-exploitation) is reducing biodiversity at an unprecedented rate (Spec 4.15).

Endemism

Endemic species are uniquely vulnerable to extinction because they have no other population elsewhere. Islands and isolated habitats tend to have high endemism (e.g. Madagascar, Galápagos).

State the three domains in the three-domain system and explain why it replaced a two-kingdom system.

🔬 Proteins
Collagen fibres have very high tensile strength. How does the molecular structure of collagen account for this property?
Collagen has three polypeptide chains wound into a triple helix — a very regular, tightly coiled structure. The chains are cross-linked to each other. Helices bundle into fibrils, and fibrils into fibres. This regular, reinforced structure resists stretching forces.
