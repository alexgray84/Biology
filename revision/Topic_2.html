2.1 – 2.5

Gas Exchange & Membranes

2.1 (i)

State the three properties of gas exchange surfaces that determine the rate of diffusion.

  • Large surface area to volume ratio — more surface for diffusion to occur across
  • Thickness of surface — the thinner the surface, the shorter the diffusion distance and the faster the rate
  • Large concentration difference — steeper gradient drives faster diffusion
2.1 (ii)

State Fick's Law of Diffusion and explain how it relates to gas exchange surface properties.

Rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × concentration difference) / thickness

A larger surface area, greater concentration difference, and thinner surface all increase the rate of diffusion. Gas exchange surfaces are adapted to maximise these factors.

2.1 (iii)

How is the mammalian lung adapted for rapid gaseous exchange?

  • Large surface area — millions of alveoli provide a huge total surface
  • Thin walls — diffusion distance is very short: one layer of squamous alveolar epithelium (type I pneumocytes) + one layer of capillary endothelium, both extremely thin
  • Rich blood supply — capillaries constantly remove O₂ and deliver CO₂, maintaining the concentration gradient
  • Moist surface — gases dissolve in the moisture lining the alveoli for easier diffusion
  • Ventilation — breathing maintains a fresh supply of air, keeping the gradient steep
2.2 (i)

Describe the structure and properties of the cell-surface membrane according to the fluid mosaic model.

  • Phospholipid bilayer — two layers; hydrophilic heads face outward (aqueous environment), hydrophobic tails face inward
  • Fluid — phospholipids can move laterally, giving flexibility
  • Mosaic — proteins embedded throughout, in varying positions like tiles in a mosaic
  • Proteins may be intrinsic (span the bilayer) or extrinsic (on the surface)
  • Also contains cholesterol, glycoproteins, and glycolipids
Exam tipExplain why "fluid" and "mosaic" are both needed — each word refers to a different feature.
2.2 (ii)

What does the fluid mosaic model illustrate about how science works?

Scientific models are interpretations of data used to explain observations. The fluid mosaic model was developed and refined as new experimental evidence accumulated (e.g. electron microscopy, freeze-fracture studies). Models are provisional — they are accepted because they best explain the available data, but may be revised if new evidence contradicts them.

2.3 CP3

Describe how Core Practical 3 investigates membrane permeability. What do you measure and how?

Aim: Investigate the effect of alcohol concentration and temperature on membrane permeability using beetroot.

  • Cut equal-sized beetroot pieces; wash thoroughly to remove any released pigment
  • Immerse in different alcohol concentrations or different temperatures
  • After a set time, measure the amount of red pigment (betalain) released into the surrounding solution using a colorimeter (absorbance or % transmission)
  • More pigment released = more permeable / more damaged membrane
2.4

Define osmosis precisely using the terms water potential and partially permeable membrane.

Osmosis is the movement of free water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential, through a partially permeable membrane, down the water potential gradient.

Note: Pure water has the highest possible water potential (0 kPa). Adding solutes lowers water potential (more negative values).

2.5 (i)

Compare passive transport (diffusion and facilitated diffusion), active transport, endocytosis and exocytosis.

  • Diffusion — movement of molecules down a concentration gradient; no energy (ATP) required; no proteins required
  • Facilitated diffusion — down a concentration gradient; no ATP; uses channel or carrier proteins
  • Active transport — against a concentration gradient; requires ATP as an immediate source of energy; uses carrier proteins
  • Endocytosis — bulk uptake of material; cell membrane engulfs substance, forming a vesicle; requires energy
  • Exocytosis — bulk release of material; vesicles fuse with cell-surface membrane and release contents outside; requires energy
2.5 (ii)

What are the roles of channel proteins and carrier proteins in membrane transport?

  • Channel proteins — form water-filled pores spanning the bilayer; allow specific hydrophilic ions and small molecules to pass down their concentration gradient (facilitated diffusion)
  • Carrier proteins — bind specific molecules and change shape to transport them across; used in both facilitated diffusion and active transport (the latter requires ATP binding to change conformation)
2.6 – 2.8

Proteins & Enzymes

2.6 (i)

Describe the basic structure of an amino acid.

Every amino acid has a central carbon atom bonded to:

  • An amino group (–NH₂)
  • A carboxyl group (–COOH)
  • A hydrogen atom (–H)
  • A variable R group (side chain) — this differs between the 20 amino acids

Structures of specific amino acids are not required in this spec.

2.6 (ii)

How are amino acids joined to form polypeptides, and what type of bond is formed?

Amino acids are joined by condensation reactions: the carboxyl group (–COOH) of one amino acid reacts with the amino group (–NH₂) of the next, releasing a molecule of water and forming a peptide bond (–CO–NH–).

A chain of many amino acids linked this way is called a polypeptide.

2.6 (iii)

Explain how a protein's primary structure determines its secondary and three-dimensional structure, and state the bonds involved. Distinguish between globular and fibrous proteins.

  • Primary structure — the specific sequence of amino acids, held by peptide bonds. This sequence determines everything else.
  • Secondary structure — local folding of the chain into α-helix or β-pleated sheet; held by hydrogen bonds between the NH and C=O groups of the peptide backbone
  • Three-dimensional (tertiary) structure — the overall 3D folded shape; held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges (covalent bonds between cysteine R groups), and hydrophobic interactions

Globular proteins — folded into compact, roughly spherical shapes; soluble in water; metabolically active (e.g. enzymes, haemoglobin).
Fibrous proteins — elongated, repetitive structure; insoluble; structural roles (e.g. collagen, keratin).

Key pointA change in primary structure (even one amino acid) can alter the shape of the active site or the whole protein — this is how mutations cause disease.
2.6 (iv)

Compare the structure of haemoglobin and collagen, and relate each to their function.

Haemoglobin (globular):

  • Quaternary structure — 4 polypeptide chains (2α + 2β), each with a haem group containing Fe²⁺
  • Compact, soluble shape → packed inside red blood cells (erythrocytes) at very high concentration
  • Each haem group can bind one O₂ → carries 4 O₂ per molecule

Collagen (fibrous):

  • Three polypeptide chains wound into a triple helix, cross-linked between chains
  • Insoluble; high tensile strength
  • Structural role: tendons, cartilage, bone matrix, skin
2.7 (i–ii)

Explain enzyme specificity and how enzymes reduce activation energy.

Specificity: Each enzyme has an active site with a unique 3D shape, complementary to its specific substrate. Only the correct substrate can fit — like a lock and key (lock-and-key model). The more precise induced fit model refines this: the enzyme changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, improving the fit and straining bonds in the substrate. Either way, an enzyme-substrate complex forms, and only the complementary substrate can bind → specificity.

Activation energy: Enzymes are biological catalysts. They lower the activation energy (minimum energy needed to start a reaction) by stressing bonds in the substrate or bringing reactants into the correct orientation, allowing the reaction to proceed more quickly at lower temperatures.

2.7 (iii)

What is the distinction between intracellular and extracellular enzymes? Give an example of each.

  • Intracellular enzymes — catalyse reactions inside cells. Example: enzymes of glycolysis/respiration (e.g. ATP synthase in mitochondria)
  • Extracellular enzymes — secreted by cells and catalyse reactions outside cells. Examples: digestive enzymes such as amylase (secreted into saliva/pancreatic juice), protease (e.g. pepsin in stomach)
2.8 CP4

Core Practical 4: What four variables are tested for their effect on enzyme-catalysed reactions, and how is the initial rate measured?

Four variables: temperature, pH, enzyme concentration, and substrate concentration.

Measuring initial rate: The initial rate is the rate at the very start of the reaction (before substrate is significantly depleted). It is found from the gradient of a progress curve (product formed vs. time) at time zero, or by measuring product/substrate change over a very short initial time period.

Example methods: colorimetry, gas collection (O₂ or CO₂ volume), pH change.

2.9 – 2.12

Nucleotides, DNA & The Genetic Code

2.9 (i)

What are the components of a DNA nucleotide? How does an RNA nucleotide differ?

DNA nucleotide: deoxyribose sugar + phosphate group + one of four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), guanine (G).

RNA nucleotide differs in two ways:

  • Sugar is ribose (not deoxyribose)
  • Uses uracil (U) instead of thymine (T)

RNA is also single-stranded; DNA is double-stranded.

2.9 (i)

How are nucleotides joined in a polynucleotide chain? What bond is formed?

Nucleotides are joined by condensation reactions, forming phosphodiester bonds between the phosphate group of one nucleotide and the sugar (deoxyribose/ribose) of the next. Water is released. This forms the sugar-phosphate backbone of the polynucleotide strand.

2.9 (ii)

Describe complementary base pairing in DNA and explain how it forms the double helix.

  • A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds)
  • C pairs with G (3 hydrogen bonds)

These specific base pairings hold the two antiparallel strands together. The strands twist around each other forming a double helix. Hydrogen bonds are individually weak but collectively provide stability due to their large number.

Exam tipAlways specify the number of hydrogen bonds: A–T has 2, C–G has 3.
2.10 (i)

Describe the process of semi-conservative DNA replication, including the role of DNA polymerase.

  1. DNA helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between base pairs, unwinding the double helix and separating the two strands
  2. Each strand acts as a template — free nucleotides align opposite each exposed strand via complementary base pairing
  3. DNA polymerase catalyses the formation of phosphodiester bonds between nucleotides, building new complementary strands
  4. Each new DNA molecule contains one original strand and one new strand — hence semi-conservative
2.10 (ii)

Describe Meselson and Stahl's experiment. Explain how it supported the semi-conservative model and refuted the competing theories.

Method: Bacteria were grown in heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N) until all DNA was labelled heavy, then transferred to light nitrogen (¹⁴N) medium.

  • After 1 generation: All DNA had intermediate density (one ¹⁵N strand + one ¹⁴N strand)
  • After 2 generations: Half intermediate, half light (¹⁴N/¹⁴N)

This matched the semi-conservative prediction exactly. It ruled out:

  • Conservative model — would predict two separate bands after gen 1 (one all-heavy, one all-light)
  • Dispersive model — would predict a single intermediate-density band after gen 1 AND after gen 2 (no change between generations, getting slightly lighter each time)
Semi-conservative uniquely predicts intermediate after gen 1, then a split into intermediate + light after gen 2 — exactly what was observed.

2.11

Describe the three key features of the genetic code: triplet, non-overlapping and degenerate.

  • Triplet code — every three consecutive bases (a codon) on mRNA codes for one amino acid
  • Non-overlapping — each base belongs to only one codon; codons are read sequentially without sharing bases
  • Degenerate — most amino acids are coded for by more than one codon (there are 64 possible codons for only 20 amino acids, plus stop codons)
2.12

Define a gene.

A gene is a sequence of bases on a DNA molecule that codes for a sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain.

Note: genes also include non-coding regulatory sequences, but the spec definition focuses on the polypeptide-coding sequence.

2.13 – 2.14

Protein Synthesis & Mutations

2.13 (i–ii)

Describe the process of transcription in protein synthesis.

  1. RNA polymerase binds to a promoter region on the DNA and unwinds the double helix
  2. Free RNA nucleotides align opposite the template (antisense) strand by complementary base pairing (A–U, T–A, C–G, G–C)
  3. RNA polymerase catalyses the formation of phosphodiester bonds between nucleotides, building the mRNA strand
  4. The mRNA strand is complementary to the template strand (and identical in sequence to the sense strand, but with U instead of T)
  5. mRNA detaches and leaves the nucleus through nuclear pores to reach ribosomes
2.13 (i–ii)

Describe the process of translation in protein synthesis, including the roles of mRNA, tRNA, ribosomes, and start/stop codons.

  1. mRNA attaches to a ribosome; the start codon (AUG) is recognised
  2. tRNA molecules carry specific amino acids; each tRNA has an anticodon complementary to the mRNA codon
  3. The anticodon on tRNA base-pairs with the complementary codon on mRNA
  4. A peptide bond forms between adjacent amino acids (catalysed by the ribosome)
  5. The ribosome moves along the mRNA (5'→3'), reading each codon in turn
  6. When a stop codon (UAA, UAG, UGA) is reached, no tRNA binds; the ribosome releases the finished polypeptide
Key distinctionCodons are on mRNA; anticodons are on tRNA; the template strand is the DNA strand used in transcription.
2.14 (i)

Describe the three types of base mutation (substitution, insertion, deletion) and compare their effects on the polypeptide.

  • Substitution — one base replaced by another. Changes only one codon; may change one amino acid (missense), produce a stop codon (nonsense), or have no effect if the code is degenerate (silent)
  • Insertion — a base is added. Causes a frameshift — all codons downstream are shifted, changing many amino acids. Usually severe
  • Deletion — a base is removed. Also causes a frameshift downstream. Usually severe
RememberInsertions and deletions have more drastic effects than substitutions because they shift the reading frame for all subsequent codons.
2.14 (ii)

Why do many mutations have no observable effect? And what are the consequences when they do have an effect?

No observable effect because:

  • The genetic code is degenerate — different codons code for the same amino acid (silent mutation)
  • The mutation may be in a non-coding region of DNA
  • The amino acid change may not affect the protein's active site or function

When mutations do have effects:

  • They can give rise to cancer (e.g. mutations in tumour suppressor genes or proto-oncogenes)
  • They can cause genetic disorders (e.g. cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia)
2.15 – 2.18

Genetics, Inheritance & Screening

2.15 (i)

Define: gene, allele, genotype, phenotype, dominant, recessive, codominance, homozygote, heterozygote.

  • Gene — a sequence of DNA coding for a polypeptide
  • Allele — a different version of a gene (different base sequence)
  • Genotype — the alleles an organism possesses for a given gene (or genes)
  • Phenotype — the observable characteristics of an organism
  • Dominant — allele expressed in both homozygous and heterozygous individuals
  • Recessive — allele only expressed when homozygous (no dominant allele present)
  • Codominance — both alleles expressed equally in the phenotype of the heterozygote
  • Homozygote — has two identical alleles for a gene (e.g. AA or aa)
  • Heterozygote — has two different alleles for a gene (e.g. Aa)
2.15 (ii)

How do you interpret a genetic pedigree diagram to determine the pattern of inheritance of a trait?

Pedigree conventions: squares = males, circles = females, filled = affected, horizontal line = mating, vertical/branched lines = offspring.

Clues to look for:

  • If two unaffected parents have an affected child → trait is recessive
  • If every affected individual has at least one affected parent → likely dominant
  • If condition appears mainly in males and is passed through unaffected females → likely X-linked recessive
  • Use Punnett squares to check proposed genotypes are consistent with observed ratios
2.15 (iii)

What is sex linkage? Use red-green colour blindness to explain how X-linked recessive conditions are inherited.

Sex linkage: A gene located on a sex chromosome (usually X). Because males have only one X chromosome (XY), they have only one copy of X-linked genes — they cannot be heterozygous for X-linked traits.

Red-green colour blindness (X-linked recessive):

  • Alleles: XB (normal vision, dominant), Xb (colour blind, recessive)
  • Males: XbY → colour blind (only one copy needed)
  • Females: XbXb → colour blind; XBXb → carrier (unaffected); XBXB → normal
  • Carrier mothers pass the allele to 50% of sons (who will be colour blind) and 50% of daughters (who will be carriers)
2.16

How does the gene mutation in cystic fibrosis impair the gaseous exchange system?

A deletion mutation in the CFTR gene (most commonly ΔF508 — deletion of 3 bases coding for phenylalanine) results in a faulty or absent CFTR chloride channel protein.

In the lungs:

  • Cl⁻ ions cannot be transported out of cells
  • Water cannot follow by osmosis → thick, sticky mucus forms
  • Cilia cannot clear the mucus → mucus accumulates in airways
  • Bacteria colonise the stagnant mucus → recurrent infections and progressive lung damage
2.16

How does cystic fibrosis affect the digestive and reproductive systems?

Digestive system:

  • Thick mucus blocks the pancreatic duct
  • Digestive enzymes (lipase, protease, amylase) cannot reach the small intestine
  • Impaired digestion and absorption of nutrients, especially fats and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)

Reproductive system:

  • Males: the vas deferens may be blocked or absent → infertility
  • Females: thick cervical mucus impedes sperm entry → reduced fertility
2.17 (i)

What is carrier identification in genetic screening? Who is it used for, and why is it useful?

Carrier identification is a form of genetic screening used to detect individuals who carry one copy of a recessive allele for a genetic disorder without showing symptoms themselves.

  • Used when there is a family history of a recessive genetic disorder (e.g. cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia)
  • A blood or saliva sample is taken; DNA is analysed for the presence of the faulty allele
  • A carrier (heterozygote) has a 25% chance of having an affected child if their partner is also a carrier
  • Allows couples to make informed reproductive decisions — e.g. opting for PGD or prenatal testing
Spec linkThe spec lists carrier identification as a distinct use of genetic screening alongside PGD and prenatal testing — make sure you can describe all three separately.
2.17 (i)

Describe pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) and explain when and why it is used.

PGD is used during IVF:

  1. Eggs are fertilised in vitro and embryos grown to the 8-cell stage
  2. One or two cells are removed from each embryo (biopsy)
  3. DNA from these cells is tested for the allele associated with the genetic disorder
  4. Only embryos free of the allele are selected for implantation into the uterus

Used by couples who are known carriers of serious genetic conditions (e.g. cystic fibrosis, Huntington's disease) to avoid passing the condition to their children.

2.17 (i)

Compare amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling (CVS) as methods of prenatal genetic testing.

Feature Amniocentesis CVS
Timing~14–16 weeks~8–12 weeks
SampleAmniotic fluid (fetal cells)Chorionic villi (placental tissue)
ProcedureNeedle through abdomen into amniotic sacNeedle/tube through cervix or abdomen
Risk~0.5–1% miscarriage risk~1–2% miscarriage risk

Both allow fetal DNA to be tested for genetic conditions. CVS gives results earlier.

2.17 (ii)

What are the implications of prenatal genetic screening for individuals and society?

  • Enables parents to make informed decisions about continuing or terminating a pregnancy
  • Results can cause significant psychological distress — anxiety, difficult decisions
  • A positive result does not always mean the condition will be severe (variable expressivity)
  • Risk of miscarriage from the procedure itself must be weighed against the information gained
  • Raises questions about the value of life with a disability and potential discrimination
  • Information may affect family planning decisions for other relatives (carriers)
2.18

Discuss the ethical and social issues relating to genetic screening from at least two different ethical viewpoints.

Religious/moral viewpoint:

  • Some religions oppose termination of pregnancy under any circumstances → conflict with prenatal testing that might lead to termination
  • Concerns about 'playing God' — selecting embryos in PGD
  • All life is considered sacred regardless of health status

Social/utilitarian viewpoint:

  • Screening can prevent suffering for both child and family
  • Risk of genetic information being used by employers or insurers to discriminate
  • Psychological impact of learning carrier status (even if unaffected)
  • Concerns about eugenics — societal pressure to screen and select
  • Consent issues — who should be screened and when?
Exam tipAlways present more than one viewpoint. Avoid stating just one side — evaluative answers must weigh different perspectives.
